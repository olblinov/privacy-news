# Overview of the Proposal

## Context and Aim

The European Commission has published a proposal for a new regulation that aims to **simplify the EU General Data Protection Regulation (“GDPR”) requirements for small mid-cap enterprises (“SMCs”)**. This Proposal is part of the European Commission’s Omnibus IV Simplification Package. The primary goal of the Proposal is to **“reduce administrative burden”** on SMCs. Both the European Data Protection Board (“EDPB”) and the European Data Protection Supervisor (“EDPS”) have expressed preliminary support for the proposal to simplify GDPR record-keeping obligations.

## Key Changes Proposed

The Proposal introduces targeted changes that extend existing SME derogations to SMCs.

### Definition of Small Mid-Cap Enterprises (SMCs)

The Proposal amends the GDPR by extending the derogation on the obligation to maintain records of processing to ‘SMCs’. The **DLA Piper source defines SMCs** as organizations with **fewer than 750 employees**, a total balance sheet not exceeding EUR129 million, and an annual net turnover not exceeding EUR150 million. The European Commission intends this new definition to include organizations that have outgrown the traditional SME definition.

However, the **EDPB and EDPS**, in their letter regarding the draft proposal, state their understanding that the Commission is considering extending the derogation to SMCs (including non-profits) with **fewer than 500 employees** and a certain annual turnover. This new definition could introduce further complexity, as other EU legislations, such as NIS2, have different classifications for enterprises (e.g., "medium-sized enterprises" under NIS2 are defined as those employing fewer than 250 persons with an annual turnover not exceeding EUR 50 million, and/or an annual balance sheet total not exceeding EUR 43 million).

### Conditions for Derogation

Currently, Article 30 GDPR exempts organizations with fewer than 250 employees from maintaining records of processing, unless the data processing is likely to result in “a risk” to data subjects' rights and freedoms, is not occasional, or includes special categories of data or criminal conviction/offence data.

The Proposal modifies these conditions:

- The derogation will apply unless an organization carries out processing activities that are **likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals** or where special category data is processed. The EDPB and EDPS welcomed that the obligation would still be required for "likely high risk" processing.
- The draft proposal considered **removing the exception related to "occasional processing"**.
- There was an understanding that the reference to processing **special categories of data or criminal convictions might be deleted** from the exceptions, although recitals might clarify that processing special categories of data for compliance with legal obligations in employment, social security, or social protection law (under Article 9(2)(b) GDPR) would not be subject to the record-keeping obligation.

### Consideration of SMC Needs

The Proposal includes a requirement that the **specific needs of SMCs must be taken into account** when Member States, supervisory authorities, the Board, and the Commission draft codes of conduct. This also applies when certification bodies or competent supervisory authorities establish data protection certification mechanisms and data protection seals and marks.

# Perspectives and Limitations

## Scope and Realities of Administrative Burden

Despite initial anticipation for broad GDPR simplification, the amendments in the current Proposal are **more limited and targeted** than first expected. In practice, many companies that fall within the proposed size definition of ‘SMCs’ may still be required to adhere to record-keeping obligations due to the **nature of their data processing activities**.

Furthermore, the Proposal does not alleviate the **"real administrative burden"** of mapping processing activities and carrying out Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs). These tasks will still be necessary to assess whether processing activities “are likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals” and to comply with other GDPR obligations. The EDPB and EDPS emphasize that even very small companies can engage in high-risk processing, and therefore, a **risk-based approach remains important**. They also note that non-occasional processing and processing of special categories of personal data may still result in a likely high risk, depending on the assessment of all relevant criteria.

## Comparison with Past Expectations and UK Initiatives

There was an expectation that simplification would include **standard records of processing for the most common processing activities**, which could then relieve compliant companies of other obligations, such as legitimate interest assessments.

In the UK, the previous Data Protection and Digital Information (No.2) Bill (“DPDI Bill”) moved towards this concept by narrowing the requirement to maintain records of processing and introducing **‘recognised legitimate interests’**. Although the specific amendments related to record-keeping were not carried over to the new UK Data (Use and Access) Bill (“DUAB”), the DUAB has maintained the concept of ‘recognised legitimate interests’ to provide a presumption of legitimacy for certain processing activities under Article 6(1)(f) (legitimate interests).

## Preliminary Support and Concerns from EDPB/EDPS

The EDPB and EDPS expressed **preliminary support** for this targeted simplification initiative, but they stressed that it **would not affect the obligation of controllers and processors to comply with other GDPR obligations**.

To better evaluate the impact of the proposed modification, the EDPB and EDPS consider it useful to understand the **number of companies and organizations that would benefit** from the simplification, and its **impact on the protection of personal data** through an analysis carried out by the Commission. This analysis is crucial for assessing whether the draft Proposal ensures a proportionate and fair balance between data protection and the interests of organizations.

# Next Steps in the Legislative Process

The Proposal will now proceed through the EU’s legislative procedure. It may be **further amended by the European Parliament or the Council**. The EDPB and EDPS anticipate a **formal consultation** after the publication of the draft legislative change, in line with Article 42(2) of Regulation 2018/1725, which will provide them with an opportunity to comment in more detail.
