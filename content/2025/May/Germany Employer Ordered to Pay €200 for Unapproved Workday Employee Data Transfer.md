# Summary

The German Federal Labour Court ruled on May 8, 2025, that an employer must pay €200 in damages to an employee for violating the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The case involved an employer who transferred more personal employee data, including salary information and private addresses, than permitted by a works agreement, to a parent company for testing the cloud-based HR software "Workday". This transfer was deemed unnecessary under Article 6(1)(1)(f) GDPR and resulted in non-material damages to the employee due to a loss of control over their data. The initial lower court decisions had dismissed the employee's claim, but the Federal Labour Court, after consulting the European Court of Justice (EuGH), partially upheld the employee's appeal.

# Details

## Background

In 2017, an employer (the defendant) planned to implement "Workday" as a uniform personnel information management system across its group. The employer processed its employees' personal data, including for payroll, using existing personnel management software. To test Workday, the employer transferred personal data of a plaintiff employee from the previous software to the group's parent company. A works agreement stipulated that the employer was permitted to transmit specific data, such as name, start date, work location, company name, and business phone number and email address. However, the employer transferred additional data belonging to the plaintiff, including **salary information, private home address, date of birth, marital status, social security number, and tax ID**.

## Legal Analysis

The plaintiff argued that they were entitled to €3,000 in non-material damages under **Article 82(1) GDPR** due to a violation of the GDPR, which became effective on May 25, 2018. The plaintiff asserted that the defendant had exceeded the boundaries set by the works agreement. Article 82(1) GDPR states that "Any person who has suffered material or non-material damage as a result of an infringement of this Regulation shall have the right to receive compensation from the controller or processor for the damage suffered". The lower courts had dismissed the plaintiff's claim. The Eighth Senate of the Federal Labour Court initially suspended the revision proceedings and sought clarification on questions regarding the interpretation of Union law from the Court of Justice of the European Union (EuGH). The EuGH provided its answers in a judgment on December 19, 2024. The Federal Labour Court ultimately determined that the employer's transfer of personal data beyond what was permitted by the works agreement was **not necessary** as per Article 6(1)(1)(f) GDPR. This specific article states that processing is lawful only if it is "necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller or by a third party, except where such interests are overridden by the interests or fundamental rights and freedoms of the data subject which require protection of personal data". The court identified the non-material damage to the plaintiff as the **loss of control** resulting from the transfer of their personal data to the parent company. #damages  The plaintiff clarified during the oral hearing that they were no longer arguing that the transfer of data covered by the works agreement was unnecessary, which meant the Senate did not have to assess whether the works agreement itself met GDPR requirements.

## Decision

The plaintiff's appeal before the Eighth Senate of the Federal Labour Court was **partially successful**. The court ruled that the plaintiff has a claim for damages against the defendant in the amount of **€200.00** under Article 82(1) GDPR. This decision, Urteil vom 8. Mai 2025 – 8 AZR 209/21 –, overturned the previous judgment by the Landesarbeitsgericht Baden-Württemberg from February 25, 2021.
