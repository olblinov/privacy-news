The Autoridade Nacional de Proteção de Dados (ANPD) presented the **results of its Public Consultation (Tomada de Subsídios) on Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Automated Decision Review** during a webinar held by its Coordenação-Geral de Normatização (CGN). This initiative aimed to foster **transparency**, encourage **social participation**, and share **relevant information** with society. The insights gathered will assist the ANPD in elaborating **norms to guide the process of reviewing automated decisions**.

# Introduction and Overview of the Public Consultation

## Purpose and Scope

The public consultation represents society's perspective on the **intersection of Artificial Intelligence and personal data processing**, as well as automated data processing. Its purpose was to collect input for the **regulation of Item 7 of ANPD's Regulatory Agenda for the 2025-2026 biennium**, specifically concerning Artificial Intelligence. Beyond the legal mandate to regulate Article 20 of the Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais (LGPD), which addresses the data subject's right to request review of automated decisions, the ANPD seeks to provide further guidance through documents such as guides and technical studies. This is crucial given the widespread use of AI by data processing agents and the **vulnerability of data subjects** who may lack advanced knowledge on the subject. Automated data processing is defined as any system, program, or technology, including algorithmic or AI systems, that collects, stores, or makes decisions based on personal data without direct human intervention.

## Timeline and Participants

The consultation period ran from **November 6, 2024, to January 24, 2025**, an extension from the initial December 5, 2024, deadline. A total of **124 contributions** were collected. These contributions were voluminous, summing up to **more than two thousand pages**, originating from diverse sectors and nearly all regions of Brazil. Social participation is a core premise of ANPD's operations and a fundamental element of good regulatory practices.

### Participation Demographics

Out of the 124 contributions:

- **99 were received via the "Participa Mais Brasil" platform**, and **25 were submitted in .pdf format via institutional email**, often due to space limitations on the platform.
- **61% originated from the Southeast Region** of Brazil.
- National participants represented all five regions, with the Southeast being most expressive (61%), followed by the Central-West (16%), South (4%), and Northeast (4%); 15% did not identify their region of origin.
- Approximately **56% (70 participants) declared responding on behalf of a data processing agent**.
- **Half of the participants belonged to the Third Sector**.
- **7% of the suggestions came from foreign entities**.
- Participant types included **individuals (50%)**, private companies (25%), third-sector organizations (22%), and public institutions (3%). Among individuals, lawyers (33%), professors (6%), designers (6%), and systems analysts/developers (4%) were represented, while 42% did not inform their profession.

## Consultation Structure

The consultation was structured around **15 questions grouped into four thematic blocks**:

1. **Principles of the LGPD** [4, 10a]
2. **Legal Hypotheses** [4, 10b]
3. **Rights of Data Subjects** [4, 10c]
4. **Good Practices and Governance** [4, 10d]

The responses to each question were consolidated and analyzed based on their convergences and divergences. All contributions were duly analyzed and compiled into a Technical Note. Some contributions submitted via email were not made public due to requests for protection of commercial or industrial secrets; access to these will be analyzed individually.

## Significance of the Initiative

The importance of this public consultation stems from the fact that **AI technologies frequently involve intensive processing of personal data for model training**. Furthermore, the LGPD sets specific rules for the review of decisions made solely based on automated personal data processing that affect the interests of data subjects. The results provide essential subsidies to the ANPD for the **development of future regulations and guidelines**.

# Key Findings: Convergences and Divergences in Response to Questions

## Block I: Principles of the LGPD

### Question 1: AI Training and the Principle of Necessity

This question explored how to reconcile AI system training, which often demands massive amounts of personal data, with the LGPD's **principle of necessity**, and what safeguards could ensure compliance while enabling AI development.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - There is a **consensus on the need to balance the extensive use of data for effective AI training with the protection of privacy and personal data**. This involves applying concepts of data minimization and proportionality, ensuring sufficient data for high-quality AI without using more than necessary.
    - The **quality and diversity of data** are deemed crucial for AI effectiveness, helping to mitigate biases.
    - Various **safeguards** were suggested to ensure compliance and prevent excessive data use. These include:
        - **Anonymization, pseudonymization, and synthetic data** to reduce reliance on personal data, especially during development and training [21a].
        - **Privacy by Design**, incorporating data protection measures from the system's conception [21b].
        - **Impact assessments**, including specific algorithmic impact assessments, to identify and mitigate privacy risks [22c]. Special mention was made for assessments involving data of children and adolescents [22d].
        - **Data governance**, with clear policies for collection, use, retention, and disposal [22e]. Some contributions suggested an AI Governance Committee for high-risk models [22e].
        - **Transparency**, informing data subjects about data origin, collection, purpose, and processing procedures, using accessible communication [23f, 23h]. Model documentation is also important [23g].
        - **Access control** to training data [24i].
        - A clear and **justified purpose** for data collection and retention, with mere future use not sufficing.
        - **Periodic reviews and monitoring** of data processing and access.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Interpretation of the necessity principle**: Some advocate for a **strict interpretation**, emphasizing minimization and limiting collection to the absolute minimum, arguing against large-scale processing for AI training. Others support a **more flexible, pragmatic view**, acknowledging that AI training often requires large volumes for quality, provided adequate safeguards and a legitimate purpose are in place. Some even suggest reinterpreting the principle to allow more data in certain circumstances.
    - **Obligation of certain safeguards**: There is disagreement on whether specific safeguards should be mandatory or left to the **discretion of each controller**, depending on their activity and risks.
    - **Focus of safeguards**: Some argue safeguards should primarily concentrate on the data **collection and system application phases**, not development, citing implementation challenges for startups and small businesses.
    - **Scope of the LGPD**: Some argue that LGPD guidelines should apply to AI systems **only when personal data is involved**, stating that accommodating large-scale AI training within the current LGPD framework might be impractical. Others assert that an impact assessment is necessary prior to processing large quantities of personal data.
- **Summary for Question 1:** Contributions reveal the complexity of reconciling AI development with personal data protection. While there's consensus on balancing data use and adopting safeguards, interpretations of the necessity principle, the mandatory nature of safeguards, their application focus, and the LGPD's scope in AI contexts remain divergent.
    

### Question 2: Good Practices and Safeguards for Clear Information Disclosure

This question addressed good practices and safeguards for defining specific purposes and providing clear, accessible information about personal data processing in AI systems.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Transparency and clear information**: A strong consensus emerged on providing data subjects with **clear, accessible, and comprehensible information** on how their data is collected, used, and processed by AI. This includes simple language, multimodal formats (e.g., infographics), and contextualization.
    - **Clear definition of purposes**: It is considered essential to **define the purposes of data processing clearly and specifically** from the outset of AI system development, ensuring they are legitimate, specific, transparent, and compliant with LGPD.
    - **Risk assessment**: Implementing a comprehensive risk assessment system, often mentioning the **Data Protection Impact Report (RIPD)**, is crucial for identifying potential risks.
    - **Governance, monitoring, and audits**: The importance of governance programs, **continuous monitoring, and regular audits** to ensure compliance and effectiveness of protection measures is highlighted.
    - **Accessible service channels**: Establishing accessible and efficient channels is fundamental for data subjects to **exercise their rights** (e.g., access, correction, deletion).
    - **Privacy policies**: These are considered important means to communicate data processing information in AI systems.
    - **Privacy by Design and Default**: The application of these principles from the conception of systems is broadly recommended.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Level of specificity in information**: Disagreement exists on how detailed the information provided to data subjects should be. Some favor "layered" information adapted to user interest, while others warn that **excessive technical detail could compromise intellectual property protection**. #transparency 
    - **Obligation to inform about AI use**: There's a divergence on whether the LGPD explicitly requires informing data subjects about the use of AI systems, with some arguing the focus should be on the purposes and effects of data processing, rather than the technology itself.
    - **Specific documentation**: Opinions vary on the need for specific documentation for AI data processing, with some finding existing privacy policies sufficient (perhaps with links to additional information), and others advocating for a dedicated "Artificial Intelligence Policy".
    - **Transparency in research projects**: While a progressive approach to transparency is generally accepted for research, views differ on how to balance transparency with the exploratory nature of research.
    - **Standards and norms**: Different perspectives exist on which standards should be followed, with some suggesting frameworks like NIST or ISO for AI governance, and others asserting that existing good practices and safeguards are sufficient.
- **Summary for Question 2:** There is consensus on the need for transparency, clear purpose definition, risk assessment, robust governance, accessible service channels, and privacy by design in AI data processing. However, divergences persist regarding the appropriate level of information specificity, the explicit obligation to disclose AI use, the necessity of specific AI documentation, transparency in research, and the adoption of specific standards.
    

### Question 3: Purpose and Transparency with General-Purpose AI (GPAI)

This question addressed how to reconcile the principles of purpose and transparency with the use of General-Purpose AI (GPAI) systems, which can perform a wide variety of tasks for different purposes.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Risk assessment**: Emphasized for mitigating risks and protecting rights, without stifling innovation. Ethical and algorithmic impact assessments were cited.
    - **Periodic review**: The need for continuous periodic review and adaptive governance, including processes for reviewing purposes and updating data subject information, was highlighted.
    - **Flexibility**: Some contributions cited international documents advocating for a flexible, context-oriented approach, balancing technical flexibility with legal compliance.
    - **Safeguards**: Consensus on adopting safeguards for personal data processing in GPAI training, noting that **new personal data created by AI is subject to the same LGPD safeguards** as input data. Accountability measures and risk level assessments were also mentioned.
    - **Explainability**: Should be promoted through accessible communication for data subjects. Mechanisms for explainability and interpretability are important for users and regulators to understand decision criteria.
    - **Transparency**: Clear information disclosure and privacy policies are crucial, implemented at development, integration, and final use levels, despite operational challenges.
    - **Collaborative governance**: Collaboration among governments, industry, and academia is considered essential for formulating guidelines.
    - **Alignment with existing principles**: GPAI systems should adhere to the same principles of purpose and transparency as other systems.
    - **Layered privacy notices**: Recommended for GPAI to ensure transparency in complex systems, using varied communication means.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Legal basis for GPAI**: While many contributions suggest **legitimate interest is an adequate legal basis for GPAI data processing**, others, while not dismissing it, highlight the possibility of using **consent**, including in a progressive and contextual manner.
    - **Level of purpose specificity**: Disagreement on how specific purposes should be: some argue for sufficient specificity related to system type and viable functionalities, while others allow for broader, more encompassing purposes. Some advocate for individualized communication, others for a layered structure (macro and sub-purposes).
    - **Transparency application**: Some suggest applying transparency differently depending on the processing phase in AI systems, while others argue AI systems should follow the same transparency principles as all other systems.
    - **Responsibility for purpose definition**: Divergence on whether responsibility should be shared or primarily rest with the AI applicator or developer. There's also disagreement on whether developers are responsible for all applications of their AI systems, or if they primarily have an obligation to guide proper use.
    - **Timing of purpose limitation assessment**: Some argue this assessment should only be mandatory at the AI system's application phase, after testing, while others emphasize that personal data processing remains purpose-specific and context-dependent throughout.
- **Summary for Question 3:** Contributions largely agree on the importance of risk assessment, periodic review, safeguards, explainability, transparency, collaborative governance, and applying existing principles to GPAI. However, key divergences emerged regarding the most appropriate legal basis for GPAI, the required specificity of purpose, the timing of purpose limitation assessments, and the allocation of responsibility for purpose definition.
    

### Question 4: Preventing Unlawful/Abusive Discrimination in AI

This question sought good practices, safeguards, parameters, or criteria to prevent unlawful or abusive discrimination throughout the AI system lifecycle.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Continuous monitoring and audits**: Consensus on the importance of regular audits to ensure fair system operation and compliance, including retrospective audits after contractual relations end, given potential delayed impacts on human rights.
    - **Data quality and diversity**: Highlighted as essential for bias mitigation.
    - **Impact assessment**: Various contributions point to the need for impact assessments, especially for high-risk scenarios, to identify and mitigate privacy risks, including Human Rights Impact Assessments (HRIA).
    - **Human supervision**: Agreement on implementing human oversight to mitigate risks of unlawful or abusive discrimination.
    - **Multidisciplinary and diverse teams**: Emphasized for structuring AI systems to identify and mitigate unconscious biases.
    - **Feedback channels and correction mechanisms**: Recommended to enable data subjects to exercise their rights within technical limitations.
    - **Sensitive data**: Convergent views on the possibility of treating sensitive personal data during testing and validation phases, often under the legal basis of compliance with a legal or regulatory obligation.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Use of sensitive data**: Some argue sensitive data should be used only when strictly necessary, while others advocate for greater flexibility to facilitate bias reduction in AI training.
    - **LGPD flexibility**: Disagreement on whether LGPD principles need to be relaxed to enable the combat against discriminatory biases.
    - **Necessity of additional requirements**: Divergence on whether AI systems require additional, specific anti-discrimination requirements, or if existing regulations are sufficient.
    - **Adoption of good practices and safeguards**: Debate on whether practices should consider the operational viability for models (to avoid hindering new businesses and competition) or if a robust structure of safeguards is universally needed throughout the AI lifecycle.
- **Summary for Question 4:** There is a general consensus on the necessity of continuous monitoring, audits, impact assessments, human supervision, diverse teams, and feedback mechanisms to prevent discrimination in AI. However, there are differing views on the use of sensitive data, the need for LGPD flexibility, additional anti-discrimination requirements for AI, and the proportionality of implementing safeguards.
    

## Block II: Legal Hypotheses

### Question 5: Consent as a Legal Basis for AI

This question explored the viability, circumstances, limitations, and necessary safeguards for using consent as a legal basis for personal data processing in AI systems.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Limitations and challenges**: The **majority agree that using consent as the sole legal basis for AI data processing faces significant challenges**, including logistical inviability, high costs for managing individual consents and revocations, and difficulty in clearly explaining complex AI purposes to data subjects.
    - **Difficulty of revocation**: Revoking consent is seen as particularly problematic for AI, as "**untraining**" a model can be technically infeasible.
    - **Need for transparency and information**: When consent is considered, transparency and detailed information are crucial, ensuring data subjects are clearly informed about data use, AI system functioning, and potential impacts.
    - **Specific and informed consent**: Consent, when applicable, must be **free, informed, and specific**, with generic authorizations considered void.
    - **Alternatives to consent**: Many contributions suggest that other legal bases, such as **legitimate interest, contract execution, or legal obligation**, might be more suitable in certain AI contexts.
    - **Careful assessment**: The use of consent should be critically evaluated due to the difficulty of obtaining prior and specific consent.
    - **Safeguards**: If consent is used, specific safeguards are necessary, including transparency, accountability, documentation, audits, revocation mechanisms, reuse limits, robust security measures, granular consent, accessible language (LGPD Art. 9), minimization, and anonymization. Additional safeguards for vulnerable groups or personalized services are also noted.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Viability of consent**: While most contributions highlight the difficulties, some argue consent is viable for specific, clearly defined purposes where there's a direct relationship with data subjects and consent can be freely and unambiguously obtained. Others contend that **consent can impede AI development, especially for large datasets or socially beneficial systems** (e.g., fraud detection).
    - **Specific vs. secondary purposes**: Discussion exists on whether specific purposes are always needed for AI training, or if training is implicitly part of data processing. Some advocate for secondary purposes if linked to the original purpose, expected by data subjects, and with adequate security.
    - **Scalability of consent**: Some contributions explicitly state that consent should not be scalable.
    - **Renewal of consent**: Divergence on whether consent needs to be renewed with each significant system update.
    - **Public data**: Disagreement on the possibility of using consent for processing public data, such as data scraping.
- **Summary for Question 5:** There is broad agreement on the significant limitations and operational challenges of using consent as the primary legal basis for AI, particularly regarding revocation and the complexity of explaining AI purposes. Consensus also exists on the need for transparency and specific safeguards if consent is relied upon, and the suitability of alternative legal bases. However, opinions diverge on the overall viability and scalability of consent for AI, the necessity of its renewal, and its application to public data.
    

### Question 6: Legitimate Interest as a Legal Basis for AI

This question explored the applicability, circumstances, and necessary safeguards for using legitimate interest as a legal basis for personal data processing in AI systems, especially considering the prohibition of using legitimate interest for sensitive personal data.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Applicability of legitimate interest**: Most contributions agree that legitimate interest can be a **valid legal basis for processing personal data in AI systems**, including model training, especially for large data volumes and process validation. It's also considered appropriate for data made manifestly public by the data subject.
    - **Need for balancing test**: It is widely accepted that a **balancing test is crucial** when using legitimate interest, weighing the controller's legitimate interests against the data subject's fundamental rights and freedoms.
    - **Transparenc y**: Transparency is consistently highlighted as a crucial element, requiring clear and accessible information to data subjects about data processing.
    - **Safeguards and mitigation measures**: Consensus on the need for safeguards to protect data subjects' rights, including **anonymization, pseudonymization, data minimization, security measures, the right to object, and Data Protection Impact Reports (RIPD)**.
    - **Data scraping**: Legitimate interest can be used for data scraping (collecting publicly accessible data), provided there is no expectation of privacy by the data subject. However, it generally should not involve sensitive personal data or children's data, and safeguards should be in place from the outset to limit collection of sensitive data.
    - **Sensitive data**: There is a **strong convergence that legitimate interest should NOT be used for processing sensitive personal data**, as explicitly prohibited by the LGPD. However, some contributions suggest that if accidental treatment of sensitive data occurs, and appropriate mitigation measures are adopted (e.g., filtering, annotation), its use might be considered.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Sensitive data**: Even with mitigation, some argue that non-intentional sensitive data treatment still prevents the use of legitimate interest, advocating for exclusion of sensitive data. Others contend that if the purpose is not to classify individuals based on protected attributes, legitimate interest could apply, as **prohibiting sensitive data might hinder bias mitigation**. EDPB guidelines allowing unintentional collection with proper mitigation are cited.
    - **Data scraping**: Some express caution regarding legitimate interest for scraping, especially when the purpose differs from the original or there's a privacy expectation. Others affirm its legitimacy if data is publicly accessible, no privacy expectation, and minimal impact on data subjects. Arguments against scraping as anti-ethical due to lack of consent/control are also presented.
    - **Scope of legitimate interest**: Interpretations vary, from limited to broader applicability, including an organization's commercial interest if lawful and passing the balancing test. It may also apply to system improvement or protection of controller/operator rights (e.g., security systems).
    - **Hierarchy among legal bases**: While LGPD doesn't establish a hierarchy, contributions debate the flexibility controllers should have. Some argue legitimate interest is flexible for new data uses but requires high accountability.
    - **Societal interest vs. private ends**: Some argue legitimate interest should only apply when aligned with societal interest, cautioning against its use for purely private ends due to privacy risks and potential abuses. It is suggested that if AI is incompatible with existing data protection laws, the problem might lie with the technology itself, not the legislation.
    - **Necessity of consent**: Legitimate interest is seen as appropriate when consent is difficult or impossible to obtain. However, others suggest that AI agents could use gradual consent for specific purposes, or that consent should be determined case-by-case, especially for sensitive data.
- **Summary for Question 6:** Contributions generally agree on the applicability of legitimate interest for AI data processing, emphasizing the need for a balancing test, transparency, and specific safeguards. While scraping can be justified under legitimate interest with caveats, there's broad consensus against its use for sensitive data. However, opinions diverge significantly on the nuanced treatment of sensitive data, the extent of legitimate interest's scope, the hierarchy of legal bases, the balance between societal and private interests, and the situations requiring consent versus legitimate interest.
    

## Block III: Rights of Data Subjects

### Question 7: Application of LGPD Data Subject Rights to AI Systems

This question investigated how data subject rights, as stipulated in the LGPD, apply to AI systems.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Applicability of LGPD**: The majority agrees that the **LGPD applies to the processing of personal data by AI systems**, encompassing the training, development, and application phases, rather than the AI systems themselves. The application should be proportional.
    - **Necessity of transparency**: There's consensus on the importance of **transparency in AI use**, ensuring data subjects are informed about data collection, use, and processing, while balancing this with the protection of trade secrets. Algorithmic transparency is also mentioned.
    - **Attention to context**: It is understood that the exercise of data subjects' rights must consider the **context of AI systems**, including technical limitations, algorithmic complexity, and the need to balance privacy protection with other legitimate interests.
    - **Governance and accountability**: The importance of implementing **AI governance programs** that include data processing documentation, access/use monitoring, and mechanisms for addressing data subjects' requests is highlighted.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Data exclusion**: Divergence exists on the technical feasibility and economic viability of **deleting personal data used in AI training**. Some argue it's often impossible or economically prohibitive, while others advocate for mechanisms allowing data adjustments without compromising system integrity ("machine unlearning" is mentioned as costly). Legal retention requirements can also restrict deletion.
    - **Right of access**: This right should be translated into concrete terms. Some argue it should be **limited to comprehensible information**, not all internal system data, to protect trade secrets. Others note difficulty in linking specific information to an individual in unstructured datasets, potentially making full access impossible for data incidentally contained in pre-training phases.
    - **Right of opposition**: This right should be concrete. Some suggest it applies only to future system versions unless the organization demonstrates its interests continue to prevail. Others argue it should be guaranteed, noting that rights might be restricted due to a balancing exercise between privacy rights and public interest (e.g., financial risk management).
    - **General application of data subjects' rights**: While generally applicable, there's debate on the **proportionality and practicality of implementing these rights** due to technical inviability in many cases. Rights are applicable to data used in training, development, and application, as well as generated data and outputs where personal data is treated. The LGPD does not apply to AI systems themselves, but to the data processing they perform. Some argue that exercising rights should not force the AI system to "forget" information, which could make AI inviable. Also, the ANPD should avoid overly specific requirements for exercising rights.
    - **Scope of LGPD application**: Debate on whether the LGPD applies only to the training and output phases of AI models, or to the entire lifecycle of AI applications that process personal data.
    - **Right of rectification**: Divergence on its applicability to AI systems. Some argue it should apply only to verifiable facts, not probabilistic AI results, and only when reasonable prejudice occurs. Others suggest mechanisms for data adjustment without compromising system integrity. Some contributions state it's impossible for trained models as it would require full retraining.
    - **Excessive requirements**: Concerns about the ANPD creating overly specific requirements for data subjects to exercise their rights, which might not account for the variety of controllers and technologies.
    - **Need for specific legislation**: Some argue that existing legislation (like PL 2338/2023) already provides a comprehensive regulatory framework for AI, making additional obligations based on personal data processing unnecessary.
- **Summary for Question 7:** Contributions generally align on LGPD's applicability to AI data processing, the need for transparency, contextual exercise of rights, and robust governance. However, significant divergences exist regarding the practical implementation of rights such as data exclusion, access, opposition, and rectification in AI contexts. Debate also continues on the precise scope of LGPD application to AI and whether additional specific legislation or ANPD requirements are necessary.
    

### Question 8: Good Practices and Safeguards for Data Subject Service Channels

This question focused on good practices and safeguards for providing service channels that allow data subjects to exercise their rights, such as access, opposition, and review of automated decisions, in the context of AI.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Importance of service channels**: A consensus exists on the **necessity of channels for data subjects to exercise their rights**.
    - **Accessibility and ease of use**: Contributions converge on the importance of channels being **easily accessible with intuitive and simple interfaces**, providing clear information in plain language, free of technical jargon, and being consistently available. Accessibility for individuals with disabilities and varying levels of digital literacy is also highlighted.
    - **Transparency**: Transparency is deemed a fundamental principle, with channels offering **clear and detailed information** on how to exercise rights and providing policies that ensure transparency regarding AI models and technologies. They should also inform data subjects about AI use in their data processing and its impacts. Suggestions include mechanisms for detailed information on inferences, criteria, and models used.
    - **Safeguards**: Robust data protection in service channels is crucial, involving **secure technology, strong authentication, and identity verification methods**. Good practices include prior notification for opposition, easy and granular opposition, ensuring right of access to metadata, information on data sharing, and specific notification when interacting with AI. Post-training techniques like filtering and deduplication, and a holistic approach to privacy protection, are also mentioned.
    - **Record and traceability**: The need to **maintain records of interactions** to ensure traceability and accountability is emphasized.
    - **Deadlines**: Service deadlines should be clear and aligned with legal requirements.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Obligation of specific AI channels**: A key divergence is whether specific service channels for AI systems are mandatory. Some argue LGPD does not require them, while others recommend controllers assess the need for specialized mechanisms in **complex or high-impact cases** (e.g., credit analysis, fraud prevention) as a **good practice rather than a legal requirement**.
    - **Standardization of channels**: Disagreement exists on the standardization of service channels. Some believe the ANPD should only provide general guidelines, respecting controller autonomy, while others suggest the ANPD define more specific guidelines.
    - **Format of channels**: Opinions vary on which channel format should be prioritized: some advocate for controllers to define formats (forms, email) based on their possibilities and criteria, while others suggest **human interaction should be prioritized**.
    - **Necessity of human review**: Divergence on the necessity of human review for automated decisions, with some advocating for escalation to human review in complex or contested cases.
- **Summary for Question 8:** Contributions widely agree on the importance, accessibility, and transparency of service channels for data subjects, along with the necessity of safeguards, record-keeping, and clear deadlines. However, opinions diverge on the obligation to create AI-specific channels, the level of standardization, the preferred channel format, and the extent of human review requirement.
    

### Question 9: Safeguards for Sensitive Data and Vulnerable Groups (Children, Adolescents, Elderly)

This question focused on whether specific safeguards and limits are needed for processing sensitive personal data and data of children, adolescents, and the elderly throughout the AI system lifecycle.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Necessity of additional safeguards**: The majority agrees on the need for **additional safeguards when processing sensitive personal data and data of vulnerable groups** (children, adolescents, elderly). This includes techniques like PETs/PPTs (anonymization, synthetic data, differential privacy, encryption), privacy by design, data minimization, continuous monitoring, and obtaining consent with clear communication tailored to the data subject's understanding. Accessible AI interfaces and simple language are also suggested.
    - **Best Interest of the Child Principle**: There is a strong consensus that the **processing of data of children and adolescents must adhere to the "best interest of the minor" principle**. This principle should be considered prior to AI system development or implementation targeting these groups, with minimization of data collection and restrictions on use for child advertising.
    - **Transparency**: Transparency in data processing, especially for the elderly, is considered fundamental.
    - **Reinforced security measures**: Convergence on the need for **reinforced security measures** for sensitive data and vulnerable groups, including creating risk profiles, training models with balanced data to avoid biases, developing intuitive interfaces, and specialized service channels.
    - **Risk and Impact Assessment (RIPD)**: Widely recommended, particularly for cases involving sensitive data or vulnerable groups.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Distinct requirements for elderly**: Some argue that data processing for the elderly should not have distinct requirements from other adults to avoid discrimination, given their presumed cognitive capacity. Others contend that the elderly require special attention due to risks of digital exclusion and difficulty understanding AI impacts, advocating for clear language and consideration of curatorship or supported decision-making. Some even suggest restricting specific safeguards to those over 80 years old.
    - **Classification of elderly data as sensitive**: Disagreement exists on classifying elderly data as sensitive. Some argue this exceeds LGPD limits, while others suggest it can be sensitive in certain contexts, highlighting the risk of ageism.
    - **Amplitude of safeguards**: Divergence on whether additional limitations should be imposed beyond those already provided in legislation. Some argue against imposing AI-specific limitations not expressly in LGPD, while others advocate for broader limitations for sensitive and vulnerable data.
    - **CONANDA Resolution 245/2024**: Debate on its impact, specifically concerning the prohibition of profiling children and adolescents for commercial purposes, with some arguing it might exclude minors from beneficial products.
    - **Consent**: One contribution suggests reconsidering consent as a legal basis for AI due to its inherent opacity, which prevents data subjects from accurately assessing risks.
    - **Proportionality Test**: Some contributions mention the sufficiency of the proportionality test as a limitation when legitimate interest is used for vulnerable data.
    - **Sensitive data definition**: Some argue sensitive data treatment only occurs when the purpose is to identify specific aspects mentioned in LGPD Art. 5 II and associate them with an individual, noting that mere inference does not qualify data as sensitive. Others suggest operations with sensitive data should be guided by strict necessity.
    - **Legislative vs. ANPD role**: Some contributions assert that the LGPD already delineates limits for sensitive, child, adolescent, and elderly data (Arts. 11, 14, 55-J, XIX), and that imposing further restrictions is the legislator's task, not the ANPD's. They recommend waiting for the progress of PL 2338/2023 to avoid legal uncertainty.
    - **Restricted use of automated decisions**: Some suggest prohibiting solely automated decisions for vulnerable groups without clear human supervision and proven benefits, advocating for high-risk classification for such systems with stricter obligations (audits, documentation, transparency) and prohibiting manipulative AI for children.
- **Summary for Question 9:** There is a strong consensus on the need for additional safeguards for sensitive data and vulnerable groups, emphasizing the "best interest of the child" principle, transparency, reinforced security measures, and risk/impact assessments. However, significant divergences are observed regarding specific requirements for the elderly, classifying their data as sensitive, the overall scope of safeguards beyond existing legislation, the impact of CONANDA's resolution, the viability of consent for opaque AI, the sufficiency of the proportionality test, the definition of sensitive data treatment, and the roles of legislative bodies versus the ANPD in imposing restrictions.
    

### Question 10: Requirements for Automated Decision Review (Art. 20 LGPD)

This question sought to define the requirements for applying the right to review automated decisions (LGPD Art. 20), what constitutes a decision based solely on automated personal data processing, and which interests might be affected.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Right to Review**: There is a consensus that **LGPD Art. 20 grants data subjects the right to request a review of decisions made exclusively through automated data processing that affect their interests**. Examples include credit analyses, candidate selection, and personalized advertisements. Such decisions can impact liberty, privacy, and lead to discrimination or unequal opportunities.
    - **Requirements for Review**: Essential requirements for guaranteeing this right include:
        - The **possibility of human review by a qualified person** [115a].
        - **Transparency** in the decision-making process [115b].
        - **Explainability** of the algorithm [115c].
        - **Adequate communication channels** [115d].
        - **Documentation** of automated decision-making processes and criteria [115e].
    - **Affectation of Interests**: The right to review is conditional on affecting the data subject's interests, with a **threat or risk to rights being sufficient**, not necessarily a material violation.
    - **Uniquely Automated Decision**: Defined by the **absence of significant human participation** in the decision-making process. "Human in the loop" roles must be substantive, not just rubber-stamping algorithmic results.
    - **Affected Interests**: Automated decisions can impact various data subject interests, including fundamental rights (liberty, privacy), access to services/opportunities, economic/financial rights, labor rights, health, reputation, and autonomy.
    - **Transparency and Access to Information**: Data subjects must have clear access to the logic behind automated decisions, including the criteria used.
    - **Need for Safeguards**: Acknowledgment of the need for robust safeguards to protect users from harm and support responsible AI use.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **High-Risk Decisions**: Some argue that review should only apply to **high-risk automated decisions** that produce legal or significantly similar effects. Others contend that the right to review is not dependent on such specific legal effects.
    - **Scope of Interest Affectation**: Disagreement on the breadth of interests affected. Some suggest not broadening the right to review to less significant impacts (e.g., ad profiling). Others argue for inclusion of significant or negative impacts on personality, legal, economic, political, privacy, reputation, public rights, and algorithmic bias.
    - **Necessity of Human Review**: Debate on the **obligatory nature of human review**. Some argue automation brings objectivity and speed, and that the LGPD does not explicitly require human review, allowing for automated review. Others state human review is only needed if the treatment is exclusively automated or for legal/significant effects, highlighting substantial challenges (costs, complexity) in implementing human review for entirely automated processes. Human review is seen by some as essential for preventing discrimination, biases, or errors.
    - **Concept of Uniquely Automated Decision**: Varied interpretations of what constitutes a "uniquely automated decision," centering on the definition of "significant" human intervention. Criteria mentioned include material impact, exclusively automated process, and material effect on the data subject's life. AI models providing outputs reviewed by a human final decider may not be considered automated decisions. Debate also exists on whether the concept extends to **intermediate decisions** within a process that can influence final outcomes.
    - **Transparency vs. Trade Secrets**: Acknowledged challenge of balancing data subjects' right to transparency and review with the need to protect intellectual property and commercial interests. Transparency should consider regulatory context without compromising trade secrets, providing clear and relevant information without detailing specific formulas.
    - **Generative AI models**: Divergence on whether generative AI models make "decisions" that fall under LGPD Art. 20, with some arguing they merely predict next words/phrases, not constituting a decision in the legal sense.
- **Summary for Question 10:** Contributions largely agree on the existence of the right to review automated decisions, the general requirements for such review (including human oversight, transparency, and explainability), the need for safeguards, and the concept of affected interests. However, significant divergences arise concerning the scope of "high-risk" decisions, the precise definition of a "uniquely automated decision" and its extension to intermediate steps, the mandatory nature and practicalities of human review, the balance between transparency and trade secrets, and the applicability of Art. 20 to generative AI models.
    

### Question 11: Necessity of Human Review for Automated Decisions

This question focused on the hypotheses and conditions under which human review of automated decisions is necessary to adequately guarantee data subjects' rights.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Legal or Similar Effects**: There is agreement that human review is necessary when automated decisions produce **legal effects or significantly similar effects**, as well as adverse effects stemming from a lack of transparency, explainability, non-compliance, or discriminatory/abusive biases.
    - **Impact on Data Subject Rights**: Human review is considered necessary when an automated decision has a **significant impact on the data subject's rights**, including situations involving discrimination, biases, sensitive data processing, or unsatisfactory explanations. Some argue any impact, even minimal, necessitates review, while others suggest focusing on high-impact decisions (e.g., credit, loans, employment).
    - **Discrimination and Biases**: The need for human review is emphasized in cases where **discrimination or biases are suspected** in automated decisions.
    - **Transparency and Explainability**: Human review is directly linked to these principles; a **lack of transparency justifies human intervention** to help the data subject understand decision criteria and data used.
    - **High Risk**: The presence of **high risk** in personal data processing within AI systems is identified as a determining factor for human review.
    - **Reviewer Qualification**: Human review should be conducted by **qualified professionals** capable of identifying errors or biases in algorithms.
    - **Right to Contest and Information**: Data subjects must have the right to **efficiently contest automated decisions** and access clear, adequate information about the criteria and data used.
    - **Review as Good Practice**: Even if not legally obligatory, human review is widely recommended as a **good practice** to prevent large-scale harm and can be a mitigating circumstance in inspections. It's also seen as a good practice for quality control and bias prevention in generative AI outputs.
    - **Supervision Mechanisms**: Consensus on the importance of **supervision mechanisms integrated into AI systems**.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Obligatoriness of Human Review**: The primary divergence lies in whether human review is legally mandatory. The LGPD does not explicitly state that reviews must be human, allowing for automated review. Some argue human review is not a legal requirement, while others contend it is mandatory for decisions involving the Judiciary.
    - **Automated vs. Human Review**: Some argue that automated review can be sufficient if effective, and that prioritizing integrated supervision mechanisms is key.
    - **Interpretation of Art. 20 LGPD**: Different interpretations exist, with some highlighting that the legislator deliberately excluded the provision for human review.
    - **Costs and Viability**: Mandating human review could make businesses inviable and increase operational costs. It's also noted that human intervention itself can be influenced by unconscious biases.
    - **Amplitude of Impact**: Divergence on what level of impact justifies human review: proportional review only for significant impacts versus any impact on an individual's life.
    - **Specific Sectors**: Some contributions highlight sectors like health, justice, public safety, and finance where human review would be particularly important.
    - **Generative AI**: No consensus on whether generative AI models fall under the review criteria of LGPD Art. 20.
- **Summary for Question 11:** There is strong consensus on the necessity of human review for automated decisions that produce legal or similar effects, significantly impact data subjects' rights (especially due to discrimination or biases), and where transparency/explainability are lacking. Agreement also exists on the need for qualified reviewers and supervision mechanisms, and that human review is a good practice. However, opinions diverge on the mandatory nature of human review, the sufficiency of automated review, interpretations of LGPD Art. 20, the cost-viability trade-offs, the precise scope of impact warranting review, and the applicability to generative AI.
    

### Question 12: Providing Clear Information and Limits of Trade Secrets

This question addressed the parameters for providing clear and adequate information about the criteria and procedures used for automated decisions (LGPD Art. 20 §1), and the limits of commercial and industrial secrets that justify non-disclosure.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Transparency and Accessibility**: Consensus that information on automated decisions must be **accessible, comprehensible, and transparent**, allowing data subjects to understand how and why a decision was made. Language should be clear and avoid complex technical terms.
    - **Purpose of Explainability**: The main purpose of AI explainability is to **enable affected individuals to question decisions**.
    - **Equilibrium between Transparency and Trade Secret**: There's a recognition of the **need to balance transparency with the protection of commercial and industrial secrets**. Excessive disclosure could lead to manipulation and malicious use of information.
    - **Flexibility and Context**: Regulations should be flexible to accommodate different contexts, without demanding companies disclose trade secrets, intellectual property, or internal security mechanisms. Guidelines should be general, allowing agents flexibility.
    - **Access and Comprehension**: Data subjects should have access to and understand the **regulatory logic** behind automated decisions, not necessarily the technical logic.
    - **Two Approaches to Explanation**: Identified as either logic-based or data-based.
    - **Parameters for Information Provision**: Converging points include: **clarity and comprehension** (accessible language, avoiding jargon), **objectivity** (relevant criteria only), **proportionality** (relevant, non-confidential details), indicating **purpose and legal basis**, promoting **accessibility** via various communication means, and **documenting** explanations during system development.
    - **Limits Related to Trade Secret**: Trade and industrial secrets can justify non-disclosure of methodologies, confidential company data, and strategic information. A balance must be sought to provide useful information without exposing sensitive elements. Protection of trade secrets must not impede LGPD rights.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Level of Detailing for Information**: Divergence on the required technical detail. Some argue that revealing algorithms or complex formulas is unnecessary; transparency on collected data and automated processing purposes suffices. Others emphasize comprehensive and exhaustive information.
    - **Definition of Commercial and Industrial Secret**: Legal uncertainty exists regarding what constitutes "clear and adequate" information and how to define "commercial and industrial secrets." Some argue that inherently technical information like formulas should not be subject to transparency requirements.
    - **Scope of Trade Secret**: Different views on what constitutes a trade or industrial secret; some include methodologies, confidential company data, and strategic information, while others restrict disclosure to essential elements for transparency without compromising innovation or market position.
    - **Transparency vs. Security**: Some argue that excessive transparency could compromise system security, leading to manipulation and malicious use of information.
    - **Auditing**: In cases where secrecy is claimed, some suggest that audits could be conducted to verify the veracity of such claims.
- **Summary for Question 12:** The analysis highlights a consistent debate on providing clear and adequate information about automated decisions while balancing transparency with the protection of trade and industrial secrets. While there's a consensus on the need for accessible and comprehensible information, disagreements persist regarding the necessary level of technical detail, the precise definition and scope of what constitutes a trade secret, and the potential impact of transparency on system security. Implementing LGPD Art. 20 guidelines requires a contextual approach to balance data subjects' rights with innovation.
    

## Block IV: Good Practices and Governance

### Question 13: Privacy Governance Programs for AI Compliance

This question explored how privacy governance programs can promote compliance of AI systems with data protection regulations and what requirements should be observed.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Necessity of governance programs**: There is a **consensus that privacy governance programs are fundamental** for ensuring compliance with the LGPD in the development and use of AI systems.
    - **Privacy by Design**: The approach of "**Privacy by Design**" is widely advocated as an essential principle for building AI systems, ensuring privacy considerations are integrated from the initial development stages.
    - **Transparency and communication**: The importance of **transparency and effective communication** with data subjects is emphasized, ensuring individuals understand how their data is used and that their rights are respected.
    - **Data Protection Impact Report (RIPD)**: The RIPD is cited as an indispensable instrument for **identifying and mitigating risks** related to data processing in AI systems.
    - **Continuous monitoring**: The need for **continuous monitoring and audits** is highlighted to ensure ongoing compliance and effectiveness of data protection measures, including periodic reviews of algorithms for potential discrimination or privacy violations.
    - **Training and capacity building**: The importance of investing in **training and capacity building** for teams involved in AI system development and operation, covering LGPD principles, ethics, and risk prevention, is stressed.
    - **Adaptable privacy policies**: Encouragement for adopting privacy policies that can **adapt to evolving security and data protection** in AI systems.
    - **Accountability**: Recognized as an integral part of good governance.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Obligatoriness of some practices**: While some contributions advocate for applying data governance principles (e.g., quality, non-discrimination, general LGPD principles) to AI systems, others argue that **not all of them should be mandatory**. Some highlight that most AI risks do not involve personal data and thus require different risk analyses.
    - **Scope of regulation**: Debate on whether new requirements and obligations for AI systems should be established outside existing legislative limits. Some argue against creating new obligations beyond legal boundaries, viewing AI governance as a good practice but not a legal obligation. Others contend that the ANPD can issue guidelines for personal data governance in AI.
    - **Level of detail in ANPD regulation**: Discussion on whether the ANPD should set specific AI-related requirements. Some suggest the ANPD should avoid anticipating specific requirements to prevent legal uncertainty and await the progress of PL 2338/23. Others argue that the ANPD lacks competence for specific AI governance regulations, or conversely, that it should provide clear guidelines.
    - **RIPD for all systems**: Divergence on whether all organizations using high-risk AI systems must prepare an RIPD, or if low-risk applications are exempt. Some suggest algorithmic impact assessments.
    - **Adoption of international standards**: While some advocate for alignment with international best practices and standards (e.g., NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO 42001/23894), others do not explicitly require such adoption.
- **Summary for Question 13:** There is broad convergence on the necessity of privacy governance programs and Privacy by Design for AI systems, along with the importance of transparency, RIPD, continuous monitoring, and team training. However, significant divergences are observed regarding the mandatory nature of certain practices, the scope of regulation for AI, the level of detail the ANPD should impose, the universal requirement for RIPD, and the adoption of international standards.
    

### Question 14: Documentation and RIPD in the AI Lifecycle

This question addressed what information should be documented throughout an AI system's lifecycle for accountability, when a Data Protection Impact Report (RIPD) is recommended or required in AI contexts, and if specific RIPD requirements can be established.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Necessity of documentation**: Various contributions agree that it is **fundamental to document all stages of data processing in AI systems**, from conception and planning through development, implementation, continuous monitoring, and eventual deactivation.
    - **Risk as RIPD determinant**: There's a consensus that the **RIPD is most necessary in high-risk contexts**, with risk assessment considering the probability and severity of impacts on data subjects' rights and freedoms.
    - **Transparency and explainability**: The importance of **transparency in algorithms and automated decisions** is widely supported, with documentation including justifications for automated decisions and their criteria.
    - **Attention to data subjects' rights**: Contributions emphasize the importance of ensuring data subjects' rights (information, access, rectification, opposition, portability) and implementing mechanisms for security incidents and data breaches.
    - **Governance and responsibility**: The need to establish a **collective governance chain** between AI developers and applicators is recognized, with documentation demonstrating LGPD compliance and accountability.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Obligatoriness of RIPD**: Some contributions argue the RIPD is always required under LGPD, regardless of AI. Others suggest a **case-by-case assessment** considering AI risk and innovation. Some also contend that the ANPD should not set specific AI-related RIPD requirements, as LGPD provides sufficient legal framework.
    - **Specificity of ANPD requirements**: Divergence exists on whether the ANPD should establish specific requirements for RIPD elaboration in AI contexts. Some argue LGPD already provides necessary guidelines, while others believe the ANPD can detail requirements for compliance and data protection.
    - **Concept of "AI lifecycle"**: Some contributions emphasize documenting all stages of the AI lifecycle, while others assert that the term "lifecycle" does not apply to AI systems themselves in this context.
    - **Scope of RIPD**: Different views on what should be included in the RIPD, from essential elements (description of processing, necessity assessment, individual risks) to broader considerations like social/ethical impacts and security measures.
    - **Interpretation of "high risk"**: Specific criteria for identifying high-risk situations in AI are debated, comparing them to non-AI contexts. Some advocate for a prerequisite risk assessment, a public version of the RIPD, and participation of affected groups and experts. Documentation should cover the AI system's purpose, scope, methods, objectives, use cases, limitations, potential social/ethical impacts, data sources, legal basis, identified risks (e.g., algorithmic discrimination), and mitigation strategies. Full lifecycle documentation is considered indispensable for traceability and transparency. Some contributions state RIPD is always needed for sensitive data, vulnerable groups, high risk, or legitimate interest use.
- **Summary for Question 14:** There is convergence on the fundamental need for comprehensive documentation throughout the AI lifecycle, the role of risk as a determinant for RIPD, the importance of transparency, protection of data subject rights, and robust governance. However, divergences persist regarding the universal obligation for RIPD, the level of specificity for ANPD requirements, the applicability of the "AI lifecycle" concept, the detailed scope of RIPD content, and the precise interpretation of "high risk" in AI contexts.
    

### Question 15: Anonymization in the AI Lifecycle

This question investigated when and in what context anonymization is viable or necessary within an AI system's lifecycle, which techniques are used, and what other security measures could be employed for privacy protection.

- **Points of Convergence:**
    
    - **Anonymization as good practice**: Widely recognized as a beneficial practice.
    - **Anonymization as security measure**: Seen as an ancillary security measure linked to data protection.
    - **Anonymization in different phases**: It can be necessary and viable at various points in an AI system's lifecycle, particularly recommended in the **collection, storage, and sharing phases** of data.
    - **Anonymization techniques**: Various techniques are cited, including **cryptography, suppression, generalization, perturbation, pseudonymization, aggregation, and hashing**.
- **Points of Divergence:**
    
    - **Obligatoriness of anonymization**: Some contributions state it's a good practice but not an explicit legal obligation.
    - **Recommendation for some AI systems**: Anonymization is not recommended for all AI systems.
    - **Data treatment**: Opinions vary on whether anonymized data still constitutes "treatment" under LGPD. Some argue it does not.
    - **Consequences for data subjects**: Some believe anonymization has no consequences for data subjects.
    - **LGPD compliance for initially anonymized data**: AI systems posing risks to fundamental rights must still comply with LGPD obligations, even if they initially use anonymized data for training or operation. This is due to the "black box" nature of some AI systems, making it uncertain if re-identification could occur.
    - **Timing of anonymization**: Debate on whether anonymization should occur **before data enters AI systems** or if techniques can be applied later in the process (e.g., tokenization, generalization).
    - **"Silver bullet" status**: Anonymization is described as **not a "silver bullet"**; it does not reduce re-identification risk to zero and requires continuous monitoring and evaluation, which can be complex or impossible for some AI systems.
    - **Overall view**: Some view anonymization as an essential security measure, while others see it as one of several measures to be implemented.
- **Summary for Question 15:** Contributions generally agree that anonymization is a recommended practice and a valuable security measure, applicable at various stages of the AI lifecycle, with several techniques available. However, divergences exist regarding its mandatory nature, its suitability for all AI systems, whether anonymized data falls under "treatment," the ideal timing for its application, and its overall effectiveness as a complete solution for privacy protection in complex AI systems. It's noted that anonymization should be part of a broader set of security measures, including encryption, access control, and audits.
    

# Conclusion

The public consultation on AI and Automated Decision Review by the ANPD gathered extensive and diverse contributions that highlight the **complexity of reconciling AI development with personal data protection principles**. While there are significant areas of consensus regarding the need for transparency, clear purpose definition, robust governance, risk assessments, and safeguards throughout the AI lifecycle, fundamental divergences persist across various key aspects.

These divergences primarily concern the **interpretation and practical application of LGPD principles** (such as necessity, consent, legitimate interest, and data subject rights) within the unique context of AI. Specific points of contention include:

- The **viability and scope of using consent and legitimate interest** as legal bases for AI data processing, especially concerning sensitive data and data scraping.
- The **practical implementation of data subjects' rights** (e.g., exclusion, access, rectification, opposition) in AI systems, often facing challenges of technical feasibility and economic viability.
- The **necessity and scope of human review** for automated decisions, including its mandatory nature, costs, and applicability to generative AI.
- The balance between **transparency and the protection of trade and industrial secrets**, particularly regarding the level of technical detail to be disclosed.
- The **role and specific requirements for privacy governance programs and RIPDs** in AI contexts, including the ANPD's competence in setting detailed regulations and the interpretation of "high-risk" scenarios.
- The **effectiveness and mandatory nature of anonymization** as a safeguard throughout the AI lifecycle.

The insights from this consultation will serve as crucial input for the ANPD's regulatory efforts regarding Artificial Intelligence for the 2025-2026 biennium, aiming to inform the development of norms that guide the review process for automated decisions. The comprehensive nature of the contributions underscores the importance of a nuanced and adaptive regulatory approach that considers technological innovation alongside fundamental rights protection.
