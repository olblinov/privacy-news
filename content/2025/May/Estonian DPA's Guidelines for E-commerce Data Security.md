# Data Processing and Transparency in E-commerce

## Legal Basis and Data Protection Terms

The processing of personal data is governed by **seven core principles** for responsible and compliant data processors, as outlined in Article 5 of the GDPR:

- **Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency**.
- **Purpose limitation**.
- **Data minimization**, meaning collecting as little data as possible.
- **Accuracy**.
- **Storage limitation**.
- **Integrity and confidentiality** (security).
- **Accountability**.

**A legal basis is mandatory for processing personal data**. In e-shops, the most common legal basis for processing consumer data is the **fulfillment of a contract**. If personal data is processed for additional purposes beyond contract fulfillment, **consumer consent** may be a suitable legal basis, which must be obtained separately. For instance, sending newsletters or collecting cookie data for website information requires consent beyond the purchase itself.

Prior to using an e-shop, consumers must be able to access the e-shop's **data protection terms**. These terms, which might be referred to as privacy policies, privacy conditions, or privacy settings, must be clearly visible and accessible under an appropriate name. It is misleading to embed them within general terms and conditions or user terms. For example, a button simply stating "I agree to the privacy policy" is not sufficient.

The purpose of data protection terms is to inform data subjects about how their data is processed; it is an informational document and not part of the contract between the data controller and data subjects. The legal basis for processing personal data must be stipulated in these terms. Furthermore, the terms should explicitly state:

- The **contact details of the e-shop** (the data processor).
- **Which personal data is processed and for what purpose**.
- **To whom and in what circumstances data may be transferred**.
- **How long the e-shop retains consumer personal data**, specifying exact dates or triggering events (e.g., until the return period for goods expires), rather than vague statements like "as long as necessary".
- How consumers can **delete their e-shop user account** if they wish.

E-shop operators are also required to explain the consumer's rights regarding their personal data, including the right to access, rectify, erase, restrict processing, data portability, object to processing, and to receive protection against automated decisions.

## Data Minimization

One of the fundamental principles of personal data processing is **data minimization**, meaning that personal data should be relevant, appropriate, and limited to what is necessary for the purpose of processing. Consequently, it is unlawful to collect personal data from consumers that is not required to fulfill the stated purpose. For example, if a consumer chooses parcel machine delivery, the e-shop should not obligatorily request residential address details like city, street, or house number, as these are only necessary if the consumer uses a courier service.

## Direct Marketing

Direct marketing refers to offers sent to both natural and legal persons related to the sale of a product or provision of a service. A commercial communication is any form of information transmission directly or indirectly promoting the offering of goods or services by a service provider or enhancing their image. E-shop newsletters or promotional offers are examples of commercial communications, while service disruptions or changes to terms are not. It is important not to embed promotional information within essential notices.

**Using a natural person's electronic contact details for direct marketing requires their prior consent**. Consent is defined as a voluntary expression of will by the consumer (natural person) to allow their personal data to be processed. At the time of giving consent, the natural person must have a clear understanding of who they are giving consent to and for what purpose. This is typically achieved through a checkbox or another information technology solution that allows the consumer to clearly and unequivocally understand that they are giving consent, and which the e-commerce company must be able to prove later. Important points regarding consent:

- **Silence or inactivity is not considered consent**.
- **The personal data processor bears the burden of proof for consent**.
- **Pre-ticked consent boxes in e-shops are legally void**.

E-commerce companies wishing to send newsletters and other advertising must provide natural persons with the opportunity to give free and voluntary consent. For legal entities, obtaining prior consent for direct marketing is not required.

For both natural and legal persons, every instance where their electronic contact details are used for direct marketing must include a **clear and understandable option to easily and freely forbid such use**. This right must be exercisable via the electronic communication network, for example, by including an unsubscribe link at the end of an email or SMS. Opting out of messages must be as simple as giving consent, avoiding complex processes that require logging in or searching through multiple pages.

The use of electronic contact details for direct marketing is forbidden if:

- The person on whose behalf the information is transmitted cannot be identified.
- The person has forbidden the use of their electronic contact details.
- There is no instruction or information enabling the user to exercise their right to refuse.

## E-shop Account Creation

E-shop operators collect and store various personal data related to consumers during service provision. Personal data is any information about an identified or identifiable natural person (data subject), identifiable by identifiers such as name, personal identification code, location data, online identifier, or physical, physiological, genetic, mental, economic, cultural, or social characteristics. Any action performed on personal data (e.g., collection, use, storage, deletion) constitutes personal data processing.

Consumers can make purchases in an e-shop in two ways:

1. **As a guest:** No prior user registration is required; the e-shop asks for necessary data before the purchase is finalized. #minimization #change 
2. **As a registered user:** The consumer creates a personal user account by filling out a form with contact details such as email address, phone number, and postal address. Access to the user account is typically via a username and password.

It is increasingly common for third parties (e.g., Facebook, Google, or Microsoft) to be used for authentication during account registration. While users can log in with an existing profile (e.g., Google), the e-shop must ensure it does not request or collect excessive data from these third-party profiles, as this would violate the principle of data minimization.

From the perspective of security, data minimization, and purpose limitation principles, it is **not advisable to always require users to create an account for purchasing** from an e-shop. E-shop operators should analyze and assess whether and for what purpose this data is needed and if requiring account creation is strictly necessary. The principle is that **the less data collected, the fewer risks and the more secure it is for the consumer**. Additionally, not requiring a new account and password for every purchase makes shopping less time-consuming.

If creating a user account is a necessary solution for the e-shop operator, it must be remembered that consumer contact details, including username, password, email address, and postal address, are personal data. The online sales and purchasing process must adhere to the principle of trustworthiness and confidentiality, ensuring appropriate security of personal data, including protection against unauthorized or unlawful processing, and accidental loss, destruction, or damage, through appropriate technical or organizational measures.

## Two-Factor Authentication

If an e-shop allows user accounts, it is **recommended to implement multi-factor authentication (MFA)** #security , often referred to as two-factor authentication (2FA), instead of simply requiring a complex password. 2FA is a secure authentication method that requires the user to identify themselves in two independent ways upon login. Examples include:

- **ID-card authentication**, which involves a physical authentication tool combined with knowledge (a PIN code).
- **ID-card, Mobile-ID, or Smart-ID**: These solutions provide strong authentication, but e-shops should consider potential international clients who may not have access to these options.
- **Google, Microsoft, or Apple two-factor authentication**: This requires a verification code sent to the user's mobile device (via SMS, email, or a dedicated smartphone application) in addition to a password. Authentication applications are generally preferable to SMS, as SMS delivery can be complex, delayed, or blocked by mobile operators. In general, **two-factor authentication provides greater security** because it requires an additional factor (like a phone) beyond just a password (knowledge) to access an account.

## Password Usage

When consumers are allowed to choose their own passwords in an e-shop, the e-shop operator should **provide recommendations regarding password length and the types of symbols to use**. For example, it is advisable to allow long passwords, the use of all characters, and to restrict the use of weak passwords. These recommendations can be displayed in a separate pop-up info window next to the password input field. It is crucial for **each e-shop user to have a unique and secure password**.

Recommended password requirements include:

- A **minimum password length of at least 15 characters**. #security 
- Using a **passphrase** instead of a simple password.
- **Avoiding the reuse of the same passwords across different environments** and ensuring passwords are not easily derivable from previously used ones.
- **Not requiring or offering password hints** (e.g., mother's maiden name, pet's name).

If the e-shop operator sets a **default or initial temporary password** for the user, they must also adhere to the previously described recommendations. Consumers should be required to change the default password within a short, predetermined period, after which the default password can no longer be used. A more secure option is to prevent login altogether without creating a new password. This recommendation is important because most consumers tend to continue using default passwords out of convenience. It is also advisable to avoid sending passwords in plain text via email, instead using a unique link for secure delivery.

## External Service Providers

E-shop operators frequently rely on various **third-party service providers** to enhance their operations, such as payment processing services, analytics, and marketing platforms. While these providers offer essential services, they also introduce security and compliance risks. Under the GDPR, **e-shop operators remain ultimately responsible** for ensuring that all third parties processing customer data on their behalf comply with appropriate data protection standards. If the security of an external service provider is not ensured, it can lead to breaches that affect e-shop customers and potentially expose the company to supervisory action or customer claims for damages.

To mitigate these risks, it is crucial to #vendor_management :

- **Select service providers that comply with data protection requirements**.
- **Conclude clear data processing agreements** that outline security expectations, data protection measures, and other essential requirements.
- **Conduct supplier audits** to assess their security practices, incident response capabilities, and data protection measures.
- **Regularly review third-party contracts**.
- Implement **strict access controls, encryption, and monitoring**. Proactive management of third-party risks helps e-shop operators strengthen overall security and maintain customer trust. For example, if an e-shop uses a payment service provider, it must ensure that user data is protected and secured, and a data processing agreement must be concluded with the payment service provider as an authorized processor.

The use of **artificial intelligence (AI)** in e-shops is also becoming increasingly popular, offering benefits such as supporting and improving customer service and enabling better decision-making. AI systems can process large volumes of data quickly, allowing e-shops to gain new insights and make their operations more efficient. However, it is **extremely important to pay attention to the processing of personal data** to protect individuals' privacy and ensure data security, thereby avoiding potential breaches and loss of trust. It is essential to remember that using AI to process personal data is still personal data processing, and therefore, the AI provider may be an authorized processor for the e-shop operator. The use of AI comes with significant responsibility and several risks, particularly concerning personal data protection, transparency, the legality of decisions, and system security, for which the e-shop operator is ultimately responsible.

# Specific Security Measures for E-commerce Platforms

E-shop operators should implement a selection of security measures to ensure secure personal data processing. This list is not exhaustive and may evolve over time and depending on the situation. It is crucial for the e-shop operator to ensure that appropriate measures are in place for each identified risk. Risks for e-shop operators include data leaks due to external attacks or technological errors, employee mistakes, ransomware attacks, outdated software, or any other situation that results in personal data being exposed, destroyed, or becoming unavailable. When developing an e-shop, these risks must be mapped, and a supplementary data protection impact assessment should be conducted if necessary. It is recommended to periodically review risks, taking technological advancements into account.

## Data Security ABC (General Measures)

E-shop security encompasses more than just passwords; it also requires the use of **updated and appropriate software solutions** to make e-shops less vulnerable to security weaknesses. For example, in October 2022, the RIA (Estonian Information System Authority) incident handling department identified hundreds of e-shops using an outdated Magento platform version, making them vulnerable to a specific security flaw.

To prevent cyber incidents and data leaks, it is important to:

- **Continuously update software**.
- **Limit access to the e-shop administrator panel on a need-to-know basis**.
- Implement **two-factor authentication for e-shop administrator accounts**, in addition to consumers.
- **Ensure logging and backup**.
- **Ensure continuous monitoring of information systems**.
- **Always close unnecessary remote desktop connections**.
- **Ensure that internal applications are not accessible from external networks**.
- **Regularly test information systems for security vulnerabilities**.
- **Train employees and cooperation partners**.

Furthermore, it is advisable to involve an **independent information security manager** within the company, create a crisis plan, and ensure cyber hygiene with the necessary tools.

Specifically, the personal data processor must:

- **Prevent unauthorized access** by unauthorized persons to data processing equipment and data carriers (including external hard drives, USB sticks, CD- and DVD-discs).
- **Prevent unauthorized reading, copying, deletion, storage, etc., of data**.
- Enable **post-hoc identification** of who accessed, saved, modified, or deleted data, and when.
- Enable **post-hoc identification** of to whom, when, why, and how personal data was transmitted.
- Ensure that e-shop employees have **access only to the data and processing methods necessary for their work tasks**, as unnecessary access rights can lead to problems with confidentiality, availability, and integrity.
- **Design work organization to comply with data protection requirements**.
- Provide **appropriate training and periodic updates on security measures** to individuals processing personal data under their authority.

E-mail domains must also be protected to prevent unauthorized takeover and subsequent sending of emails from those domains. Technologies such as SPF, DMARC, DKIM, MTA-STS, DNSSEC, and DANE should be employed to make email address spoofing as difficult as possible.

## Password Security

Passwords must **not be stored in plain text on the server**, as they are not sufficiently secured. To allow the server to verify the correctness of a password without storing it in plain text, **hashing and salting** are used for password storage.

- **Hashing** is a one-way operation where the same word (password, document) always produces the same mathematical code, but it is generally impossible to derive the original message from the hash.
- **Salting** involves adding a non-secret (usually random) value during encryption or hashing, making it harder to crack the password even if hashing is used. Two identical files will always have the same hash, while two different files will always have different hashes, even if the difference between them is minimal. Therefore, **hashing is recommended for password protection**, and passwords must not be stored in plain text on the server.

**Password managers** are a suitable measure primarily for consumers, as they help generate unique, strong passwords for each website and thus reduce the risk of multiple user accounts being compromised if one password leaks. They also save users the trouble of remembering passwords and simplify logging into websites by automatically filling in username and password fields. Well-known password managers include LastPass, Bitwarden, 1Password, Dashlane, and KeePass.

In addition to multi-factor authentication and strong password usage, e-shops must ensure that **consumer authentication sessions do not remain active indefinitely**. The number of allowed incorrect password attempts should also be limited. For example, if a consumer simply closes a web page instead of logging out, the session may remain valid on the e-shop server for some time, allowing an attacker to hijack such active sessions without requiring re-authentication.

## Use of Cookies and Tracking Pixels

Websites use various types of cookies, categorized into essential and additional cookies:

- **Strictly necessary cookies:** These support core website functions like user authentication, session management, or language preferences and do not directly measure user activity.
- **Additional cookies:** These include analytical or advertising cookies that can directly or indirectly identify individuals. **Prior voluntary consent from the user is required** for placing these types of cookies. Consent must be informed, meaning the website visitor must be aware of the duration of cookie usage and whether third parties can access them. This information should be provided in the e-shop's data protection terms. Furthermore, consent must be **withdrawable as easily as it was given** on the website. An example of information collected via cookies is an e-shop distinguishing between first-time and returning website users. #cookies 

**Tracking pixels** are small, imperceptible graphic elements added to website or email code. They are loaded from the e-shop's server when a consumer opens a marketing email sent by the e-shop or clicks on various links within the e-shop. This allows the e-shop operator to know if and when the consumer opened the email or what they viewed in the e-shop. Pixels work in conjunction with cookies and thus enable tracking of consumer activities. Legally, this constitutes access to information stored on the user's terminal equipment and falls under the e-Privacy Directive. For example, a pixel transmits information about user activities, allowing the e-shop to know who opened a specific offer, page, or email. E-shops need user consent for using pixels and must provide consumers with the option to opt out of such activity tracking.

## Encryption of Data Transfer and Storage

In an e-shop, consumers are identified based on their user account data. After registering or logging in as an existing user, the consumer typically clicks "Enter" (Sisene). Subsequently, the consumer's device's web browser transmits their personal data to the e-shop's web server.

AKI's current practice has shown that unencrypted internet connections are sometimes still used for transmitting personal data, which allows network traffic to be intercepted or interfered with. As a result, consumer personal data (e.g., username and password) can fall into the hands of unauthorized persons, who may then misuse it.

It is important to remember that e-shops may contain sensitive information directly related to consumers, such as health data, or personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political or religious views, or sexual orientation. Therefore, it is **extremely important to always use encryption for receiving, transmitting, and storing personal data** in an e-shop. #security #change The purpose of data encryption is to make it unreadable to third parties. Encryption is particularly crucial when individuals shop online and enter, for example, their bank card details. A secure data transfer website can be identified by **HTTPS** in the address bar; if the address starts with HTTP, the connection is not secure, and data may leak.

If chatbots are used, the **conversation between the client and the chatbot must also be encrypted** to keep the data secure. Furthermore, if a third-party provider offers the chatbot service, a data processing agreement must be concluded between the parties, addressing security aspects. If data is not sufficiently protected, third parties could use it, for example, to gain access to the consumer's various social media accounts or email services, or for financial fraud and extortion. For example, the TLS (Transport Layer Security) information security protocol is suitable for secure data transfer, helping to ensure the integrity and confidentiality of consumer authentication data and email transmissions in the e-shop. Similarly, when sending invoices or notifications via email (e.g., when goods have arrived), it is crucial to use encryption during transmission, at least at the StartTLS level.

## Logging and Backup

E-shops must use **effective monitoring systems and logs** to track activities within the system, enabling the detection of unusual activity or queries. Generating and managing identifiable traces or logs of data processing operations is one of the central aspects of implementing integrated data protection. The data processor (e-shop) has an obligation to identify and register all personal data breaches. These are security breaches that lead to the accidental or unlawful loss, destruction, alteration, or unauthorized access to or disclosure of processed personal data. Thus, without maintaining logs, it is impossible to adhere to personal data processing principles or ensure the security of data processing. The GDPR does not specify the format for logs, but it is important that traces are left for data input, modification, viewing, transmission, and deletion.

**Data backup** (or copying) is also essential to enable data restoration in case something happens to the data. This helps prevent data loss during major system failures. At least one backup copy should definitely be stored **offline (outside the main network)**.

## Deceptive Web Design

Design tactics, referred to as **"dark patterns,"** influence shoppers to make decisions that harm their privacy. The main characteristics of dark patterns include: #dark_pattern 

- **Complex and confusing language**.
- **Inappropriate layout** that makes privacy-damaging choices more prominent, such as pre-ticked cookie consent boxes in an e-shop.
- **Nagging**, which means repetitive pressure.
- **Hindering the achievement of privacy-related goals**, for example, making the process of changing settings or deleting accounts difficult.
- **Forcing users to provide more data than necessary**.

Therefore, e-shops should ensure they do not use deceptive patterns in their websites, such as imposing excessive data collection requirements, like making it mandatory to provide additional data for account deletion.

Privacy-supporting design patterns are those that:

- Use **clear and neutral language**.
- Offer **appropriately timed and relevant consent options**.
- Ensure **privacy-protective default settings**.
- **Reduce the number of clicks required to change privacy preferences**. This approach helps ensure that personal data collection aligns with principles such as transparency, user control, data minimization, and informed consent. More information on the impact of website and mobile application design on privacy can be found on the AKI website. For instance, a 2024 Global Privacy Enforcement Network monitoring identified that in 14% of cases, users were repeatedly asked to reconsider their privacy settings or account deletion decisions, thereby reducing the likelihood of completing the action and making the process time-consuming and cumbersome.

## Website Platform Considerations

E-shop operators function on various platforms, ranging from modern cloud-based solutions like Shopify, Magento, and WooCommerce, to older custom systems that have been in use for years. Older and/or self-managed solutions may lack crucial updates, making them more vulnerable to cyber threats and non-compliant with GDPR security requirements. Newer platforms often feature built-in security and compliance functionalities. Maintaining security across different platforms requires a proactive approach, prioritizing **regular updates, patch management, and security configurations** to prevent breaches and data leaks.

**Older systems pose a higher security risk** because they may no longer receive vendor support or security patches. Businesses using outdated e-commerce software must assess whether these systems can be protected through additional firewalls, encryption, and access controls, or if a migration to a more secure platform is necessary. Regardless of the platform used, the guiding principle should be **security by design and by default**, ensuring that customer data is protected at every stage of the purchase.

## Audits and Testing

**Regular security audits and tests are essential** for maintaining a secure and GDPR-compliant e-commerce environment. Audits help identify errors or vulnerabilities in platforms, databases, and third-party interfaces, thereby ensuring the secure operation of e-shop information systems. Companies should conduct audits of data access, retention policies, and security controls, while also considering external penetration testing and vulnerability assessments to evaluate their protection against real threats.

Beyond technical testing, companies should also **audit their data processing operations** to confirm compliance with GDPR requirements. This includes reviewing consent management practices, verifying data processing agreements with third-party providers, and ensuring that data protection impact assessments are conducted when introducing new technologies. By regularly auditing both their security infrastructure and data management practices, e-shop operators can reduce risks, demonstrate accountability, and create a secure online shopping experience that safeguards customer privacy.

# Incident Management

A data breach in an e-shop can have severe consequences, including financial damage, fines related to the breach, and damage to customer trust. Under GDPR (Articles 33 and 34), companies must act swiftly to contain the breach, assess its impact, and, if necessary, **notify both authorities and affected individuals**.

## Containment and Impact Assessment

As soon as a breach is discovered, immediate measures must be taken to stop, for example, unauthorized access and prevent further damage. This may include:

- **Blocking suspicious IP addresses** or compromised user accounts.
- **Temporarily suspending affected systems or databases**.
- **Resetting passwords** and reviewing access logs for unusual activity.

Concurrently, the breach must be assessed to determine:

- **What data was compromised?** (e.g., customer names, payment data, passwords).
- **How many individuals are affected?**.
- **How did the breach occur?** (e.g., hacking, human error, system vulnerability).

## Notification of Data Breach

If the breach is likely to pose a risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, the **Andmekaitse Inspektsioon must be notified within 72 hours** of becoming aware of the incident. The report (also known as a breach notification) must include:

- **A description of the breach and its cause**.
- **The categories and number of affected individuals and data records**.
- **The measures taken to mitigate the breach and prevent its recurrence**.

If the breach poses a **high risk to individuals** (e.g., leaked financial data, passwords, or sensitive data), affected customers must be notified immediately with clear instructions on how they can protect themselves (e.g., resetting passwords, monitoring for fraud).

## Analysis, Remediation, and Security Enhancement

After containing the breach initially, a thorough investigation must be conducted to determine the root cause and implement corrective measures. This may involve:

- **Patching security vulnerabilities** (e.g., software patches, stronger access controls).
- **Reviewing internal policies and employee training** to prevent future errors.
- **Improving data encryption and monitoring systems** to detect suspicious activity earlier.

## Documentation and Learning

The GDPR requires organizations to **document all data breaches**, even if notification is not mandatory. Detailed records of the incident, the investigation, the response measures taken, and any preventive measures implemented must be kept. This documentation helps demonstrate compliance during audits by supervisory authorities. Furthermore, a well-prepared incident response plan ensures that the company can react quickly and effectively to future breaches, minimizing damage and maintaining customer trust.

# Responsibility and Penalties

The Andmekaitse Inspektsioon (AKI) has the authority to initiate a national supervisory procedure against an e-shop operator, either based on a memorandum or complaint, or on its own initiative. If this supervisory procedure concludes with a precept (an official order), and the e-commerce company disregards the precept and continues its unlawful activities, AKI has the right to apply **coercive fines to enforce compliance**. Examples of such violations include: an e-shop collecting an unjustified amount of personal data from consumers, consumer personal data leaking due to weak security measures, or sending direct marketing without consent.

The maximum coercive fine for failing to comply with an AKI precept is **up to 20,000,000 euros or up to 4% of the company's total annual worldwide turnover from the previous financial year**, whichever amount is greater. In addition, AKI may initiate misdemeanor proceedings against the e-commerce company if a misdemeanor offense is identified. For violations of personal data processing requirements, the fine for a natural person can be **up to 10,000,000 euros**. For a legal person, the corresponding fine can be **up to 10,000,000 euros or up to 2% of its total annual worldwide turnover from the previous financial year**, whichever amount is greater.
