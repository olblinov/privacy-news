# Summary

Inteligo Media SA, publisher of the online news publication avocatnet.ro, was fined RON 42,714 (approximately EUR 9,000) by the Romanian National Supervisory Authority for the Processing of Personal Data (ANSPDCP) for allegedly processing user email addresses without proper consent for its daily "Personal Update" email newsletter. The case concerns the interpretation of Directive 2002/58/EC and its relationship with the GDPR, specifically whether the email addresses were obtained "in the context of the sale of a product or a service" and if the newsletter constitutes "direct marketing" under Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58/EC. The Advocate General proposes that Inteligo Media's marketing practices do not conflict with EU data protection rules, asserting that the email addresses were indeed obtained in the context of a sale (due to indirect remuneration through promotion of paid subscriptions) and the newsletter qualifies as direct marketing for similar services, making Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58 applicable and Article 6 of the GDPR unnecessary.

# Details

## Background

Inteligo Media SA operates avocatnet.ro, an online news publication that provides information to the public about daily legislative changes in Romania. Users could initially view a limited number of articles (six) for free. In July 2018, Inteligo Media introduced a **"Premium Service" paid subscription system** for users who wished to access more articles. To subscribe to the Premium Service, users had to create a free account, which required providing their email address and accepting contractual terms. Subscribers to this service received the right to access two additional articles per month and a daily email newsletter called **"Personal Update"**, unless they opted out. The "Personal Update" newsletter summarized legislative news from the previous day, including **hyperlinks to relevant articles** on the publication's website. Users were given the opportunity to opt out of receiving the "Personal Update" when creating their account via a tickbox, and also had an "Unsubscribe" option in each email. #direct_marketing 

On September 26, 2019, the Romanian National Supervisory Authority for the Processing of Personal Data (ANSPDCP) fined Inteligo Media RON 42,714 (equivalent to EUR 9,000). The ANSPDCP concluded that Inteligo Media had processed the personal data (email addresses, passwords, usernames) of 4,357 users since July 2018 without an appropriate legal basis, specifically without obtaining express consent for the daily transmission of the "Personal Update," thereby infringing **Article 5(1)(a) and (b), Article 6(1)(a), and Article 7 of the GDPR**. Inteligo Media challenged this administrative penalty, and after initial dismissals and appeals, the case was referred to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling by the Curtea de Apel București (Court of Appeal, Bucharest, Romania). The referring court sought clarity on the appropriate legal basis for processing, the conditions for lawfulness under Directive 2002/58/EC and the GDPR, and specifically the concepts of **"sale of a product or a service"** and **"direct marketing"**.

## Legal Analysis

The core legal questions revolve around Article 13 of Directive 2002/58/EC, which governs unsolicited communications. Article 13(1) generally requires **prior consent ("opt-in")** for using electronic mail for direct marketing. However, Article 13(2) provides an exception: electronic contact details obtained from customers "in the context of the sale of a product or a service" can be used for direct marketing of "own similar products or services" **without prior consent**, provided customers are given a clear opportunity to **object ("opt-out")** at collection and in each message.

The Advocate General first analyzed whether the "Personal Update" newsletter constituted **direct marketing**. He asserted that for a communication to be direct marketing, it must pursue a **commercial purpose** and be addressed directly and individually to a consumer. He found that the "Personal Update" had a commercial purpose because, by providing hyperlinks and "teasers" to articles, it **enticed users to consume their limited free articles quickly**, thereby encouraging them to purchase a full subscription under Inteligo Media's "metered paywall" model. This strategy aims to generate revenue. Since the newsletter was delivered directly to users' email inboxes, the condition of being addressed directly and individually was also met. Thus, the Advocate General concluded that the "Personal Update" constitutes direct marketing.

Next, the Advocate General examined whether the email addresses were obtained **"in the context of the sale of a product or a service"** within the meaning of Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58/EC. While a "sale" typically implies payment, the Advocate General referred to case-law where remuneration for a service is not necessarily paid by the direct beneficiaries. He argued that the "free of charge" service provided by Inteligo Media (the newsletter and limited free articles) was intended to promote the full subscription plan, meaning the cost of this activity was **incorporated into the price of the main service**. This indirect form of remuneration was deemed sufficient to satisfy the "payment" requirement of a sale. Furthermore, the Advocate General noted that in the modern digital age, **data itself is treated as a commodity**, suggesting that furnishing personal data (like an email address) in exchange for a valuable good or service could be sufficient for data collection "in the context of a sale". #change 

Regarding the relationship between the GDPR and Directive 2002/58/EC, Article 95 of the GDPR states that it does not impose additional obligations where specific, objective-aligned obligations already exist in Directive 2002/58/EC. The Advocate General argued that Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58 **comprehensively governs the conditions and purposes of processing for automated direct marketing** in the context of a sale, including the question of consent, as it is an exception to the prior consent rule in Article 13(1). Therefore, he concluded that the **lawfulness of processing can be established solely on the basis of Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58, making recourse to Article 6(1)(a) to (f) of the GDPR "neither possible nor necessary"**. #consent 

The Advocate General considered the third question, which asked about national legislation substituting "direct marketing" with "commercial communication," to be **hypothetical and inadmissible** as it was not necessary to resolve the main dispute. Similarly, the fifth question, concerning the supervisory authority's obligation to explain each criterion under Article 83(2) of the GDPR when imposing fines, was deemed unnecessary to answer given his conclusion that the GDPR was not applicable. However, he conditionally observed that supervisory authorities must provide **sufficiently specific and concrete reasons** for their decisions, based on the general principle of good administration and the right to due process, without necessarily detailing every criterion in Article 83(2).

## Decision

Based on his assessment, the Advocate General proposed the following answers to the preliminary questions:

- A user’s email address obtained when creating an online account that grants free access to articles, a daily email newsletter, and optional paid access to more content, **is obtained "in connection with the sale of a product or a service"** within the meaning of Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58/EC.
- The transmission of the daily newsletter described (the "Personal Update") **constitutes "direct marketing" for "similar products or services"** within the meaning of Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58/EC.
- Therefore, when a controller uses a user's email address for a daily newsletter in accordance with Article 13(2) of Directive 2002/58/EC, and the processing is deemed lawful under that provision, **Article 6 of the GDPR is not applicable** due to the relationship governed by Article 95 of the GDPR. #consent 
- The second part of the fourth question (regarding penalties for non-compliance with consent conditions) and the third question (comparing "direct marketing" and "commercial communication") were deemed **unnecessary or inadmissible** given the proposed answers to the other questions.
- The fifth question (regarding the supervisory authority's explanation of fines under GDPR Article 83(2)) was also deemed unnecessary. However, if the GDPR were applicable, the Advocate General noted that a supervisory authority must analyze and explain the basis of the fine with **sufficient specificity** to allow the individual to understand the grounds of the decision, but it is not required to detail the effect of each of the criteria listed in Article 83(2) individually.
