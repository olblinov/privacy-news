# Introduction

The Internet Advertising Bureau (IAB UK) is the industry body for digital advertising, aiming to build a better future for the sector. IAB UK appreciates the opportunity to respond to the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) consultation on its draft revised guidance regarding the use of storage and access technologies. However, IAB UK and its members have **significant concerns about the substance and timing of the guidance and its associated Impact Assessment (IA)**.

The draft guidance significantly expands its scope, posing major issues for digital businesses and potentially impacting their operational and commercial continuity, especially within the digital advertising ecosystem. This consultation arrives during a period of **complex regulatory evolution**, including work by the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) on digital market competition and the ICO's own 'consent or pay' guidance, necessitating careful coordination. IAB UK finds the draft IA to be **cursory and incomplete**, requiring further work to serve as a robust foundation for decision-making. The guidance risks **unintended negative impacts on the digital economy**, disproportionately affecting ad-supported services through substantial cost burdens and reduced revenues, potentially forcing many to exit the market. IAB UK recommends that the **ICO pauses the adoption of the guidance** for broader consultation, alignment with other regulators (like the CMA), prioritization of other work (such as reviewing 'strictly necessary exemptions' under PECR), and a full assessment of market dynamics and potential consequences.

# Draft Revised Guidance: Overarching Concerns

## Breadth of the ICO’s Approach

The proposed revised guidance **significantly expands the scope of PECR Regulation 6** by categorizing a broader range of widely used techniques as 'storage and access,' thereby requiring user consent. This expansion impacts fundamental technical operations in digital advertising and wider online commerce, including alternative advertising models and privacy-enhancing technologies that the ICO itself promotes. The guidance and IA do not adequately explain how digital services can operate when users choose to "reject all" uses of PECR-regulated technologies.

## Economic Impact on the Digital Advertising Industry

Implementation and enforcement of this guidance are expected to **negatively affect the digital economy**, particularly the digital advertising industry. IAB UK believes the draft IA **underestimates the true cost and disruption** for businesses and consumers. While the Government has asked regulators to support investment and growth, industry stakeholders feel the ICO's previous guidance and its strategy to deter identifiers for ad targeting have constrained the market, and the revised guidance will exacerbate these difficulties. The IA acknowledges that increased compliance costs and reduced advertising revenue could lead to **slower growth and market exit for some ad-supported services**, which heavily rely on advertising revenue and may lack viable alternative business models. This stance conflicts with the Government's agenda for investment and growth, as the ICO's approach prioritizes legal certainty and data protection over balancing other user rights and supporting innovation and competition.

## Timing and Regulatory Coordination

The unexpected publication of this guidance, along with the 'online tracking strategy,' **contradicted the ICO's public commitment to providing predictable guidance**. This lack of advance notice creates challenges for **regulatory certainty and business planning**. The timing also causes confusion regarding alignment with other regulators, such as the CMA, and the Government’s broader agenda, making it unclear if these initiatives are coordinated towards the same outcome.

## Damaging Narrative Around Personalised Advertising

IAB UK is concerned that the ICO's guidance creates a **damaging negative narrative around personalised advertising and the 'consent or pay' model**. This narrative has led to misunderstandings, such as the mistaken belief that paid-for options constitute 'paying for data protection,' which the ICO needs to address in its communications.

## Forced Business Model Changes

The ICO's continued efforts to **steer the market away from targeted advertising towards commercially unproven alternatives** are a significant concern. The guidance lacks a detailed legal rationale for this approach and makes unsubstantiated statements about unquantified risks, disregarding evidence of consumers' growing understanding of ad-supported models. The combined effect of the storage & access guidance and the 'consent or pay' guidance is seen as **dictating business models** to online service providers, leaving them with limited or unfeasible commercial choices. These choices include:

- Adopting non-personalised ads, which may be impractical, generate substantially lower revenue, and still require consent.
- Providing services entirely for free to unconsented users, which is economically unviable for commercial organizations.
- Implementing paid-for options, which many users are unwilling or unable to pay for, especially younger audiences and new market entrants. The likely consequence for many ad-supported services is **market exit, reducing consumer choice and media plurality in the UK**, making the market less competitive. IAB UK argues that such market interventions are **outside the ICO’s statutory remit**, as its guidance should remain business neutral.

## ‘Essential/Strictly Necessary’ Storage

The ICO has chosen not to define what constitutes 'essential' use of technology for economically viable ad-supported services. The revised guidance problematically asserts that 'strictly necessary' should be viewed **solely from the user’s perspective** (not the provider’s) and that digital advertising storage/access can **never be considered strictly necessary** because ad-supported services are 'technically' possible without it. IAB UK disputes this technical possibility and highlights that these points are not set out in law. This interpretation needs thorough reassessment due to its broader implications for the digital economy. Existing interpretations of PECR have created legal barriers to functions supporting a healthy digital advertising market, even those unrelated to personalised ad targeting, such as audience measurement. IAB UK urges the ICO to **pause work on this guidance to first review and revise the interpretation of 'strictly necessary'** in the context of today's internet, particularly given the expanded scope of 'storage and access'. The ICO's proposal to HM Treasury to 'relax' enforcement of consent requirements to encourage alternative models is not a legally robust or acceptable option, as the revised guidance would bind the ICO and expose businesses to increased litigation risks.

## Critical Gaps in Relation to Alternative Advertising Models

While the ICO states its work will support growth by clarifying the legal framework and enabling alternative advertising forms, the revised guidance **lacks crucial detail**. For example, it generally states that contextual advertising makes compliance easier but does not explain which alternatives the ICO has considered or how they could operate in a 'reject all' scenario. The guidance contradicts itself by stating consent is required for all uses of storage and access technologies for online advertising purposes (including contextual/non-personalised ads), and that these do not qualify for the 'strictly necessary' exemption. This inconsistency creates uncertainty. **Further work is needed to identify viable, profitable, and commercially attractive alternative models**, and their implications for UK consumers, to provide online service providers with a roadmap to future legal stability and revenue.

## Fragmentation

The ICO’s guidance is becoming **increasingly fragmented**, with the revised storage and access guidance separate from and inconsistent with the 'consent or pay' guidance, the ICO/CMA paper on 'harmful design,' and a separate paper on 'expectations for online advertising proposals'. This fragmentation makes compliance difficult as multiple documents address different aspects of a user experience. The ICO should **consolidate its guidance into a single resource with practical examples**.

## Summary of Challenges

As drafted, the guidance:

- **Expands the scope of PECR**, subjecting common technology uses to prior consent.
- **Lacks details on viable alternatives** for serving ads to unconsented users, despite encouraging their adoption.
- **Seeks to impose unproven and potentially unfeasible revenue models**, exceeding the ICO’s remit.
- **Compounds adverse effects** by prioritizing its adoption before other critical work.
- **Risks harming the digital advertising industry** through increased costs and reduced revenue, potentially forcing market exit.
- Was **published without notice**, undermining predictability and creating confusion.
- **Contributes to a negative narrative** about personalised advertising.
- **Interprets "strictly necessary" storage as problematically narrow**, constraining low-risk practices and innovation.

# Clarity of Guidance

## Section: What are storage and access technologies

- **Link decoration and navigational tracking**: IAB UK argues that the position on link decoration/URL tracking falling under PECR Regulation 6 is **not convincingly made** and requests the ICO re-examine this and provide a clearer legal rationale. Clarity is also needed on the **differentiation between static and dynamic link decoration** based on the type of additional data (non-user vs. user level) rather than technology, and which party is responsible for compliance.
- **Privacy-Enhancing Technologies (PETs)**: The ICO should clarify how PECR and UK GDPR apply to PETs that rely on access or storage, and consider the impact on investment and adoption of PETs.

## Section: How do the PECR rules relate to the UK GDPR? What does PECR say about subsequent processing?

This section would benefit from **expansion on the meaning of 'subsequent' processing** and its relation to original PECR consent, ideally with a flowchart or case examples.

## Section: ‘How do we manage consent in practice?’ Consent UI and Graphics Issues

Further detail and clarification are needed for this section:

- The consent UI graphics are **difficult to follow** when viewed sequentially, and their **color coding lacks clear explanation**, risking misinterpretation.
- Ambiguity exists regarding whether graphics are **examples or prescriptive requirements**.
- The guidance should be flexible enough to accommodate the **Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF)'s established language** for communicating cookie purposes.
- The examples **do not address 'consent or pay' or subscriber models**, a significant gap, and are inconsistently cross-referenced with the separate 'consent or pay' guidance.
- The relationship between this guidance and the ICO/CMA paper on 'harmful design' remains unclear.

## Withdrawal of Consent and Data Deletion

- **General**: More detail and clarity are needed on the actions and responsibilities of different parties in the supply chain regarding data deletion, including sending and receiving erasure request notifications.
- **Conflation of Separate Legal Rights**: The guidance **merges the right to withdraw consent (Article 7 UK GDPR) and the right to erasure (Article 17 UK GDPR)**, creating confusion. IAB UK suggests redrafting to provide legal clarity, distinguishing between PECR consent withdrawal (stopping access/storage for the given purpose) and UK GDPR erasure requests (affecting personal data processing where consent was the sole legal basis). Withdrawal of consent may require deleting personal data tied to a specific purpose, but a deletion request does not automatically mean consent has been withdrawn for all purposes.
- **Scope of Deletion**: Clarity is needed on whether deletion in response to PECR consent withdrawal applies to **data already stored on a user's terminal equipment**, if deletion is technically unfeasible, and if access for deletion is permitted. It should also clarify if **cookies used to store consent preferences can be retained**. Expectations for third parties (e.g., when a third-party cookie is placed) need to be clarified. Guidance is also needed on the deletion of **non-personal data stored outside the user's device**, noting that searching for and deleting such data may be impractical or impossible.
- **Unintended Consequences of Data Deletion**: Users withdrawing consent may not intend to delete all historical data, especially if it negatively impacts their user experience. Organizations should be allowed to provide users with meaningful choices about whether consent withdrawal automatically triggers complete erasure.
- **Practical Complexities**: The guidance creates practical complexities for data deletion in digital advertising contexts, requiring more detail on what information needs to be deleted, potentially using examples like the Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF) implementation.
- **Notification: Technical Feasibility**: Complying with notification requirements (informing users and third parties of consent withdrawal/deletion) is **technically challenging, if not infeasible**, particularly in programmatic advertising (Real-Time Bidding - RTB).
    - Online service providers using Consent Management Platforms (CMPs) often lack means to identify or contact users.
    - In RTB, data flows to multiple partners, no universal mechanism tracks where data has been sent, and propagating deletion signals is nearly impossible with current technology.
    - While the IAB Tech Lab's Data Deletion Request Framework offers a starting point, full implementation requires significant resources and time. The ICO's enforcement approach and IA should account for these practical constraints and costs.

## Section: ‘How do the rules apply to online advertising?’

- **Measurement: Definition and Scope**: While the guidance acknowledges ad measurement is "intrinsically linked" to online advertising, more detail is needed on:
    - The **definition and scope of 'measurement'**.
    - Whether consent for 'advertising' implicitly covers measurement.
    - Consent requirements for ad measurement in **non-personalised advertising contexts** (contextual ads, search ads), and how measurement can be carried out when users opt out of interest-based ads. Publishers and service providers need to measure non-targeted ads.
    - How the "intrinsically linked" principle applies to activities supporting ad inventory management, functional ad delivery, or anti-fraud measures.
    - How measurement applies to **affiliate marketing**, clarifying that a 'single' or 'grouped' consent for 'advertising purposes' can cover measurement for different parties within a closed affiliate network.
- **Consent Exemption for Ad Measurement**: IAB UK maintains that **ad measurement cookies should be categorized as essential and exempted from consent requirements**. They recommend explicitly acknowledging that storing/accessing information for **aggregated measurement of non-targeted advertising should not require consent** under PECR Regulation 6, provided it's limited to that purpose.

## Other

- **Alternative Advertising Methods**: The guidance presents non-personalised advertising as a viable alternative but **fails to detail how these alternatives can operate viably and compliantly** without consent for underlying storage and access technologies (e.g., for frequency capping, fraud detection). This undermines the development of non-personalised models, as they face the same consent challenges as personalised ones. Inconsistencies exist with the 'consent or pay' guidance, which implies contextual targeting as an alternative to personalised advertising.
- **Granular Consent**: The guidance's tension between requiring specific/granular consent and avoiding "long lists of checkboxes" creates uncertainty. Increased consent requirements due to the guidance's expansive scope will **counterproductively increase user disruption**. The guidance needs to explain how to resolve these competing expectations.
- **Bundled/Grouped Purposes**: A clearer and more consistent approach to **grouping related consent purposes** would be beneficial. The guidance currently addresses this in fragmented ways, and explicit confirmation of categorized groupings and compliant types is needed.

# Draft Impact Assessment Feedback

## Process and Methodology

IAB UK members seriously question the IA's conclusion that negative effects for businesses are outweighed by marginal societal benefits of reduced data protection harms and increased regulatory certainty. The draft IA **falls significantly short of providing a comprehensive analysis** and has substantial gaps. It disproportionately focuses on digital advertising while overlooking broader implications for any digital business reliant on 'storage and access' technologies. The ICO appears **not to have consulted with other regulatory bodies, especially the CMA**, whose expertise is crucial for understanding the impact on digital markets and consumers. The IA needs to include more explanation and analysis of how different rights (consumer, business) have been balanced and justifications for conclusions. The **compound impact of this guidance, 'consent or pay' guidance, and ICO's stance on alternative ad models should be assessed holistically**.

## Incomplete Impact Assessment

The draft IA does not fully reflect the ICO’s own framework, which requires balancing data protection rights against other rights and freedoms proportionately, ensuring regulatory action is proportionate and not unduly burdensome, and promoting economic growth. The document **lacks evidence that such balancing has occurred** or how business continuity/viability and consumer access to services have been weighed against privacy concerns.

## Viable Business Model Analysis

The IA **does not adequately consider the effect of the guidance on the operation and viability of different ad models**. It lacks analysis of the likely demand for advertising models that use less personal data, ignoring that advertisers pay a premium for targeted ads and significantly less for unaddressable audiences. Analysis by the CMA found that UK publishers earned **around 70% less revenue when unable to sell personalised advertising**. This significant adverse impact should be central to the IA's cost-benefit analysis.

## Conflict with Economic Growth Objectives

Despite the ICO's stated commitment to "empower responsible innovation and sustainable economic growth," the IA **identifies no benefits that would advance this goal**. The guidance exacerbates existing difficulties for digital advertising businesses rather than resolving them. The costs captured in the IA clearly show negative impacts on businesses, with likely direct and indirect consequences for economic growth and innovation that are not explored.

## Underestimated Costs

The IA's cost analysis **does not adequately reflect real-world requirements**. Familiarisation costs are severely underestimated at £124 per organization, as storage and access technology management is highly complex and spans multiple business functions. The IA **fails to quantify business costs in monetary terms** and lacks analysis of the economic impact on affected sectors, including digital advertising.

## Overlooked Indirect and Operational Costs

The IA does not identify indirect impacts, such as:

- **Increased 'bounce' rates** for online service providers due to 'consent or pay' options, reducing audience size, ad inventory, and engagement. This is problematic even for non-personalised ad models.
- Publishers needing to integrate and maintain **secure payment gateways**, handle disputes, and manage evolving user expectations.
- **Revenue uncertainties** from subscription or pay-per-use models. These factors contribute to a long-term financial and operational burden that should be fully accounted for.

## Overestimation of Consumer Risk

The IA's risk analysis relies on **disproportionate examples** (gambling, financial, health-related scenarios) that mischaracterize typical digital advertising risks. It fails to acknowledge that these examples are not general market practice and that existing regulations already address them. The IA also overlooks that many uses of storage and access technologies are **low intrusion and low risk**, and personal data is often pseudonymized. Most digital advertising is not harmful, and there is little evidence of materialised harm. The IA's benefits are thus inaccurate, as they don't reflect the actual lower risk and don't outweigh significant adverse effects. The risk assessment needs to be reworked to be more illustrative and grounded in typical advertiser practices.

## Consumer Preferences and Expectations

The IA presents an **imbalanced consumer perspective**, disconnected from actual consumer sentiment. The ICO's own report shows personalised advertising is the **least concerning aspect of data handling** for most people, and less than a third disagree with seeing targeted ads in exchange for free content. Research for IAB UK found over half of the population prefers personalised ads for free content. Consumers are expected to see ads and functional ad management (e.g., geographical ad selection, frequency-capping, anti-fraud) without requiring consent, and without these, their user experience would be poorer. This needs fuller exploration in the IA.

## Cost Impacts for Consumers

The IA **does not sufficiently recognize the direct and indirect costs to consumers** of reduced access to free, ad-supported content and services. Digital advertising saves UK households an average of £580 per year, and 70% of adults consider it vital that these services remain free. The guidance's consequence is likely to be **more content moved behind paywalls**, which is detrimental to consumers, given their low propensity to pay for online content. The IA lacks evidence that consumers want or prefer paid-for options or how they affect user experience. It also **does not explore implications for digital inclusion**.

## Impact on Advertisers

The IA's theoretical approach ignores actual advertiser behavior, which is driven by performance and ROI, typically higher for targeted advertising. Untargeted advertising performs poorly, and **advertiser demand for it remains weak**, undermining the investment case for alternative models the ICO prefers. This conflicts with the Government's goal of fostering growth.

## Impact on Competition

The IA **lacks analysis of competition and market impact**, including for ad-supported services. The guidance risks **undermining investment in the UK market and leading to consolidation**, as smaller online service providers struggle with profitability and increased compliance costs. This could result in fewer choices for consumers, increased market share for larger providers, reduced innovation, and higher prices. The ICO should consult the CMA to assess the impact on competition.

## Inconsistency of Stated Costs and Benefits

The IA claims to address consumer 'fatigue and irritation' from consent banners but acknowledges the guidance will **increase friction due to more consent requirements**. If functions like frequency-capping require consent, users will see **repeated ads, increasing annoyance**. The claim that the guidance will provide regulatory certainty for businesses is also contradicted by the likely added complexity and legal confusion it would bring.

## Conclusion

Before finalization, the ICO must address significant issues with the draft IA, including its methodology, balancing of interests (business viability, consumer access, privacy), analysis of advertising business models, commitment to economic growth, cost assessments (underestimated familiarisation, overlooked indirect/operational), presentation of harms, consumer preferences, competition impact, and contradictory claims. A **more balanced, evidence-based approach is essential** to genuinely consider the full spectrum of impacts on businesses, consumers, and the digital economy.

# Appendix 1

## Data Deletion: Clarifying Separate PECR and UK GDPR Requirements

IAB UK suggests a redraft to clarify the distinction between **PECR consent withdrawal** and **UK GDPR consent withdrawal and/or requests for erasure**.

- Under PECR, if a user withdraws consent for access and storage on a device, organizations must stop accessing and storing data on the device for the purpose for which consent was given.
- Under UK GDPR, if a user withdraws consent for personal data processing via storage and access technologies, organizations must stop using them, cease processing personal data, and inform third parties. This redraft would clarify that UK GDPR obligations (Articles 17 and 19) only arise when PECR consent for processing personal data is withdrawn. It also differentiates that withdrawal of consent may require deleting personal data tied to a specific purpose, but a deletion request does not automatically mean consent has been withdrawn for all purposes.

## Data Deletion Illustrative Example

An example using the Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF) purposes (e.g., Purpose 3: Create profiles for personalised advertising; Purpose 4: Use profiles to select personalised advertising) highlights questions about deletion scope:

- If consent for Purpose 3 is withdrawn, does it require **deleting the entire profile** or just stopping further data mixing? Are organizations expected to track the origin of every data point?
- If consent for Purpose 4 is withdrawn, does it **prevent further use of the profile entirely**, or only in the specific context where consent was withdrawn? Does it trigger profile deletion?
- What type of **notification** must be provided, and what are the ICO's expectations for each party to meet withdrawal and deletion obligations? This example demonstrates the need for more practical detail on how deletion and notification requirements apply in practice, particularly in Real-Time Bidding (RTB).
