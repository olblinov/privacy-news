# Overview of the Preventive Check

## Purpose and Scope of the Inspection

The Data State Inspectorate (Inspekcija) carried out a **preventive check** in 2024, as part of its annual work plan, to assess compliance with personal data protection requirements in organizational privacy policies. This inspection specifically targeted businesses primarily engaged in **mail-order or online retail**. The core objectives were to proactively **prevent potential violations** and gather data-driven insights into how data controllers (pārziņi) interpret the topic of privacy policies. Additionally, the check aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the Inspectorate's previous educational efforts. The scope of the check involved examining the **publicly accessible privacy policies of 30 companies registered in Latvia**.

## Regulatory Framework and Inspectorate's Role

The assessment primarily focused on adherence to the **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**, particularly the information requirements stipulated in **Article 13 and Article 14**. GDPR Article 13 applies when personal data is collected directly from the data subject, while Article 14 applies when data is obtained from other sources. For the majority of cases in this check, data was obtained directly from data subjects, so the Inspectorate largely assessed compliance with GDPR Article 13. The Inspectorate's broader functions, as Latvia's primary data protection supervisory authority, include informing the public about data protection matters and analyzing data processing activities.

## Background and Educational Initiatives

This preventive check was conducted after a period of extensive educational and explanatory activities carried out by the Inspectorate.

- **"Data is Value – Protect It!" Campaign (Spring 2023):** This informational campaign was launched to **educate and inform small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)** about various relevant data protection issues. It included the development of informational materials (such as articles and explanations) and the organization of **in-person workshops** across different Latvian regions, specifically Rēzekne, Liepāja, Jelgava, Valmiera, and Riga, with an option for online participation. A significant outcome of these workshops was the creation and publication of a **universal privacy policy template**.
- **Online Seminar (January 29, 2024):** The Inspectorate also held an online seminar titled "Privacy Policy and Tips for its Development". This seminar was organized in response to observations that many data controllers continued to overlook the importance of creating a privacy policy or were simply unaware of their obligation to do so.
- **Additional Guidance Material:** Furthermore, the Inspectorate has prepared a detailed explanation titled "What is a privacy policy?", intended for both data controllers and their clients (data subjects), to enhance understanding of the document's purpose and necessity.

# Selection Process for Merchants

## Industry Focus

For this initial large-scale privacy policy check, the Inspectorate specifically chose the retail sector, with a focus on **mail-order and online stores**. #change  This sector was considered particularly pertinent because it inherently involves the processing of personal data, thereby reducing the likelihood of selected merchants claiming they do not process such data. The decision to examine **30 merchants** was based on the assessment that this number was manageable to complete within a year, while still providing sufficient insights into the sector's practices and overall compliance with the GDPR.

## Merchant Selection Criteria

The 30 merchants selected for the check were drawn from a larger list of 3680 companies that were registered as mail-order or online retailers as of May 16, 2024, a list obtained from the State Revenue Service (VID). The selection adhered to a specific set of criteria, applied in the following order:

- **Legal Entity Status:** The selected companies had to be active, registered private legal entities in Latvia, excluding individual merchants or self-employed individuals.
- **Primary Business Activity:** Their primary activity had to be registered under **NACE 2 code 47.91**, which covers retail sale via mail-order houses or via the Internet.
- **Prior Inspections:** The companies must not have been subject to any previous inspections (including initiative, preventive, or other types) by the Inspectorate in 2022 or 2023.
- **Financial Turnover:** Their turnover needed to fall within the definition of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), specifically between **2 million and 50 million EUR**. If an insufficient number of candidates met this criterion, micro-enterprises with turnover closer to the 2 million EUR threshold were also considered.
- **Corporate Structure:** The selected entities could not be part of a group of companies or a larger corporate structure.
- **Online Presence:** They were required to have an active website with a **.lv domain name**.

# Check Methodology and Findings

## Check Process Details

Upon selecting the merchants, the Inspectorate developed a **control checklist** to systematically evaluate their privacy policies. This checklist incorporated key information points mandated by GDPR Articles 13 and 14, such as the data controller's name, contact details, purposes of data processing, legal bases, recipients of data, data storage duration, and data subject rights. The level of compliance for each criterion was recorded and visually represented using a color-coding system (green for fulfilled, orange for partially fulfilled, and red for not fulfilled).

Any identified non-compliances were formally documented, including written observations and screenshots from the respective websites. Subsequently, letters were dispatched to each data controller, providing information about the check, its objectives, and detailing any detected deficiencies, along with a request to rectify these issues within a reasonable timeframe. The Inspectorate also used this opportunity to explain the importance of publishing a privacy policy and the fundamental principle of accountability.

## Overall Results and Common Deficiencies

While all 30 audited merchants had published some form of privacy policy or information on data processing, the inspection revealed that **none of the privacy policies were found to be fully compliant with all the requirements of GDPR Article 13**. In fact, at least some deficiencies were identified in every single document reviewed. #enforcement_pattern 

### Policy Accessibility and Structure

- **Discoverability:** Although **23 out of 30** privacy policies were easily located on the main page of the website with clear titles (e.g., "Privacy Policy"), others were less accessible, hidden under titles like "Store Rules" or "Warranty and Withdrawal Rights," or were only available via a cookie banner or at the point of payment. The Inspectorate emphasized the importance of privacy policies being readily discoverable and accessible **before any personal data processing commences**.
- **Logical Structure:** Many of the reviewed privacy policies lacked a clear, logical structure, which made them **unclear and challenging for data subjects to comprehend**.
- **Standardized Content:** A recurring issue was the use of **standardized privacy policies that were not tailored to the specific commercial activities of the business**, often containing irrelevant or misleading information. These policies frequently appeared to be direct translations or copies from other websites or competitors, suggesting a potential lack of understanding of GDPR requirements or the internal data processing operations within the company.

### Specific Deficiencies in Content

The most significant and frequently observed deficiencies were directly related to the explanation of **data subject rights, the specification of data storage terms, and the application of legal bases for data processing**, alongside issues concerning the clear identification of data recipients.

- **Controller Identification (GDPR Art. 13(1)(a)):** All controllers did include contact information, although its accessibility was sometimes hindered by the policy's placement. While **24 out of 30** clearly stated the full controller's name, others used vague terms like "we," referred to the "website owner," or even identified the website itself as the controller. These were generally considered minor issues.
- **Data Protection Officer (DPO) Information (GDPR Art. 13(1)(b)):** None of the inspected companies met the criteria that would mandate the appointment of a DPO. However, three companies still opted to provide DPO contact information.
- **Processing Purposes (GDPR Art. 13(1)(c)):** Generally, the stated purposes were clear, but three controllers provided purposes that were imprecise, ambiguous, or irrelevant (e.g., a purpose inconsistent with the services offered, only mentioning "commercial notifications," or a vague statement like "correctly process the order"). #transparency 
- **Legal Basis for Processing (GDPR Art. 13(1)(c)):** This area was identified as one of the **most critical with substantial deficiencies**. Only **12 out of 30** controllers provided adequate information regarding the legal basis. Another 13 showed partial compliance (e.g., listing all possible legal bases without specific relevance, or providing generic information without citing specific laws or justifying legitimate interests as required by Article 13(1)(d)). **Five controllers failed to provide any information on the legal basis whatsoever**. There was also a common misunderstanding where "consent" was incorrectly cited as a legal basis in situations where data submission was mandatory or essential for the provision of a service (e.g., data transfer to courier services).
- **Data Recipients or Categories (GDPR Art. 13(1)(e)):** **15 out of 30** controllers only provided generalized information about data recipients (e.g., "service providers," "subcontractors," "strategic partners"), even in cases where specific names of consistent partners (such as outsourced service providers or payment processors) could have been listed. 14 policies were compliant in this regard, with one showing a specific exception. No instances of data transfer to third countries were observed during the check. #transparency #vendor_management  #change 
- **Data Storage Duration (GDPR Art. 13(2)(a)):** While **20 out of 30** controllers clearly indicated specific data storage periods, **six failed to provide this information entirely**. Four partially met the requirement by providing general terms like "according to processing purpose" or "no longer than legal requirements". The Inspectorate stressed the importance of clearly specifying storage periods, especially when mandated by law or clearly defined by the controller. #retention #change 
- **Data Subject Rights and Exercise (GDPR Art. 13(2)):** This was another **critical area of non-compliance**. Only **13 out of 30** controllers provided sufficient information. Another 13 showed partial compliance (e.g., incomplete lists of rights, incorrect Inspectorate contact details, or no mention of the right to lodge a complaint). There was a noticeable lack of understanding regarding the right to withdraw consent and the practical exercise of other rights, sometimes imposing inconvenient requirements like in-person identity verification. **Four controllers completely failed to describe data subject rights**. #rights 
- **Automated Decision-Making/Profiling (GDPR Art. 13(2)(f)) and Mandatory Data Provision (GDPR Art. 13(2)(e)):** No instances of automated decision-making or profiling were identified among the audited companies. The requirement for mandatory data provision was assessed in conjunction with the analysis of the legal basis for processing.

## Inspectorate's Feedback and Recommendations

The Inspectorate focused on providing **clear and straightforward guidance** to controllers, deliberately avoiding complex terminology, particularly to assist new businesses or those less familiar with data protection. They recommended a tabular format for clearly presenting data processing purposes and their corresponding legal bases. Despite no policy being fully compliant, even minor issues prompted the issuance of corrective letters. It's notable that **six controllers did not respond** to multiple communications, which led to further procedural actions for failure to provide information to the authority.

The Inspectorate clarified a crucial point: **data subject consent is not required for the content of a privacy policy itself**, as the policy's function is to inform about existing data processing, not to obtain consent for the processing activity. The Inspectorate also highlighted deficiencies observed in cookie banners and their related policies, although these were not a direct part of the preventive check's primary scope.

# Conclusions and Future Implications

The preventive check was considered successful, yielding valuable insights that will be utilized for future inspections and for more effectively explaining data protection issues to the public. The findings strongly suggest that controllers' understanding of the necessity and content of privacy policies still requires active promotion through both individual and public awareness efforts. Consequently, the Inspectorate intends to devote more attention to privacy policies, potentially incorporating such checks into other, unrelated inspections. The identified deficiencies underscore that controllers may not fully grasp the principle of accountability or possess a deep understanding of their own internal data processing operations.
