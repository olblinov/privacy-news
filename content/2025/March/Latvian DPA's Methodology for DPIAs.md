# What Triggers a DPIA (NIDA)

A Data Protection Impact Assessment (NIDA) is required for data processing activities that may pose a **high risk** to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. The Data State Inspectorate (Inspekcija) has compiled a list of such processing activities that **mandatorily require** a NIDA, based on Article 35(4) of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and guidelines from the Article 29 Working Party (now EDPB). This list is not exhaustive and can be supplemented over time.

A NIDA is generally necessary if the processing meets at least two criteria established by the EDPB (European Data Protection Board). However, in some cases, a NIDA might still be required even if only one criterion is met, meaning organizations must assess each processing case individually.

The Inspectorate's list of processing activities that require a NIDA includes, but is not limited to:

- **Assessment or evaluation, including profiling and prediction**, particularly concerning performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences or interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movement of the data subject. Examples include financial institutions assessing client data using credit inquiry databases or AML/CTF databases, biotechnology companies offering genetic tests to predict health risks, or companies creating behavioral or marketing profiles based on website usage.
- **Automated decision-making** with legal or similarly significant effects, such as decisions that could exclude or discriminate against individuals, unless the processing causes no significant harm or impact.
- **Systematic monitoring** of data subjects, including data obtained from the web or public areas, especially when data subjects might be unaware of data collection or unable to avoid it. Examples include video surveillance in public areas.
- Processing of **sensitive data or highly personal data**, as defined in GDPR Article 9 (e.g., political views) or data related to criminal convictions and offenses (GDPR Article 10). Examples include medical institutions managing patient health data or private investigators maintaining records of offenders.
- **Large-scale data processing**, where factors like the number of data subjects, volume and range of data items, duration or permanence of processing, and territorial scope are considered.
- **Matching or combining data sets** from two or more processing operations carried out for different purposes and/or by different data processors, exceeding data subjects' reasonable expectations.
- Processing data concerning **particularly vulnerable data subjects**. This includes children, employees, mentally ill individuals, asylum seekers, the elderly, patients, or any situation where there is an imbalance in the relationship between the data subject and the controller.
- Use of **innovative new technologies or solutions**, or their application in innovative ways. This includes "Internet of Things" applications, or combining fingerprint and facial recognition for access control.
- Processing that **"prevents data subjects from exercising rights or using a service or contract"** (GDPR Recital 22 and 91). An example is a bank using a credit inquiry database to decide on loan issuance.
- Processing related to **criminal convictions, offenses, or other security measures**.
- Processing personal data for **scientific or historical purposes without data subject consent**, combined with at least one of the other criteria.
- Cases where providing information to the data subject as per GDPR Article 19 is **impossible**.
- Processing **genetic data** to identify a natural person, combined with at least one criterion.
- **Monitoring employees**.
- **Large-scale tracking of data subjects**, including lifestyle apps or logistics companies.
- Use of **data subject location data**, combined with at least one criterion.
- Processing data related to **information society services offered directly to a child**.
- **Automatic processing of personal data on a large scale** and data processing based on **profiling**.
- Data processing for the purpose of **combining data from different sources** to ensure reconciliation, comparison, and reuse.
- Processing **biometric data** to identify a natural person, combined with at least one criterion.

# Context of the Risk (Circumstances Factored in)

Risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons is understood as the **combination of the probability of harm and its impact**. When assessing risk, various circumstances and factors are considered:

- **Risk Factors:** These can stem from the processing itself, such as its purpose, the specific processing activity, or the technologies used.
- **Characterizing Features of Risk:** These include likelihood (possibility of an event), opportunity (favorable conditions for objectives), probability (measure of likelihood), driver of risk (factors influencing risk), and threat (source of potential harm).
- **Risk Sources:** These can be positive or negative events, decisions, actions, processes, or situations with uncertain outcomes. Any type of uncertainty can be a risk source. Risk sources can be dynamic, related to human behavior, organizational structures, or societal influences, making predicting their occurrence difficult.
- **Causes and Consequences:** An event can have multiple causes and lead to various consequences, which may not be immediately visible or measurable but can accumulate over time.
- **Impact Factors related to processing:**
    - **Purpose of processing:** Derived from the processing objectives.
    - **Types of data used:** Relates to the personal data collected, processed, or transferred throughout the data lifecycle. Data sensitivity (ordinary vs. special categories), data volume, and potential harm from malicious use are considered.
    - **Volume of data processed:** Connected to the number of data subjects, diversity of processed data, duration, and frequency of processing.
    - **Categories of data subjects:** Involves classifications like employees, minors, the elderly, vulnerable individuals, or others. Data subjects' expectations and attitudes towards processing are also considered.
    - **Technical aspects of processing:** Arise from the type of processing and the specific technical means used. This includes whether new or untested technologies are used, security measures like encryption and access control, and the potential for misuse of technology (e.g., facial recognition for surveillance).
    - **Data acquisition:** How data is collected or generated, whether directly from the individual, inferred, or from third parties. Reliability of data sources and data subject awareness are also important.
    - **Data disclosure:** The context in which personal data is disclosed to third parties.
    - **Data protection breach:** The likelihood of a personal data breach and its potential consequences.
    - **Consequences incompatible with initial purpose:** If unforeseen consequences arise that are incompatible with the original processing purpose.
- **Controller/Processor's field of activity:** The size and scale of the organization, its compliance history (e.g., past penalties or breaches), and the industry sector (e.g., healthcare, finance, retail).
- **Data transfer to third countries or international organizations:** Considerations include whether the destination country or organization provides adequate data protection, the transfer mechanisms used (e.g., standard contractual clauses), and jurisdictional risks (e.g., conflicting laws, government surveillance).
- **Human and organizational factors:** These play a role in risk assessment.

# Methodology and Gradations of Likelihood and Impact

There is no single universal method for conducting a NIDA, and the chosen approach depends on the complexity of the data processing situation and the technological solutions used. The Inspectorate's methodology, based on ISO IEC 31010:2019 and AEPD guidelines, is illustrative and adaptable to an organization's specific needs.

Risk assessment can be **qualitative, quantitative, or semi-quantitative**. Qualitative methods rely on detailed descriptions and expert opinions, quantitative methods use mathematical and statistical means, and semi-quantitative methods combine both.

## Impact Level Gradations (Kvantitatīvi: 1-4)

The impact refers to the potential consequences of harm to data subjects, which can be physical, material, or non-material.

- **Ļoti nozīmīgs (Very significant) - 4:** Impacts are **irreversible** and affect the exercise of rights and freedoms. This includes special categories of data or criminal offenses leading to irreversible harm, significant social harm (e.g., discrimination), irreversible impact on vulnerable data subjects (especially children), or significant and irreversible moral or material losses.
- **Nozīmīgs (Significant) - 3:** Similar to very significant cases, but the **impact is reversible**. This includes loss of control over personal data on a large scale, identity theft, significant financial loss, loss of confidentiality for secret data, or social harm to data subjects or groups.
- **Maznozīmīgs (Minor) - 2:** **Very limited loss of control** over some personal data for specific data subjects, excluding special category data.
- **Neietekmē (No impact) - 1:** All consequences are **preventable** in cases mentioned under "minor" impact.

## Likelihood Level Gradations (Kvantitatīvi: 1-4)

Likelihood refers to the probability of a specific event or consequence occurring.

- **Ļoti augsts (Very high) - 4:** The risk factor has **already materialized multiple times** in the last year, or there is evidence of significant deficiencies in organizational procedures or technical means related to this risk.
- **Augsts (High) - 3:** There is evidence that such a risk has materialized in another organization in the last year, or studies indicate a high probability, or audits/studies confirm potential deficiencies, or elements related to risk factors are implemented with new technologies or procedures without adhering to quality standards.
- **Zems (Low) - 2:** Evidence of the risk materializing exists over a longer period, e.g., **more than 5 years**.
- **Maz ticams (Unlikely) - 1:** Evidence suggests the risk will **not materialize**, or it has not been detected in the last 5 years.

# Risk Matrix, Acceptable, and Reducible Risk Levels

## Risk Matrix

The overall risk level is determined by combining the impact and likelihood, often using a matrix.

| Likelihood (Varbūtība) | Nenozīmīga (1) | Maznozīmīga (2) | Nozīmīga (3) | Ļoti nozīmīga (4) |
| :--------------------- | :------------- | :-------------- | :----------- | :---------------- |
| **Ļoti augsta (4)**    | Medium         | High            | Very high    | Very high         |
| **Augsta (3)**         | Low            | Medium          | High         | Very high         |
| **Zema (2)**           | Low            | Medium          | Medium       | High              |
| **Maz ticama (1)**     | Low            | Low             | Low          | Medium            |

_This table shows the qualitative risk level based on the intersection of likelihood and impact._

For a quantitative interpretation, the risk level is calculated by **multiplying the quantitative likelihood (1-4) by the quantitative impact (1-4)**.

- **Zems risks (Low risk):** If the result is **less than 3**.
- **Vidējs risks (Medium risk):** If the result is **between 4 and 7**.
- **Augsts risks (High risk):** If the result is **between 8 and 11**.
- **Ļoti augsts risks (Very high risk):** If the result is **equal to or greater than 12**.

## Acceptable and Reducible Risk Levels

Organizations are expected to identify, assess, and mitigate possible risks to the rights and freedoms of natural persons arising from data processing. The "risk appetite" is the residual risk remaining after protection measures are implemented, with the goal of reducing it to an acceptable level.

- **Acceptable Risk:** A **low and/or medium residual risk level** that requires proportionate management efforts throughout the data processing lifecycle is considered acceptable. In such cases, organizations do not need to consult with the Data State Inspectorate.
- **Risks to be Reduced:** If, after implementing risk mitigation measures, the **residual risk level is higher than the medium value**, further measures must be taken to manage the identified risks. If the **remaining risks are still high**, organizations are **required to request prior consultation with the Data State Inspectorate** before commencing processing. The Inspectorate will then assess the situation and may either deem the risks acceptable, suggest additional mitigation measures, or conclude that the planned processing poses a disproportionate threat to data subjects' rights and freedoms. There is no "zero risk" in data processing; organizations must find a compromise between the achieved residual risk level and the feasibility of processing.
