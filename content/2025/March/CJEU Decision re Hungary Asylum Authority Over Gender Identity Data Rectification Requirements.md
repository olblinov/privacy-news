# Summary

The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) ruled that Hungarian authorities must rectify personal data relating to an individual's **gender identity** in public registers if those data are inaccurate. This judgment stemmed from a case involving VP, an Iranian refugee in Hungary, whose request to change their registered gender from female to male was rejected by the asylum authority for lack of proof of gender reassignment surgery. The CJEU clarified that while a data subject can be required to provide relevant and sufficient evidence to prove data inaccuracy, an administrative practice making rectification conditional upon **gender reassignment surgery** is impermissible. Such a requirement undermines fundamental rights, including the right to integrity and privacy, and is neither necessary nor proportionate, as other evidence like medical certificates can be sufficient.

# Details

## Background

VP, an Iranian national, obtained refugee status in Hungary in 2014, relying on their **transgender identity** (born female, identifying as male) and submitting supporting medical certificates from psychiatry and gynaecology specialists. Despite this, VP was registered as female in the asylum register maintained by the Országos Idegenrendészeti Főigazgatóság (National Directorate-General for Immigration Policing, Hungary), referred to as 'the asylum authority'.

In 2022, VP requested the asylum authority rectify the gender entry in the register to male and amend their forename, basing the request on **Article 16 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** and providing the aforementioned medical certificates. The asylum authority rejected VP's request on the grounds that VP had not proved they had undergone gender reassignment surgery and that the certificates merely established their transgender identity.

VP challenged this rejection before the Fővárosi Törvényszék (Budapest High Court, Hungary), arguing that requiring gender reassignment surgery for recognition of gender identity change contradicts the case-law of the European Court of Human Rights and **Articles 3 and 7 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (Charter)**. VP also highlighted that several other EU Member States recognize changes in gender identity based on declarations by the individuals concerned.

The referring Hungarian court noted that while Hungarian law contains a general provision for rectifying incorrect entries, it lacks specific procedures or conditions for recognizing a change of gender identity or forename in this context. It cited past judgments from the Hungarian Constitutional Court and the European Court of Human Rights (e.g., _Rana v. Hungary_), which found Hungary's failure to provide such a procedure for legally resident non-nationals, including refugees, unconstitutional and a violation of the European Convention for the Protection of Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms (ECHR). This legal vacuum has persisted, and since 2020, even Hungarian nationals have lost the possibility of legal recognition for gender identity changes. Due to this national legal void, VP brought the action based on GDPR Article 16.

## Legal Analysis

The core of the case involved the interpretation of **Article 16 of the GDPR**, which grants data subjects the right to rectification of inaccurate personal data, read in conjunction with **Article 5(1)(d) of the GDPR**, which enshrines the principle of accuracy. The Court emphasized that the protection of personal data is a **fundamental right** enshrined in Article 8(1) of the Charter and Article 16(1) TFEU, and Article 16 of the GDPR specifically expresses the right to have data rectified under Article 8(2) of the Charter.

The Court affirmed that personal data must be accurate and kept up to date, and any inaccurate data must be rectified without delay. The assessment of data accuracy must be made in light of the purpose for which the data was collected. The Court determined that information relating to VP's gender identity constitutes "personal data," and its collection and registration by the asylum authority in a public register is "processing" within the GDPR's material scope.

The Court advised the referring court to verify the accuracy of the data based on its collection purpose. If the purpose of collecting gender data in the asylum register (as per Hungarian Law No LXXX of 2007 on the right to asylum) is for **personal identification**, then the data should refer to the individual's **lived gender identity**, not their gender assigned at birth. Given that Hungary had accepted VP as a transgender person during the refugee status granting procedure, the registration of VP as female in the asylum register appeared to be incorrect from the outset.

The Court clarified that Member States cannot use national specific provisions (under Article 6(2) and (3) GDPR) to limit the right to rectification, as these provisions are intended to specify, not derogate from, GDPR rules. The right to rectification can only be restricted under the strict conditions of **Article 23 of the GDPR**, which requires a **legislative measure** that respects the essence of fundamental rights and is necessary and proportionate to safeguard public interests, such as the reliability of public registers. The Court noted that the asylum authority's refusal was not based on a statutory restriction under Article 23 GDPR but on VP's alleged failure to provide sufficient proof.

Furthermore, the Court stated that a Member State cannot rely on the absence of a national procedure for legal recognition of transgender identity to limit the right to rectification. Although EU law respects Member States' competence in civil status matters, they must exercise this competence in compliance with EU law, including the GDPR and the Charter.

Regarding the evidence required for rectification, Article 16 GDPR does not specify which evidence is necessary. #rights While a data subject can be required to provide **relevant and sufficient evidence** that can reasonably be expected to establish data inaccuracy, an **administrative practice** that makes the exercise of this right conditional on proof of gender reassignment surgery is unacceptable. This is because it is not a **legislative measure** as required by Article 23 GDPR.

Crucially, such a requirement **undermines the essence of fundamental rights**, specifically the **right to the integrity of the person and the right to respect for private life**, as enshrined in Articles 3 and 7 of the Charter, which correspond to Article 8 ECHR. The Court cited the European Court of Human Rights' consistent case-law, which holds that the recognition of a transgender person's gender identity cannot be conditional on surgical treatment they do not desire. Lastly, requiring proof of gender reassignment surgery is deemed **neither necessary nor proportionate** to ensure the reliability and consistency of a public register, as medical certificates, including psychiatric diagnoses, can constitute relevant and sufficient evidence. #rights 

## Decision

The Court ruled:

- **Article 16 of the GDPR requires a national authority responsible for keeping a public register to rectify personal data relating to a natural person's gender identity where those data are inaccurate** within the meaning of Article 5(1)(d) of the GDPR.
- For the purpose of exercising the right to rectification, the data subject may be required to **provide relevant and sufficient evidence** to establish the inaccuracy of the data.
- However, a Member State **may not, under any circumstances, by way of an administrative practice, make the exercise of this right conditional upon the production of evidence of gender reassignment surgery**. Such a requirement undermines the essence of fundamental rights, including the right to integrity and respect for private life (Articles 3 and 7 of the Charter), and is neither necessary nor proportionate. Medical certificates, including psychiatric diagnoses, can constitute sufficient evidence.
