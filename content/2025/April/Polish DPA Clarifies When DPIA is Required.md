# When is a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) Required?

## General Obligation

Every administrator must conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) when their planned processing operations are **highly likely to cause a high risk to the rights or freedoms of natural persons**. This assessment is not required for every processing operation but is mandatory when the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing—especially with new technologies—pose such a high risk. Administrators should analyze this obligation during the design phase of a data processing operation to ensure timely action. It is important to remember that an administrator must always perform an initial assessment to determine if planned operations are likely to pose a high risk to individuals' rights or freedoms before processing personal data.

## Specific Examples from RODO Article 35(3)

RODO Article 35(3) provides specific examples of processing operations that may cause high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons, requiring a DPIA. These include, in particular:

- **Systematic and comprehensive evaluation of personal aspects** relating to natural persons, based on automated processing, including profiling, which forms the basis for decisions that produce legal effects concerning the individual or similarly significantly affect them.
- **Large-scale processing of special categories of personal data** (as per Article 9(1)) or personal data relating to criminal convictions and offenses (as per Article 10).
- **Systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas on a large scale**.

# Guidance and Criteria for Conducting a DPIA

## UODO Communication and Its 12 Criteria

The examples provided in RODO are not an exhaustive list; other data processing operations may also pose a high risk. More specific guidance is found in the lists of processing operations requiring a DPIA, which supervisory authorities establish and publish in accordance with RODO Article 35(4). The Communication of the President of the Personal Data Protection Office (UODO) from June 17, 2019, includes such a list as an annex.

This list supplements and concretizes the guidelines of the Article 29 Working Party (WP 248), titled "Guidelines on Data Protection Impact Assessment and determining whether processing 'is likely to result in a high risk' for the purposes of Regulation 2016/679," which were approved by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) on May 25, 2018. These guidelines introduce **9 criteria** that should be considered when assessing whether a DPIA is required, generally assuming that **meeting two of these criteria necessitates a DPIA**.

The UODO Communication annex contains **12 criteria/types of processing operations**, along with examples of operations where a high risk of infringing rights or freedoms may occur, and examples of potential areas covered by these operations. These criteria/operations include:

- **Evaluation or scoring**, including profiling and prediction (behavioral analysis), for purposes that cause negative legal, physical, financial, or other disadvantages for natural persons.
- **Automated decision-making** producing legal, financial, or similarly significant effects.
- **Systematic large-scale monitoring of publicly accessible areas** using recognition features or object properties in the monitored space. This category does not include video surveillance systems where images are recorded and used only for incident analysis.
- **Processing of special categories of personal data** and data concerning criminal convictions and offenses (sensitive data according to WP 29 opinion).
- **Processing of biometric data** solely for the purpose of identifying a natural person or for access control.
- **Processing of genetic data**.
- **Large-scale data processing**, where "large scale" refers to the number of data subjects, the scope of processing, the data retention period, and the geographical scope of processing. #dpia 
- **Performing comparisons, evaluations, or inferences based on data analysis obtained from various sources**.
- **Processing data concerning individuals whose evaluation and services provided to them depend on entities or persons with supervisory or evaluative powers**.
- **Innovative use or application of technological or organizational solutions**.
- **When processing itself prevents data subjects from exercising a right or using a service or contract**.
- **Processing of location data**.

### How to Use the UODO List

Generally, processing that meets **at least two of the listed criteria/types of operations will require a DPIA**. The more criteria met, the higher the probability of high risk to data subjects' rights or freedoms, and thus the greater the necessity for a DPIA. However, an administrator may determine that processing meeting only one criterion requires a DPIA due to its nature, scope, context, and purposes.

### Examples from the UODO List

- Implementing a system to monitor employee working hours and information flow in their tools (email, internet) requires a DPIA due to meeting criteria 3 (systematic monitoring) and 9 (data concerning persons dependent on supervisory entities) from the list.
- The EDPB has clarified that, given the typical goals of video monitoring (protection of persons and property, crime detection and prevention, evidence collection, and biometric identification of suspects), it is reasonable to assume that **in many cases involving video monitoring, a DPIA will be necessary**.
- The EDPB has also stated that it is very probable that **Virtual Voice Assistant (VVA) services fall into categories and conditions requiring a DPIA**. This includes considerations if the device monitors individuals or involves large-scale systematic monitoring, new technology, or processing of sensitive data or data of vulnerable persons.

## Other Triggers for a DPIA

The obligation to conduct a DPIA may also directly result from separate legal provisions. For instance, Article 8 of Directive 2024/2831 states that processing personal data by digital labor platforms using automated monitoring or decision-making systems is a type of processing highly likely to cause a high risk to natural persons' rights and freedoms under RODO Article 35(1).

# Doubts and Benefits of Conducting a DPIA

## When in Doubt, Conduct a DPIA

The EDPB recommends that if there is any doubt about whether a DPIA is mandatory, it should be conducted.

## Benefits of a DPIA

A DPIA is an instrument that helps administrators comply with personal data protection regulations. Implementing an effective DPIA procedure not only **minimizes risks** but also **builds trust** among data subjects, for example, by helping assess measures to facilitate their rights or ensure transparency in data processing. Therefore, it can be beneficial for an administrator to conduct a DPIA even when it is not mandatory (voluntary DPIA).

## Risk Assessment Obligation

Regardless of whether a DPIA is mandatory, the administrator always has an obligation to **properly assess and manage the risks associated with their personal data processing**.

# Responsibility for Conducting a DPIA

## The Administrator's Obligation

The **obligation to conduct a DPIA rests with the administrator**. While a DPIA can be useful for assessing the impact of a technological product (like hardware or software) that administrators may use to process personal data, this does not relieve the administrator implementing the product of their DPIA obligation. However, information obtained from the supplier can facilitate this task. The EDPB emphasized that suppliers or processors should share useful information without revealing secrets or creating security threats by exposing weaknesses.

## Role of Data Protection Officer (DPO) and Providers

The administrator **cannot delegate this obligation to a Data Protection Officer (DPO)**, but they should consult with the DPO in this regard.

# Consultation with Data Subjects

RODO Article 35(9) requires administrators, where appropriate, to **seek the opinions of data subjects or their representatives** regarding the intended processing, without prejudice to the protection of commercial or public interests or the security of processing operations. This obligation can be particularly important in the context of creating and implementing Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems, as the nature of associated threats to individuals' rights and freedoms may require situating planned data processing operations within a broader social context.

# Artificial Intelligence (AI) Systems and DPIA

## AI Systems Definition and Data Processing

With the dynamic development of technologies such as AI and their changing applications, a DPIA becomes a crucial element of the personal data protection system. The AI Act defines an AI system as a **"machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy after its deployment and that can exhibit adaptiveness after its deployment, and that—for explicit or implicit objectives—infers how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments"** (Art. 3, point 1). AI models are essential components of AI systems but do not constitute AI systems by themselves, as they require additional elements like a user interface.

Personal data processing can occur at various stages of the AI system lifecycle (creation and deployment). The EDPB has clarified that AI models trained on personal data cannot always be considered anonymous. Even if a model is not intentionally designed to generate information about identified or identifiable individuals from training data, but such information can be obtained through reasonably likely means, the model is not anonymous.

## DPIA Across the AI System Lifecycle

At different stages of creating or implementing an AI system, a DPIA may be necessary due to diverse processing purposes and means, leading to different risks for data subjects. Administrators should assess whether planned data processing operations meet the criteria listed in RODO Article 35(3) or the UODO Communication annex.

### Creating AI Systems (Training Data)

When creating AI systems, particularly when the training data set contains personal data, the following criteria from the UODO list are especially relevant for determining if a DPIA is needed:

- Processing of **special categories of personal data**, including "behavioral data" analysis, and data concerning criminal convictions and offenses (4).
- Processing of **biometric data** solely for identification or access control (5).
- Processing of **genetic data** (6).
- **Large-scale data processing** (7).
- **Performing comparisons, evaluations, or inferences based on data analysis obtained from various sources** (8).
- **Innovative use or application of technological or organizational solutions** (10). It is also necessary to conduct a DPIA if the creation of a training data set could significantly affect natural persons, especially if data processing could lead to discrimination, data security breaches, or improper use.

### Applying AI Systems

Entities applying AI systems should particularly consider the following criteria for a DPIA:

- **Evaluation or scoring**, including profiling and prediction (behavioral analysis) for purposes that cause negative legal, physical, financial, or other disadvantages for natural persons (1).
- **Automated decision-making** producing legal, financial, or similarly significant effects (2).
- **Systematic large-scale monitoring of publicly accessible areas** (3).
- **Large-scale data processing** (7).
- **Performing comparisons, evaluations, or inferences based on data analysis obtained from various sources** (8).
- **When processing itself prevents data subjects from exercising a right or using a service or contract** (9).
- **Innovative use or application of technological or organizational solutions** (10).

**Examples of AI applications requiring a DPIA:** #ai 

- **Creditworthiness assessment using AI algorithms** that are subject to secrecy obligations will require a DPIA due to meeting criteria 1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 11 from the list.
- **Customer profiling systems for transport services and dynamic pricing based on profiles** will require a DPIA due to meeting criteria 1, 2, 10, 11, 12 from the list.
- **Monitoring systems for traffic management** that allow detailed surveillance of drivers and their behavior, particularly systems enabling automatic vehicle identification, will require a DPIA due to meeting criteria 3 and 10 from the list.
- A **clinical hospital implementing an AI tool to support medical imaging diagnostics** that recognizes pathological changes and creates examination descriptions will require a DPIA due to meeting criteria 4, 7, 10 from the list.

## High-Risk AI Systems

Generally, data processing for the creation and implementation of **high-risk AI systems** as defined by the AI Act can be highly likely to cause a high risk to the rights or freedoms of natural persons, and thus requires a DPIA.

## Utilizing Provider Information for High-Risk AI

An entity applying a high-risk AI system will be able to use information provided by the system's supplier in accordance with Article 13 of the AI Act when conducting a DPIA.

# Additional Resources and Ongoing Monitoring

Administrators should regularly monitor new guidelines and interpretations from supervisory authorities, including the EDPB, to adapt their actions to current regulations. The EDPB is working to ensure that RODO and AI Act provisions concerning DPIA obligations are applied consistently.
