# I. Right to the Protection of Personal Data Recognised by the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union

## 1. Compliance of secondary EU law with the right to the protection of personal data

The CJEU has consistently scrutinized EU secondary legislation against fundamental data protection rights.

- **Public Funds Transparency vs. Data Protection (Volker und Markus Schecke and Eifert, 2010):** The Court ruled on the publication of personal data of agricultural fund beneficiaries. It found that publishing names and aid amounts on a freely accessible website constituted an **interference** with beneficiaries' right to private life and data protection. Such interference must be provided by law, respect the essence of rights, and be **necessary and proportionate**, strictly limited to objectives of general interest. The Court **declared invalid** provisions that required publication of data for _any_ beneficiary without distinction based on criteria like aid period, frequency, or amount, as they failed to strike a proper balance.
- **Data Retention for Crime Fighting (Digital Rights Ireland and Seitlinger and Others, 2014):** The Court addressed the validity of Directive 2006/24/EC, which mandated the retention of electronic communications data. It found this directive to impose a **particularly serious interference** with the fundamental rights to private life and personal data protection (Articles 7 and 8 of the Charter). While fighting organized crime is a legitimate general interest objective, the directive was **declared invalid** because it exceeded proportionality limits. Its flaws included:
    - **General and indiscriminate** coverage of all persons, communication means, and traffic data without differentiation.
    - Failure to specify objective criteria for national authorities' access and use of data.
    - Lack of clear substantive and procedural conditions for access/use.
    - Retention period (at least six months) without distinction based on data categories or usefulness.
    - Insufficient safeguards against abuse, unlawful access, and the absence of a requirement to retain data within the EU.
    - Failure to ensure effective control by an independent authority as required by the Charter.
- **Passenger Name Record (PNR) Data (Ligue des droits humains, 2022):** The Court confirmed the validity of the PNR Directive for combating terrorist offenses and serious crime, provided it is interpreted restrictively and consistently with the Charter. It acknowledged that the directive involves **undeniably serious interferences** due to its continuous, untargeted, and systematic surveillance regime. Justification requires proportionality to the seriousness of interference. The Court provided strict interpretations:
    - PNR system must cover **clearly identifiable and circumscribed information** as per Annex I, with specific limitations (e.g., payment information limited to ticket billing, general remarks to minors).
    - Application limited to terrorist offenses and **serious crime with an objective (even indirect) link to air travel**, excluding ordinary crime.
    - Extension to **intra-EU flights must be strictly necessary** and subject to effective judicial or independent administrative review. Such an extension to all intra-EU flights is justified only by a genuine, present, or foreseeable terrorist threat, for a strictly necessary period. Otherwise, it must be limited to specific routes/airports with justification, reviewed regularly.
    - Automated advance assessment by Passenger Information Units (PIUs) can only compare data against "useful" non-discriminatory databases related to the fight against terrorist offenses and serious crime. It **cannot use self-learning AI (machine learning)** to modify assessment criteria. Criteria must be targeted, proportionate, specific, and non-discriminatory.
    - **Non-automated verification** of positive results is crucial due to inherent margin of error and "false positives". Member States must provide clear rules for PIU agents to ensure non-discrimination. Data subjects must be able to understand how criteria/programs work to exercise judicial redress, with courts able to examine all grounds/evidence.
    - Subsequent disclosure/assessment of PNR data (after arrival/departure) is allowed **only based on new circumstances and objective evidence** of involvement in serious crime or effective contribution to combating it. This disclosure generally requires prior court or independent administrative authority review, unless in duly justified urgent cases.
- **Public Access to Beneficial Ownership Information (Luxembourg Business Registers, 2022):** The Court addressed a directive requiring Member States to make beneficial ownership information accessible to the general public to combat money laundering and terrorist financing. The Court **declared Directive 2018/843 invalid** insofar as it mandated public access, finding it a **serious interference** with fundamental rights to private life and data protection (Articles 7 and 8 Charter). The seriousness stems from the ability to profile individuals' wealth and investments, the potentially unlimited access (especially online), and the exacerbated difficulty for data subjects to defend against abuse once data is publicly available. While combating money laundering is a legitimate general interest, the interference was deemed **not strictly necessary**. Difficulties in implementing a "legitimate interest" criterion did not justify generalized public access. The objective of greater civil society scrutiny also did not justify general access, as relevant entities (press, NGOs, financial institutions, authorities) already have or should have legitimate access. The directive's use of "at least" for data types lacked clarity and precision, making the rules disproportionate. The Court concluded that the increased interference of general public access was not offset by comparable benefits in combating financial crime, which is primarily the responsibility of public authorities and obliged entities.

## 2. Respect for the right to the protection of personal data in the implementation of EU law

The CJEU ensures that national implementations of EU law respect fundamental data protection rights.

- **General Data Retention by Member States (Tele2 Sverige, 2016):** Following its "Digital Rights Ireland" judgment, the Court again ruled against national legislation (in Sweden and the UK) imposing a **general and indiscriminate retention of all traffic and location data** for all electronic communication users for crime fighting purposes. The Court found such legislation to be **precluded** by Directive 2002/58/EC (ePrivacy Directive) read in conjunction with the Charter, as it **exceeded the limits of what is strictly necessary** and could not be justified in a democratic society. Access by national authorities to retained data is precluded if not restricted solely to fighting **serious crime**, not subject to **prior judicial or independent administrative review**, or if data retention is not confined within the EU. However, **targeted retention** is permissible as a preventive measure for serious crime, provided it is strictly necessary, based on clear and precise rules, and meets objective criteria to circumscribe the affected public.

# II. Processing of Personal Data Within the Meaning of the Relevant General Rules

## 1. Scope of the general rules

The CJEU defines what constitutes "processing of personal data" and the territorial scope for data protection regulations.

- **Public Security Exclusion (Parliament v Council, 2006):** The Court clarified that processing of personal data for **public security and criminal law enforcement purposes is excluded from the scope of Directive 95/46**. Even if data (like PNR) is initially collected for commercial activities (e.g., airline ticket sales), its subsequent processing for public security falls outside the directive's scope if established by public authorities. Consequently, Commission and Council decisions based on this directive for PNR data transfers to the US were **annulled**.
- **Territorial Scope of Search Engines (Google Spain and Google, 2014):** The Court ruled that processing of personal data occurs "in the context of the activities of an establishment of the controller on the territory of a Member State" even if the search engine operator is based in a third country, provided it has a branch or subsidiary in a Member State that promotes and sells advertising space directed at that Member State's inhabitants, as these activities are inextricably linked.
- **Personal/Household Activity Exception (Ryneš, 2014):** A surveillance camera installed on a private home that also monitors a **public space** does **not** fall under the "purely personal or household activities" exception of Directive 95/46. This exception must be **narrowly construed** to protect the fundamental right to private life.
- **Parliamentary Committee Activities (Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde, 2024):** The GDPR's exclusion for processing data "in the course of an activity which is intended to safeguard national security or which can be classified in the same category" (Article 2(2)(a) GDPR) refers to categories of activities, not persons. Therefore, the **parliamentary nature of an inquiry committee does not automatically exclude its activities from GDPR scope**. Political scrutiny of the executive is generally not an activity intended to safeguard national security. While Member States can define their security interests, this does not render EU law inapplicable. Restrictions on GDPR obligations and rights for national security must be provided by legislative measure, respect the essence of fundamental rights, and be necessary and proportionate.

## 2. Concept of ‘personal data’

The CJEU has broadened the interpretation of "personal data."

- **Dynamic IP Addresses (Breyer, 2016):** A **dynamic IP address** recorded by an online media service provider constitutes "personal data" if the provider has the **legal means to identify the data subject** by combining it with additional data held by the internet service provider. It is not required that all identifying information be held by a single entity.
- **Examination Answers and Comments (Nowak, 2017):** **Written answers submitted by a candidate** in a professional examination and any **examiner's comments** on those answers are **personal data**. This is because the content reflects the candidate's knowledge, competence, intellect, and judgment, and the evaluation affects their rights and interests (e.g., entering a profession). The examining body can identify the candidate. Rights of access and rectification (Article 12(a) and (b) of Directive 95/46) apply to these, but not to the examination questions themselves.

## 3. Concept of ‘processing of personal data’

"Processing" is broadly interpreted to cover various data handling activities.

- **Publishing on the Internet (Lindqvist, 2003):** The act of **referring to persons by name or other identifiers (e.g., phone number, work conditions, hobbies) on an internet page** constitutes "processing of personal data wholly or partly by automated means". Exceptions for public security or purely personal/household activity do not apply to such charitable or religious activities.
- **Search Engine Activities (Google Spain and Google, 2014):** A search engine's activity of finding, indexing, temporarily storing, and displaying internet information is classified as **processing of personal data** if that information contains personal data. A general derogation for already published material would largely undermine the directive's effect.
- **Door-to-Door Preaching (Jehovan todistajat, 2018):** The **collection of personal data by members of a religious community during door-to-door preaching and its subsequent processing** (e.g., notes as memory aids for future visits) does not fall under exceptions for public security or purely personal/domestic activities, and is thus considered processing within the scope of Directive 95/46.
- **Road Traffic Penalty Points (Latvijas Republikas Saeima, 2021):** The disclosure of information relating to penalty points imposed for road traffic offenses by a public body (Road Safety Directorate) is **"processing of personal data relating to criminal convictions and offences"** under Article 10 of the GDPR, which provides enhanced protection. This processing falls within the broad material scope of the GDPR and is not covered by national security or "competent authority" exceptions. The concept of "offence" in GDPR is an autonomous EU law concept, interpreted broadly to include offenses that have a punitive purpose and a certain severity, even if classified as administrative under national law.
- **IT Testing with Real Data (Nacionalinis visuomenės sveikatos centras, 2023):** The **use of personal data for computer testing of a mobile application constitutes processing** under the GDPR. This classification applies regardless of the purpose of the test. However, this does not apply to anonymous or fictitious data, as they are not personal data.

## 4. Concept of ‘personal data filing system’

- **Structured Data in Preaching (Jehovan todistajat, 2018):** A "filing system" covers a set of personal data (names, addresses, other information) collected during door-to-door preaching if it is **structured according to specific criteria that enable easy retrieval for subsequent use**, even without specific data sheets or search methods.

## 5. Concept of ‘personal data controller’

The "controller" is the entity determining the purposes and means of processing.

- **Religious Community (Jehovan todistajat, 2018):** A religious community that organizes, coordinates, and encourages door-to-door preaching is considered a **controller, jointly with its members**, for the personal data processing carried out during these activities. This holds true even if the community doesn't directly access the data or provide written guidelines. The obligation to comply with data protection law does not interfere with the organizational autonomy of religious communities.
- **Facebook Fan Page Administrator (Wirtschaftsakademie Schleswig Holstein, 2018):** The administrator of a Facebook fan page is a **controller, jointly with Facebook Ireland**, because by defining parameters (e.g., target audience, activity promotion), they participate in determining the purposes and means of processing personal data of their fan page visitors.
- **Website with Plug-ins (Fashion ID, 2019):** A website operator who integrates a social media plug-in (like Facebook's "like" button) and transmits user data to the plug-in provider is a **controller for the collection and disclosure by transmission** of that data. However, the operator is **not** a controller for subsequent processing operations carried out by the plug-in provider. Both parties must pursue a **legitimate interest** for the processing to be justified. Consent is only required from the data subject for the processing operations for which the operator determines the purposes and means.
- **Commissioning Application Development (Nacionalinis visuomenės sveikatos centras, 2023):** An entity that commissions an undertaking to develop a mobile application and participates in determining the purposes and means of personal data processing via that app can be considered a **controller**. This is true even if the entity did not perform processing itself, explicitly agree to public release, or acquire the app, unless it expressly objected to the app's public availability and associated data processing.

## 6. Concept of ‘joint controller’

Joint controllership does not necessarily require a formal agreement.

- **No Formal Agreement Needed (Nacionalinis visuomenės sveikatos centras, 2023):** The classification of two entities as "joint controllers" **does not presuppose a prior agreement** between them on purposes/means or joint responsibility. An arrangement defining responsibilities is an _obligation_ once entities are classified as joint controllers, not a precondition for the classification itself. Joint determination can arise from common decisions or **converging decisions** that have a tangible impact on the purposes and means of processing.

## 7. Conditions governing the lawfulness of the processing of personal data

Lawfulness requires specific conditions, often involving necessity and proportionality.

- **Necessity and Discrimination (Huber, 2008):** A central register of foreign nationals for residence legislation support is legitimate but must contain **only necessary data**. Storage and processing for purely **statistical purposes cannot be considered necessary**. Furthermore, systematic processing of personal data solely for non-nationals for crime fighting purposes constitutes **prohibited discrimination** (Article 12(1) EC / Article 18 TFEU) if nationals are not subject to similar processing for the same objective. The concept of "necessity" (Article 7(e) Directive 95/46) has an autonomous EU meaning requiring full reflection of the directive's objectives.
- **Balancing Interests (Breyer, 2016):** Article 7(f) of Directive 95/46, concerning legitimate interests, **precludes national law that categorically excludes certain data processing** (e.g., for general operability of online media after a browsing session) without allowing for a balancing of opposing rights and interests in specific cases. The balancing act is crucial for lawfulness under legitimate interest grounds.
- **Tax Collection Lists (Puškár, 2017):** Processing personal data for tax collection and combating tax fraud, such as drawing up "front men" lists, is **not precluded** by Article 7(e) of Directive 95/46 if authorities act in public interest, the list is adequate/necessary, and there are sufficient indications for inclusion. The national court must assess necessity, purpose, legal effects, public nature, and consider less restrictive means, adhering to proportionality. Inclusion on such lists can infringe reputation, tax relations, presumption of innocence (Article 48(1) Charter), and freedom to conduct business (Article 16 Charter), and is only proportionate if based on **sufficient grounds for suspicion**. Limitations on data subject rights must be necessary for public interest and based on legislative measures.
- **Valid Consent (Orange Romania, 2020):** For personal data processing to be lawful based on consent, that consent must be **free, specific, informed, and unambiguous**. **Silence, pre-ticked boxes, or inactivity are not valid consent**. If consent is part of a written statement, it must be comprehensible and clearly worded. Contractual terms must not mislead the data subject about their ability to enter the contract without consenting to data processing. The data controller (Orange România) bears the **burden of demonstrating valid consent**. Requiring a customer to complete an additional form to _refuse_ consent unduly affects their freedom to object, as the company must establish active consent, not require active refusal.
- **Public Disclosure of Penalty Points (Latvijas Republikas Saeima, 2021):** The GDPR **precludes national regulations requiring public access to data on road traffic penalty points** without a specific interest shown by the requestor. While road safety is a public interest, public disclosure was **not established as necessary** to achieve this objective, especially given less restrictive alternatives. The sensitivity of the data and the potential for social disapproval and stigmatization mean the rights to private life and data protection **prevail** over public access to official documents and freedom of information. Disclosure to economic operators for re-use is also precluded. Furthermore, EU law's **primacy** means national courts **cannot maintain the legal effects** of national legislation found incompatible with EU law.

# III. Processing of Personal Data Within the Meaning of Sector-Specific Regulations

## 1. Processing of personal data in the electronic communications sector

This sector is particularly regulated due to the sensitive nature of communication data.

- **Access to Communications Data for Crime Fighting (Ministerio Fiscal, 2018):** Access by public authorities to user identification data (e.g., names, addresses) of SIM card holders from a stolen phone constitutes **interference** with fundamental rights, but it is **not considered "serious" interference** as it doesn't allow precise conclusions about private lives. Consequently, such access can be justified for the objective of preventing, investigating, detecting, and prosecuting **"criminal offences" generally**, not only "serious crime". This differs from serious interferences, which require justification by serious crime objectives.
- **General/Indiscriminate Data Retention and Access (Privacy International, La Quadrature du Net and Others, 2020):**
    - National regulations requiring **general and indiscriminate retention or transmission of traffic and location data** to security/intelligence authorities for national security purposes are **precluded** by Directive 2002/58 (ePrivacy Directive) read with the Charter. Such obligations are **particularly serious interferences** due to the absence of a link between individuals' conduct and the objective. GDPR also precludes general and indiscriminate retention of personal data by online service providers.
    - **Exceptions and Permitted Measures:**
        - In situations of a **genuine, present, or foreseeable serious threat to national security**, Directive 2002/58 does not preclude a targeted **order for general and indiscriminate retention of traffic and location data**, for a strictly limited period, subject to effective judicial or independent administrative review. Automated analysis of such data is allowed in this context.
        - **Targeted retention** (limited in time, based on objective/non-discriminatory factors, categories of data subjects, or geographical criteria) is permitted. This can include retention for places with high visitor volume or strategic locations (airports, stations).
        - **General and indiscriminate retention of IP addresses assigned to the source of a communication** is permitted if the retention period is strictly necessary.
        - **Retention of civil identity data of electronic communications system users** is permitted without a specified retention period limit. This includes national legislation requiring ID checks and registration for prepaid SIM card purchases, with access for authorities.
        - **Expedited retention ("quick freeze")** of existing data is allowed to shed light on serious criminal offenses or national security acts that are established or reasonably suspected. This can extend to data of persons indirectly linked (victims, social circle).
        - **Real-time collection** of traffic and location data is allowed if limited to persons reasonably suspected of terrorist activities and subject to prior judicial or independent administrative review (expedited in urgent cases).
    - All these legislative measures must include **clear and precise rules** with substantive and procedural conditions and effective safeguards against abuse. They can be applied concurrently.
    - **Hierarchy of objectives**: Access to data retained for national security purposes cannot be justified for a "lesser importance" objective like combating serious crime, as this would undermine the prohibition on general and indiscriminate retention for the latter purpose.
    - **Admissibility of evidence**: National courts **cannot limit the temporal effects** of a declaration of invalidity of national legislation that is incompatible with Directive 2002/58. The admissibility of evidence obtained in breach of EU law is a matter for national law, subject to effectiveness. National criminal courts must **disregard evidence** obtained via general and indiscriminate data retention in breach of EU law if data subjects cannot effectively comment on it and if it is highly influential or unfamiliar to judges.
- **Prior Judicial/Independent Administrative Review (Prokuratuur, 2021):**
    - The Court reinforced that access to traffic or location data that reveals precise conclusions about private life, for purposes of preventing, investigating, detecting, or prosecuting criminal offenses, **must be confined to procedures combating serious crime or preventing serious threats to public security**. The length of the access period or quantity of data is irrelevant for determining the seriousness of the interference.
    - Such access must be subject to **prior review by a court or an independent administrative body**. This reviewing body must have all necessary powers and guarantees to balance the competing interests, be a **third party** (not involved in the investigation), and maintain a neutral stance. A **public prosecutor's office that directs investigations is not considered independent** for this purpose.
- **Reaffirmation of Principles (Commissioner of An Garda Síochána and Others, 2022):** The Court again confirmed that **general and indiscriminate retention of traffic and location data is not permitted** for combating serious crime and preventing serious threats to public security. It reiterated that crime (even serious crime) cannot be treated identically to a threat to national security, to avoid creating an intermediate category that dilutes the strict requirements. The principles regarding targeted retention, retention of IP addresses and civil identity data, expedited retention, and the requirement for independent prior review were affirmed. The Court also reaffirmed that national courts cannot limit the temporal effects of a declaration of invalidity.
- **Market Abuse Offences (VD and SR, 2022):** The Court ruled that the Market Abuse Directive and Regulation **do not provide a legal basis for a general obligation to retain traffic data** by electronic communications service providers for combating market abuse offenses. Although such data is crucial for detecting market abuse, the regulations only refer to "existing" or "held" records, not a general retention obligation. Therefore, national legislative measures providing for temporary, general, and indiscriminate retention of traffic data for market abuse offenses are **precluded** by Directive 2002/58 read with the Charter. The ruling also confirmed that national courts cannot limit the temporal effects of such invalidity declarations.
- **IP Addresses and Counterfeiting (La Quadrature du Net and others, 2024):**
    - The Court clarified that **general and indiscriminate retention of IP addresses does not _necessarily_ constitute serious interference** with fundamental rights if arrangements genuinely **rule out drawing precise conclusions about private life** (e.g., through no linking with traffic/location data). This requires a **watertight separation** of data categories (e.g., civil identity and IP addresses) through a secure and reliable computer system, with linking allowed only through effective technical processes that don't undermine this separation. The reliability of this separation must be regularly reviewed by an independent third-party public authority. If these strict requirements are met, general IP retention for general criminal offenses is not precluded, for a strictly necessary period.
    - **Access to Civil Identity Data Linked to IP Addresses**: National legislation allowing public authorities to access these separately retained data solely for identifying copyright infringers and taking measures **is not precluded**. Officials must be prohibited from disclosing content, tracking browsing, or using IPs for other purposes.
    - **Balancing of Rights**: Fundamental rights are not absolute and can yield to public-interest imperatives like crime prevention or protecting others' rights. Accessing IP addresses may be the **only means of investigation** to identify perpetrators of online criminal offenses, preventing systemic impunity.
    - **Prior Review for Access**:
        - Required when access carries a **risk of serious interference** (allowing precise conclusions or a detailed profile of the data subject).
        - **Not required** when interference is **not serious** (e.g., sole purpose of identifying an IP holder with watertight data separation).
        - However, in **atypical situations** (e.g., repeated/large-scale infringements revealing sensitive info) or when a public authority refers a matter for criminal prosecution, the interference can become serious. A **prior check by a court or independent administrative body** is then needed before linking civil identity data to such a comprehensive data set and before sending notification letters for criminal proceedings. This review cannot be entirely automated and requires human intervention to balance interests.
    - **System Review**: The public authority's data processing system must be subject to **regular review by an independent third-party body** to verify its integrity, safeguards against abuse, and effectiveness in detecting offending conduct. This processing must comply with Directive 2016/680 for criminal matters.

## 2. Processing of personal data in criminal matters

- **Interpol Red Notices (Bundesrepublik Deutschland, 2021):** The processing of personal data in Interpol Red Notices falls under Directive 2016/680. This processing is permissible and can be indispensable to determine whether the _ne bis in idem_ principle (prohibiting double prosecution) applies. However, if a final judicial decision establishes that _ne bis in idem_ applies, the recording of such data in national wanted lists is **no longer necessary**, and the data subject must be able to request erasure. If data remains, it must be accompanied by a note indicating the principle's applicability.
- **PNR Data Use and Retention Limits (Ligue des droits humains, 2022):** The PNR Directive lists objectives (combating terrorist offenses and serious crime) **exhaustively**. Therefore, national legislation authorizing PNR data processing for other purposes (e.g., intelligence, border controls, illegal immigration) is **precluded**. PNR data cannot be retained in a single database used for multiple purposes. An authority (PIU) is **not independent** if its staff can be seconded from authorities requesting access, and thus cannot approve data disclosure. A **general retention period of five years for all PNR data is precluded**; after an initial six months, retention must be limited to those air passengers for whom objective evidence indicates a risk. Applying the PNR system to other intra-EU transport modes requires a **genuine and present or foreseeable terrorist threat**; otherwise, it must be limited to specific routes/stations with justification, regularly reviewed.

# IV. Transfer of Personal Data to Third Countries

Transferring personal data outside the EU is subject to strict protection requirements.

- **Internet Accessibility vs. "Transfer" (Lindqvist, 2003):** Simply loading personal data onto an internet page hosted in a Member State, making it accessible worldwide, **does not constitute a "transfer to a third country"** under Directive 95/46.
- **Safe Harbour Invalidation (Schrems I, 2015):** The Court **declared Commission Decision 2000/520/EC (Safe Harbour)**, which deemed the US adequate for data protection, **invalid**. The US legal framework, which prioritized national security and law enforcement, **did not provide an "essentially equivalent" level of protection** to EU law, particularly due to the lack of clear limitations on public authorities' access, and effective judicial remedies for individuals. National supervisory authorities retain the power to check if data transfers comply with EU requirements.
- **EU-Canada PNR Agreement Opinion (2017):** The CJEU found that the draft EU-Canada PNR Agreement, providing for systematic transfer and processing of PNR data, could **not be concluded in its proposed form** because several provisions were **incompatible with fundamental EU rights**. The agreement constituted **serious interference** with privacy and data protection due to the extensive nature of PNR data (which can reveal sensitive information). The agreement was deemed **not strictly necessary** and lacked clear/precise rules, particularly regarding the transfer of sensitive data without sufficient justification. Continuous storage of _all_ PNR data after departure was also found not strictly necessary for those not identified as risks. Data subjects must be notified of data transfers and have rights of access, rectification, and an effective remedy.
- **Privacy Shield Invalidation (Schrems II, 2020):** The Court **declared Commission Implementing Decision 2016/1250 (EU-US Privacy Shield) invalid**. It confirmed that EU law, including GDPR, applies to transfers for commercial purposes even if data may later be processed by third-country authorities for public security. The required level of protection must be **"essentially equivalent"** to that in the EU, considering both contractual clauses and the third country's legal system, particularly public authority access. National supervisory authorities are **obliged to suspend or prohibit transfers** if standard data protection clauses (SCCs) cannot be complied with in the third country and protection isn't otherwise ensured. While Decision 2010/87 (on SCCs) was found valid because it requires effective mechanisms and verification by exporters/recipients, the Privacy Shield was invalid because US law continued to grant **primacy to national security, public interest, and law enforcement**, allowing for surveillance programs that were **not strictly necessary** and lacked sufficient guarantees or actionable rights for non-US persons. The Ombudsperson mechanism provided by the Privacy Shield did not offer **substantially equivalent judicial protection**.

# V. Protection of Personal Data on the Internet

The internet presents unique challenges for data protection, leading to specific rulings.

## 1. Right to object to the processing of personal data (‘right to be forgotten’)

- **De-referencing from Search Results (Google Spain and Google, 2014):** The Court established the **"right to be forgotten"** for search engine operators. It ruled that an operator is obliged to **remove links from search results** (when a search is made on a person's name) to web pages containing information about that person, even if the original publication is lawful and the information is not erased from the source page. This obligation applies when the data is no longer necessary, inadequate, irrelevant, or excessive, or due to elapsed time. The data subject's fundamental rights (Articles 7 and 8 Charter) generally **override the economic interest of the search engine operator and the general public's interest** in finding the information, unless the data subject's role in public life justifies a preponderant public interest.

## 2. Processing of personal data and intellectual property rights

- **Disclosure for Copyright Infringement (Promusicae, 2008 and Bonnier Audio and Others, 2012):** EU directives on electronic commerce, copyright, and enforcement **do not explicitly require Member States to mandate the disclosure of personal data** to private entities for civil copyright infringement proceedings. However, Member States must ensure a **fair balance** between conflicting fundamental rights (e.g., private life versus protection of property/effective remedy) and general principles like proportionality when implementing such measures. National courts must weigh these conflicting interests on a case-by-case basis.

## 3. De-referencing of personal data

- **Sensitive Data (GC and Others, 2019):** The **prohibitions and restrictions on processing special categories of personal data** (e.g., racial/ethnic origin, health, criminal convictions) apply to search engine operators. Operators are generally **required to accede to de-referencing requests for sensitive data**, unless exceptions apply (e.g., data manifestly made public by the data subject). The operator must determine if linking is strictly necessary for freedom of information, balancing it against the data subject's rights, considering the nature/sensitivity of the information and public interest (which may vary based on the data subject's public role). For data relating to criminal proceedings, if the information no longer reflects the current situation, the operator must assess the right to de-referencing; if not fully de-referenced, the results list must be adjusted to reflect the **current legal position** (e.g., more recent information appears first).
- **Territorial Scope of De-referencing (Google, 2019):** When a de-referencing request is granted, the search engine operator is required to implement it on the versions of its search engine **corresponding to all EU Member States**, rather than globally. This ensures a consistent and high level of data protection across the EU. The operator must also take effective measures to discourage EU internet users from accessing the de-referenced links via third-country versions of the search engine. While EU law does not _require_ global de-referencing, it **does not prohibit** it, allowing national authorities or courts to order such a broader de-referencing based on national standards of fundamental rights protection.
- **Allegedly Inaccurate Content (Google, 2022):** The Court clarified obligations for de-referencing requests based on allegedly inaccurate information. De-referencing is **not conditional on a prior judicial determination of inaccuracy** against the content provider. The person requesting de-referencing must provide evidence of the **manifest inaccuracy** of a non-minor part of the information. The search engine operator is **not obliged to investigate the facts or organize an adversarial debate** on accuracy, as this would be an excessive burden with a risk of deterring freedom of expression. If manifest inaccuracy is established by the data subject (with evidence or judicial decision), the operator must accede to the request. If not obvious, the operator is not required to de-reference without a judicial decision. If the information is likely to contribute to a public interest debate, freedom of expression is emphasized. Data subjects can appeal an operator's refusal to a supervisory or judicial authority.
    - **Thumbnails in Image Search**: For image searches, the **informative value of photographs** must be considered regardless of their original context, although directly accompanying text can be relevant. Displaying photographs as thumbnails is a **particularly significant interference** with private life. The search engine's display of thumbnails is an **autonomous processing** activity, requiring its own assessment.

## 4. Website users consent to the storage of information

- **Cookies Consent (Planet49, 2019):** Consent for storing or accessing information via cookies (e.g., on a user's terminal device) is **not valid if obtained via a pre-ticked checkbox**, regardless of whether the information constitutes personal data. Consent requires **active, affirmative action** from the user; "silence, pre-ticked boxes or inactivity" are insufficient. Service providers must also inform users about the **duration of cookie operation** and **whether third parties have access** to them.

## 5. Processing personal data on online social networks

- **Meta Platforms and Dominant Position (Meta Platforms and Others, 2023):**
    - Processing of user data by a social network operator (like Meta Platforms) from visits to third-party websites/apps, especially concerning **special categories of personal data** (e.g., health, sexual orientation), constitutes "processing of special categories of personal data" if it reveals such information. Such processing is generally prohibited. Users do **not "manifestly make public"** this data unless they explicitly chose to make it publicly accessible to an unlimited number of persons with full knowledge.
    - **Lawfulness conditions**: Processing is lawful if based on:
        - **Valid consent**: Must be free, specific, informed, and unambiguous.
        - **Necessity for contract performance**: Only if processing is **objectively indispensable** for a purpose integral to the contractual obligation, without which the main subject matter cannot be achieved.
        - **Legitimate interests**: Requires informing users, strict necessity for the interest, and a balancing exercise where user rights/freedoms **do not override** the legitimate interest. In the absence of consent, user interests **override** the operator's interest in personalized advertising for financing its activity.
        - **Legal obligation**: Must be genuinely necessary, based on EU/MS law, meet public interest, be proportionate, and strictly necessary.
    - **Validity of Consent under Dominant Position**: A dominant market position (e.g., on online social networks) does **not inherently preclude valid consent**, but it is an **important factor** to consider whether consent was freely given, given the potential imbalance and effect on user choice. The operator must prove consent validity. Users must be free to refuse consent for data processing not necessary for contract performance **without being forced to stop using the network entirely**, implying the offer of an equivalent alternative (possibly for a fee). Separate consent for "off-Facebook data" must be possible.

# VI. National Supervisory Authorities

National supervisory authorities play a crucial role in enforcing data protection laws and must operate independently.

## 1. Scope of the independence requirement

- **Complete Independence (Commission v Germany, 2010; Commission v Austria, 2012; Commission v Hungary, 2014):** National supervisory authorities must operate with **"complete independence"** (Article 28(1) Directive 95/46 / GDPR) to effectively and reliably monitor data protection compliance. This independence means being **free from any external influence**, whether direct or indirect, including political influence from scrutinizing authorities, to ensure objectivity and impartiality. The **mere risk of political influence** or suspicion of partiality is enough to hinder their independent performance. State scrutiny of supervisory authorities is **not compatible** with this independence. Prematurely ending a supervisory authority's term also violates this obligation, as it creates a threat of "prior compliance" with political authority. While they need not have a separate budget, their equipment and staff allocation must not impede their independence.

## 2. Determination of the applicable law and the competent supervisory authority

- **Cross-border Activity (Weltimmo, 2015):** The law of a Member State other than the one where the data controller is registered can apply if the controller conducts a **real and effective (even minimal) activity** through stable arrangements in that Member State, within which the processing occurs. Factors include the website's language, target audience, and presence of a local representative. A supervisory authority receiving a complaint can examine it regardless of which national law applies. However, if it finds another Member State's law applicable, it **cannot impose penalties outside its own territory** and must cooperate with the other Member State's authority.

## 3. Powers of the national supervisory authorities

- **Wide-ranging Powers (Schrems I, 2015):** National supervisory authorities have a **wide range of powers**, including investigative, intervention (e.g., banning data processing), and legal proceedings, necessary to perform their duties. While their direct powers are limited to their own Member State's territory, the **transfer of personal data _from_ a Member State _to_ a third country is itself a processing operation carried out in the Member State**, granting the national authority the power to check its compliance with EU law.
- **Intervention in Cross-Border Processing (Wirtschaftsakademie Schleswig Holstein, 2018):** When a company has multiple establishments in different Member States (e.g., a US parent company with EU subsidiaries), a supervisory authority in one Member State can exercise its powers over an establishment in its territory, even if data collection/processing for the entire EU is handled by an establishment in another Member State. This authority can **independently assess the lawfulness of data processing** and exercise intervention powers without first seeking the intervention of the other Member State's supervisory authority.
- **GDPR "One-Stop Shop" and Court Actions (Facebook Ireland and Others, 2021):** The GDPR's "one-stop shop" mechanism allocates primary competence to a "lead supervisory authority" for cross-border data processing. However, other national supervisory authorities can, under certain conditions, **exercise their power to bring alleged GDPR infringements before a court in their Member State** and initiate or engage in legal proceedings. This requires the authority to have the competence to adopt a decision finding an infringement and to respect the GDPR's cooperation and consistency procedures. While the lead authority's competence is generally central, it **cannot ignore reasoned objections from other concerned authorities**. This mechanism, including its balance of competences, is compatible with Charter rights to data protection and effective remedy. A supervisory authority can bring legal proceedings even if the controller/processor doesn't have a main establishment in that Member State, as long as there is _an_ establishment in the EU and the action falls within GDPR's territorial scope. Legal actions started before the GDPR's entry into force can continue under Directive 95/46 for prior infringements, and for post-GDPR infringements if the authority has exceptional competence and follows cooperation procedures. The GDPR provision granting supervisory authorities the power to engage in legal proceedings has **direct effect**, meaning they can rely on it even if not specifically implemented in national law.
- **Direct Effect and Constitutional Limits (Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde, 2024):** GDPR provisions on supervisory authority competence (Article 55(1)) and the right to object (Article 77(1)) are **directly effective**, clear, precise, and unconditional. While Member States have discretion over the _number_ of authorities, the GDPR defines the _extent_ of their competences. If a Member State establishes a single authority, it must have all GDPR competences. **National constitutional provisions cannot undermine the unity and effectiveness of EU law** or prevent the single supervisory authority from overseeing processing by legislative bodies, even if the authority is part of the executive.

## 4. Conditions for imposing administrative fines

- **Intentional or Negligent Infringement (Nacionalinis visuomenės sveikatos centras, 2023; Deutsche Wohnen, 2023):** An administrative fine under Article 83 of the GDPR can be imposed on a controller **only if the infringement was found to be intentional or negligent**. The EU legislature did not grant Member States discretion on these substantive conditions for imposing fines. This interpretation aligns with GDPR's purpose to incentivize compliance and deter infringements, but not to impose fines in the absence of wrongdoing. Fines can be imposed on a controller for operations performed by a processor, unless the processor acted for its own purposes or incompatibly without the controller's consent, in which case the processor becomes the controller.
- **Legal Persons as Controllers (Deutsche Wohnen, 2023):** The GDPR **precludes national legislation that requires an administrative fine on a legal person (as controller) to be first attributed to an identified natural person**. GDPR applies to "controllers" regardless of whether they are natural or legal persons, and their liability stems from determining the purposes and means of processing. Legal persons are liable for infringements committed by their representatives or **any person acting on their behalf in the course of business**. Fines must be capable of being imposed directly on legal persons. Such a national requirement would undermine GDPR's effectiveness and deterrent effect. The concept of "undertaking" is relevant for the _amount_ of the fine, not the conditions for its imposition.

## 5. Relationship between the powers of national supervisory authorities and those of other national authorities

- **Competition Authorities and GDPR (Meta Platforms and Others, 2023):** A national **competition authority can find that personal data processing is not GDPR-compliant** if this finding is necessary to establish an abuse of a dominant position by an undertaking. However, the competition authority **does not replace** the data protection supervisory authorities. It must fulfill its **duty of loyal cooperation** (Article 4(3) TEU) with data protection authorities, consulting and cooperating earnestly with them. If a data protection authority or the CJEU has already decided on similar conduct, the competition authority **cannot depart from that decision** (though it can draw its own conclusions under competition law). If there are doubts or simultaneous examinations, the competition authority must consult the data protection authorities and may proceed with its investigation if there is no objection or reply within a reasonable time.
