# Summary

The Court of Justice of the European Union, in a preliminary ruling involving the Czech Ministry of Health, clarified that the **disclosure of a natural person's first name, surname, signature, and contact details when they represent a legal person constitutes the processing of personal data** under the GDPR, irrespective of whether the purpose is solely for identifying the legal representative. Furthermore, the Court ruled that **national case-law requiring a public authority to inform and consult data subjects prior to disclosing official documents containing their personal data is compatible with the GDPR**, provided this obligation is **not impossible to implement, does not require disproportionate effort, and does not result in a disproportionate restriction on public access** to those documents. This judgment emphasizes the balance between public access to official documents and the protection of personal data, underscoring that the right to data protection is not absolute.

# Details

## Background

The case originated from a request by L.H. to the Czech Ministry of Health for information regarding individuals who signed contracts for the purchase of COVID-19 screening tests and related certificates. The Ministry partially granted the request but **redacted personal information** such as the forename, surname, signature, position, and in some cases, email addresses, telephone numbers, and websites of the natural persons who signed on behalf of legal entities, citing GDPR requirements for personal data protection.

L.H. challenged this decision, and the Prague City Court upheld the action, stating that the Ministry could not refuse disclosure without first **informing or consulting the data subjects**, as required by relevant national case-law. The court also noted that the Ministry had not adequately assessed whether any of the lawfulness conditions under Article 6(1) of the GDPR were met. The Ministry of Health appealed, arguing that informing and consulting these individuals was impossible since they operated from China and the United Kingdom, and their domiciles were unknown.

The Nejvyšší správní soud (Supreme Administrative Court, Czech Republic) then referred two questions to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling. The first question concerned whether the data of a natural person representing a legal person constitutes 'personal data' under the GDPR. The second question asked whether national case-law imposing a requirement to inform and consult data subjects prior to disclosure of such data is compatible with EU law, especially when the GDPR itself does not explicitly require consent in such situations.

## Legal Analysis

The Court first addressed whether the information concerning natural persons representing legal entities constitutes 'personal data' and 'processing' under the GDPR.

- The Court affirmed that the definition of "personal data" in Article 4(1) of the GDPR has a **wide scope**, encompassing "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person". This includes information provided as part of a professional activity. Therefore, **first name, surname, and signature of an identified or identifiable natural person are personal data**. Contact details may also be personal data, requiring the national court to ascertain if they are linked to an identifiable natural person.
- Regarding "processing" under Article 4(2) of the GDPR, the Court stated that it also has a **broad scope**, covering "any operation" performed on personal data, including **disclosure by transmission or dissemination**. The purpose of disclosure (e.g., identifying a legal representative) is **irrelevant to its classification as 'processing'**. This interpretation aligns with the GDPR's objective of ensuring a high level of protection for fundamental rights and freedoms, particularly the right to personal data protection.

Secondly, the Court analyzed the compatibility of national case-law requiring prior information and consultation of data subjects with Article 6(1)(c) and (e) of the GDPR.

- The Court acknowledged that **processing must be lawful, fair, and transparent** (Article 5(1)(a) GDPR). Member States can introduce specific provisions to adapt GDPR rules for processing under legal obligations or public interest tasks (Article 6(2) and (3) GDPR), especially for public access to official documents (Article 86 GDPR).
- The right to data protection is **not an absolute right** and must be balanced against other fundamental rights, such as public access to official documents, which can be in the public interest.
- Member States have discretion to specify rules but must do so within GDPR limits and **without undermining its objectives or content**. Any additional requirements must also adhere to the **principle of proportionality**, ensuring practical consequences are not excessive.
- The Court concluded that national case-law requiring prior information and consultation of data subjects **is not, in principle, precluded** by the GDPR. Such an obligation can ensure **lawful, fair, and transparent processing** (Article 5(1)(a) GDPR) by allowing data subjects to express their views and helping public authorities reconcile interests under Article 86 GDPR.
- However, the Court cautioned that an **absolute application of this obligation could disproportionately restrict public access** to official documents. If informing/consulting data subjects proves **impossible or requires disproportionate effort**, it cannot justify a systematic refusal of disclosure, as this would prevent the necessary reconciliation of interests. The Ministry of Health's decision in this case appeared to be based solely on practical impossibility without attempting reconciliation.

## Decision

The Court (First Chamber) ruled:

1. **Points 1 and 2 of Article 4 of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR) must be interpreted as meaning that the disclosure of the first name, surname, signature, and contact details of a natural person representing a legal person constitutes processing of personal data.** #personal_data  The fact that this disclosure is made solely to identify the authorized representative of a legal person is irrelevant.
2. **Article 6(1)(c) and (e) of Regulation 2016/679, read in conjunction with Article 86 of that regulation, must be interpreted as not precluding national case-law which requires a controller (a public authority) to inform and consult the natural person concerned prior to the disclosure of official documents containing such data, provided that such an obligation is not impossible to implement and that it does not require disproportionate effort and, therefore, it does not result in a disproportionate restriction on public access to those documents**.
