# General Rules for Cookie Usage

The State Data Protection Inspectorate (VDAI) prepared a summary of cookie usage practices based on 2024 monitoring results, aiming to address proper implementation and provide examples of good practice.

Key rules for cookie usage include:

- **Consent must be obtained** from the website visitor if non-essential cookies are used.
- Consent requires an **active, affirmative action**; pre-ticked boxes are not considered valid consent.
- "SUTINKU" (Agree) and "NESUTINKU" (Disagree) buttons must be **equally accessible**, and their design should not encourage consent. A uniform button design is considered best practice.
- It must be **as easy to withdraw consent as to give it**.
- Website visitors must receive **clear and comprehensive information** about the types of cookies used, their purposes, and other details regarding personal data processing.

# Types of Cookies

Cookies are commonly categorized based on their creation location, duration, and function.

## By Creation Location

- **First-party cookies** are created by the website the visitor is currently browsing.
- **Third-party cookies** are created by other websites that own content displayed on the site the visitor is browsing.

## By Duration

- **Session cookies** are recorded from the moment a visitor opens a website until they close it, after which they are automatically deleted.
- **Persistent cookies** are stored on the user's computer until deleted by the user through their browser, consent is withdrawn, or their validity period expires.

## By Function

- **Essential cookies** activate basic website navigation or access functions and ensure the proper operation of the website.
- **Functional cookies** remember information that determines the website's appearance (display on the visitor's device), language options, location detection, and other settings.
- **Statistical (performance-enhancing) cookies** collect information and provide reports that help website administrators understand how visitors use the site, such as visit frequency, time spent, feature usage, or error encounters.
- **Marketing cookies** are used to track visitors across multiple websites to deliver personalized advertisements and help evaluate the effectiveness of advertising campaigns.

# General Legal Regulations for Cookies

The Lithuanian Republic Law on Electronic Communications (ERĮ), specifically Article 73(4), stipulates that storing information or enabling access to already stored information on a user's terminal equipment is only permitted with the user's consent. This consent requires clear and comprehensive information, including data processing purposes, in line with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (EU) 2016/679.

Exceptions to this consent requirement include technical storage or data use solely for transmitting information via an electronic communication network, or when strictly necessary to provide information society services requested by the user. This provision implements Article 5(3) of the e-Privacy Directive 2002/58/EB.

Based on the ERĮ provision, **it is necessary to obtain visitor consent before enabling any type of cookie, except for essential cookies**. Given that consent is the legal basis for using all non-essential cookies, website cookie implementation and management must comply with the conditions for consent set out in GDPR Article 7. This notably includes the data subject's right to **withdraw their consent at any time**, and it must be as easy to withdraw as it was to give.

## Type A Practice: Absence of "Disagree" Button

It is considered an ERĮ and GDPR violation if a "Disagree," "Reject," "No, only essential cookies," or similar button is absent from the **first level of the cookie banner** (alongside "Agree," "More information," etc.) when non-essential cookies are used.

- **Unsuitable example (5)** illustrates a situation where the visitor is not given any option to disagree with cookie usage; pressing an "Exit" button instead removes the visitor from the page.

If a website uses **only essential cookies**, consent is not required, but information about their use must still be provided. Example 6 is considered a suitable way to present this information. #transparency 

## Type B Practice: Pre-ticked Boxes

It is considered an ERĮ and GDPR violation when, upon clicking "Cookie Settings," **cookie categories (marketing, statistics, etc.) are pre-ticked**, unless they are essential cookies.

- **Suitable examples (7, 8)** show proper implementation where only essential cookies are pre-ticked.
- **Unsuitable example (9)** demonstrates improper practice where analytical, settings, and unclassified cookies are pre-ticked in addition to essential ones.

## Type C Practice: Hidden "Disagree" Option

It is considered an ERĮ and GDPR violation when the only alternative to giving consent is embedding the "Disagree" option in the general cookie banner text or outside the banner, thereby failing to draw sufficient attention to the option to refuse cookies. Each specific situation must be individually evaluated.

- A **suitable example (10)** is one where both the consent and refusal buttons are equally conspicuous (visible) on the website.
- **Unsuitable example (11)** shows "continuer sans accepter" (continue without accepting) located outside the cookie banner and barely visible.
- **Unsuitable example (12)** depicts the "refusez" (reject) option hidden within the text. #change #consent #cookies 

## Type D, E Practice: Misleading Button Design

It is considered misleading practice when button design (color, contrast, etc.) is configured to make the website visitor more likely to choose "Agree to all cookies" over "Disagree" or "More settings". While each case is unique, it is an e-Privacy Directive and GDPR violation if alternatives to the "Agree" button have **very low contrast** between the button and its text, making the text almost unreadable.

- A **suitable example (13)** shows all buttons with identical design and color.
- An **obvious violation example (14)** displays the text "ablehnen" (reject) as almost unreadable on a white background.

## Type H Practice: Legitimate Interest as Legal Basis

Using **legitimate interest as the legal basis for cookie usage** is considered an ERĮ and GDPR violation. Example 15 is explicitly labeled as an obviously unsuitable implementation of this rule.

## Type I Practice: Incorrect Classification of Cookies

Some data controllers incorrectly classify essential cookies. The classification of cookies into types is not merely a theoretical formality; **proper classification of cookies, especially by their function, is crucial** for ensuring that website cookie management complies with legal requirements. Incorrect cookie classification (e.g., misclassifying cookies that require separate consent as "essential") leads to cookie management that contradicts personal data protection regulations.

## Type K Practice: Ease of Consent Withdrawal

Website administrators must implement solutions that allow visitors to **easily withdraw consent at any time**, such as a small, floating, and constantly visible icon or a link in a prominent/easy-to-find location. Each implemented solution for consent withdrawal must be evaluated individually to ensure that consent is as easy to withdraw as to give.

- A **suitable example (16)** involves a floating, continuously visible icon for managing cookie settings (given consent).
- Another **suitable example (17)** is a website's cookie policy providing information about current cookie settings and offering the ability to change these settings (withdraw/give consent).

# Information on Cookie Usage

According to GDPR Article 5(1)(a), personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly, and transparently. GDPR Article 13 outlines the information that must be provided to data subjects when personal data is collected directly from them. Therefore, data subjects must be informed about cookie usage and associated personal data processing on the website by providing the required information.

A common oversight in information notices is failing to specify the **legal basis for processing personal data** collected via cookies, as per GDPR Article 6(1). While consent is needed for non-essential cookies, subsequent use of personal data collected with cookies might rely on other legal bases listed in GDPR Article 6(1), and users must be informed about these. Additionally, considering the nature of cookie usage and data collection, it is essential to explain what cookies are, provide a list of used cookies, specifying their types, the information collected, and the data storage period.

Information about cookie usage can be presented in layers:

- **First level:** Should provide information about which cookies (essential, non-essential) are used and their purposes.
- **Second level (via an active link):** Should contain all other detailed information about cookie usage and personal data processing.

# Data Transfer to Third Countries

Cookie information notices frequently omit information about **data transfer to third parties and/or third countries**, as required by GDPR Article 13. GDPR Chapter V governs the transfer of personal data to third countries or international organizations. #change #transparency 

The European Commission may issue an **"adequacy decision,"** declaring that a third country ensures an adequate level of data protection. This allows data transfer to companies in that country without requiring the data exporter to implement additional safeguards. For example, on July 10, 2023, the European Commission adopted an adequacy decision regarding data transfers to the USA, approving the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, which permits personal data transfer between EU and certified US entities. A list of certified US entities is available.

If no "adequacy decision" is in place, data can still be transferred using **appropriate safeguards**, provided individuals are granted enforceable rights and effective remedies. Such safeguards include:

- **Binding Corporate Rules (BCR)** for groups of undertakings or groups engaged in a joint economic activity (GDPR Article 47).
- Agreements with the personal data recipient based on **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)** approved by the European Commission (GDPR Article 46(2)(f)).
- Implementation of **codes of conduct or certification mechanisms** that obligate the recipient to apply appropriate safeguards to protect transferred data (GDPR Article 46(2)(f)).

If personal data is intended for transfer to a third country without an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards, transfers can occur based on **derogations for specific situations**. An example is when an individual explicitly consents to the proposed data transfer after receiving all necessary information about the associated risks.
