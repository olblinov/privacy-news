# Common Deficiencies in Privacy Policies

Organizations frequently collect and process personal data, typically providing information about this processing via privacy policies published on their websites. The DVI reviews these policies to ensure they accurately reflect actual data processing and comply with GDPR requirements. The DVI has identified several common shortcomings in published data processing rules.

## Accessibility Issues

A frequent problem is that privacy policies are **not easily found or accessed** on an organization's website.

- Policies are often placed within general sections with vague or unrelated titles, such as "Important!" or "For Clients," instead of having a **dedicated, separate section**.
- Sometimes, the link to the privacy policy is only included in a **cookie pop-up banner**; once the user selects cookie settings and the banner disappears, the policy becomes inaccessible.
- **Recommendation**: A privacy policy **should be accessible from the first page** as a separate section with a **clear, descriptive title** like "Privacy Policy" or "Data Processing Rules".

## Lack of Customization and Accuracy

Organizations often publish **standardized or copied privacy policies** that are not tailored to their specific commercial activities.

- This results in policies containing **irrelevant, incomplete, or misleading information**, failing to provide accurate and current details about the specific data processing performed by that controller.
- **Example**: An electronics sales company's privacy policy describing data processing related to travel planning services, which the company does not offer.
- **Recommendation**: The privacy policy must **only include information relevant to the actual data processing** conducted by the organization. Since each business operates uniquely, there is no one-size-fits-all solution for privacy policies.

## Structural and Clarity Problems

Privacy policies are sometimes drafted **without a logical structure**, failing to divide crucial data processing aspects into distinct sections.

- This makes policies **unclear and difficult to comprehend**.
- Information is often repeated, and **complex terminology or generalized phrases** are used, making the policy longer but not more informative.
- **Recommendation**: Organizations must ensure their privacy policies are **easily understandable for individuals without prior knowledge** of data protection regulations. Policies should be **concise, easy to review, and unambiguous**, leaving no room for interpretation.
- **Example**: Using the phrase "the said data will be processed according to your wishes" when "data is processed based on personal consent" would be clearer and more precise.

## Incomplete Information on Processing Purposes and Legal Bases

Organizations frequently **fail to provide complete information on all processing purposes** and their corresponding legal bases in their privacy policies.

- Information might be limited to, for example, data processing for product sales, while other processing activities, such as sending commercial communications based on email addresses collected on the website, are not explicitly described in the policy.
- **Recommendation**: Organizations must clearly state the **purpose and legal basis for _each_ data processing activity**, describing them together to allow individuals to verify the lawfulness of each operation. This enables individuals to understand their rights and potential limitations.
- **Incorrect approach**: Stating processing purposes and legal bases in separate, unlinked sections.
- **Correct approach (example)**: A table format clearly linking each purpose to its specific legal basis as enumerated in GDPR Article 6, Paragraph 1. For instance, "Product sales and delivery to customers" (purpose) paired with "Performance of a contract or service at the Data Subject's request prior to concluding a contract (GDPR Article 6, Paragraph 1, Point b)" (legal basis).

## Insufficient Detail on Legitimate Interests

When organizations process personal data based on **legitimate interests** (GDPR Article 6, Paragraph 1, Point f), they often **fail to specify _which_ legitimate interests** are being secured, contrary to GDPR requirements (Article 13, Paragraph 1, Point d).

- **Example**: An organization should specify if data is processed for "advertising company services" or "preserving evidence in case of disputes".

## Vague Data Retention Periods

Privacy policies often vaguely state that "data is processed as long as necessary to achieve the processing purposes".

- This statement is **too general**.
- **Recommendation**: Organizations should **specify the retention period** (e.g., in months, days, years) or, if not possible, provide **concrete criteria** used to determine the period, adapted to the specific data category and processing purposes. This is a requirement under GDPR Article 13, Paragraph 2, Point a.

## Unspecified Data Recipients

Privacy policies often **fail to specifically name personal data recipients or categories of recipients**, even when stating that data is transferred to third parties. This is contrary to GDPR Article 13, Paragraph 1, Point e.

- **Recommendation**: If the organization has agreements or other justifications for data transfer, it typically possesses the names of these recipient organizations. These **names must be disclosed to individuals**. If public disclosure is not feasible for some reason, **categories of recipients** (e.g., delivery service providers, accounting service providers) should be specified.

## Missing Information on Data Subject Rights

Controllers frequently **fail to list the data subject rights** guaranteed by GDPR (such as the right to object, request data deletion, or data portability) and the **procedures for exercising these rights**.

- **Example**: A policy might state that commercial communications are sent based on consent but fail to mention the **right to withdraw that consent at any time** (GDPR Article 13, Paragraph 2, Point c).
- **Example**: Policies may list rights but **omit the contact information, channels, or website tools** available for individuals to request information or actions regarding their data.
- **Example**: Some policies outline **overly complex mechanisms** for exercising rights, such as requiring an in-person visit with an identity document.
- **Recommendation**: Privacy policies must be supplemented with clear instructions on the **steps data subjects need to take** to exercise their rights. Organizations should establish and state **convenient and accessible methods** for exercising these rights, such as submitting electronically signed applications or accessing information via a user profile.
