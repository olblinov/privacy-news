The Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act), which **entered into force on August 1, 2024**, introduces a classification system for Artificial Intelligence Systems (AIS) based on their risk level, ranging from "low" to "unacceptable". AIS classified as **"unacceptable risk" systems**, listed in Article 5 of the AI Act, are **strictly prohibited** because they are considered contrary to the values of the European Union. This prohibition has been applicable since **February 2, 2025**.

# Prohibition and Consequences

Since February 2, 2025, these unacceptable risk systems **cannot be deployed within the European Union**. Their deployment could lead to legal action before courts in cases of damage caused to a third party. The CNPD (Luxembourg's National Commission for Data Protection) emphasizes that most of these unacceptable risk AIS would also be problematic under several **principles of the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)**, particularly concerning the **principle of transparency**. Consequently, regardless of the CNPD's specific role under national AI provisions (like draft law no. 8476), such systems could prompt complaints or investigations from the CNPD if deployed in Luxembourg.

# Strictly Prohibited AI Systems (Article 5 of the AI Act)

The AI Act specifically prohibits the following practices and systems:

- **Subliminal techniques** (Art. 5, § 1, a).
- **Exploitation of vulnerabilities**: AIS targeting individuals' vulnerabilities (e.g., age, motor skills) to materially alter their behavior in a way that causes or could cause significant harm. For instance, an application designed to induce children to make in-app purchases by exploiting their lack of discernment would be forbidden. Similarly, an AIS detecting a person's depressive state to sell them personalized holidays would be problematic.
- **Social scoring under specific conditions**: Prohibited if it leads to prejudicial or unfavorable treatment in well-defined situations (Art. 5, § 1, c).
- **Predictive policing operations**, particularly without human intervention (Art. 5 § 1, d).
- **Creation or development of facial recognition databases through untargeted scraping of facial images** from the internet or video surveillance (Art. 5, § 1, e). An example is a system that collects or is developed by collecting the maximum number of faces available online to compare them with faces of individuals under video surveillance for identification purposes.
- **Use of AIS for emotion recognition in the workplace or educational establishments** (Art. 5, § 1, f). This prohibition applies unless the use of the AIS is for medical or safety reasons. An example is an AIS designed to analyze employees' facial expressions, vocal tone, or gestures to measure engagement, satisfaction, or stress for the purpose of improving well-being or productivity.
- **Biometric categorization individually categorizing natural persons** to deduce or infer sensitive characteristics such as race, political opinions, or sexual orientation (Art. 5, § 1, g).
- **Use of real-time remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces for law enforcement purposes**, unless strictly necessary for specific victim searches, prevention of serious threats, or criminal investigations for particularly serious offenses (Art. 5, § 1, h). The AI Act lists detailed additional conditions for these specific exceptions.

It is important to note that a **case-by-case analysis is necessary** as some AI practices are prohibited only in specific contexts. The European Commission published its guidelines on prohibited AI practices on February 4, 2025, providing precise definitions.

# Overlap with GDPR Principles

Many of the prohibited AIS under the AI Act also pose significant issues under the GDPR. The source illustrates this link with examples:

- **Exploitation of Vulnerabilities**: An AI system detecting a person's depressive state to sell them holidays involves health data, which is generally prohibited under **Article 9 of the GDPR**, unless specific conditions apply. For children, the GDPR already sets **conditions for consent regarding information society services in its Article 8**.
- **Untargeted Facial Recognition Databases**: The collection and use of such databases for identification purposes would involve the processing of **biometric data for unique identification**, which is prohibited under **Article 9 of the GDPR**, as none of the exceptions would apply.
- **Emotion Recognition in the Workplace/Education**: This practice, unless for medical or safety reasons, would constitute generalized employee surveillance. This raises problems concerning several GDPR principles, including **lawfulness, transparency, and data minimization**.

# Additional AI Act Obligation

Beyond the prohibitions, **Article 4 of the AI Act** imposes another obligation on companies using artificial intelligence: **all persons involved in the operation and use of AI systems must possess a sufficient level of mastery**. This obligation also took effect on February 2, 2025, and further details will be provided by the CNPD in a future publication.
