**Opinion of Advocate General Szpunar** was delivered on February 6, 2025, in Case C-492/23 concerning **X v. Russmedia Digital SRL, Inform Media Press SRL**. This opinion addresses a request for a preliminary ruling from a Romanian court regarding the **liability of online marketplace operators** in relation to **electronic commerce (Directive 2000/31/EC)** and **personal data protection (Regulation (EU) 2016/679 - GDPR)**.

The core of the case revolves around a unique situation where an advertisement containing personal data (photographs, phone number) was published without consent on an online marketplace, indicating the individual offered sexual services, which was disparaging and offensive. The dispute raises fundamental questions about the **relationship between Directive 2000/31/EC and the GDPR**, specifically whether an online marketplace operator is liable under GDPR and eligible for liability exemption under Directive 2000/31/EC.

# I. Legal Context

## A. European Union Law

### 1. Directive 2000/31/EC (E-commerce Directive)

This directive aims to approximate laws relating to electronic commerce.

- **Scope:** It generally **does not apply** to questions concerning information society services covered by data protection directives (e.g., Directives 95/46/EC and 97/66/EC, now replaced by GDPR and Directive 2002/58/EC).
- **Liability of Intermediary Service Providers:** Articles 12, 13, and 14 establish conditions under which information society service providers are **exempt from liability** for information provided by users, depending on their activity (mere conduit, caching, or hosting).
- **Article 14 (Hosting):** A service provider is **not liable** for stored information if it **does not have actual knowledge of illegal activity or information** (or is not aware of facts/circumstances making it apparent) OR if, upon obtaining such knowledge/awareness, it **acts expeditiously to remove or disable access**.
- **Article 15 (No General Obligation to Monitor):** Member States **cannot impose a general obligation** on providers to monitor information they transmit or store, nor to actively seek facts indicating illegal activity. However, they may require providers to inform authorities of alleged illegal activities or communicate user identification information upon request.

### 2. Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)

- **Material Scope:** Article 2(4) states that the GDPR applies **"without prejudice"** to Directive 2000/31/EC, particularly its liability rules for intermediary service providers (Articles 12-15).
- **Definitions (Article 4):**
    - **"controller"**: A natural or legal person determining the **purposes and means of processing personal data**.
    - **"processor"**: A natural or legal person processing personal data **on behalf of the controller**.
    - **"consent"**: Any **freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous** indication of the data subject's wishes for processing their personal data.
- **Principles relating to processing of personal data (Article 5):** Personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly, transparently, for specified legitimate purposes, and with appropriate security measures ("integrity and confidentiality"). The **controller is responsible for and must demonstrate compliance** with these principles ("accountability").
- **Lawfulness of processing (Article 6):** Processing is lawful only if, among other things, the **data subject has given consent** for specific purposes.
- **Conditions for consent (Article 7):** If processing is based on consent, the **controller must be able to demonstrate** that the data subject has consented.
- **Responsibility of the controller (Article 24):** The controller must implement **appropriate technical and organisational measures** to ensure and demonstrate GDPR compliance, considering the nature, scope, context, purposes, and risks of processing.
- **Data protection by design and by default (Article 25):** The controller must implement measures to integrate **data-protection principles and safeguards** effectively into processing, ensuring by default that only necessary personal data are processed and are not made accessible without the individual's intervention to an indefinite number of persons.
- **Joint controllers (Article 26):** Where two or more controllers jointly determine purposes and means, they are joint controllers and must transparently determine their responsibilities.

## B. Romanian Law

- **Law No 365/2002 on electronic commerce:** This law incorporates provisions similar to Directive 2000/31, stating that service providers are liable for information they supply but are generally **not liable for information transmitted or stored** under conditions laid down in Articles 12-15. Article 14(1) of this law mirrors Directive 2000/31's hosting exemption.

# II. The Facts of the Dispute in the Main Proceedings

**Russmedia Digital SRL** operates the online marketplace **www.publi24.ro** for advertisements. On August 1, 2018, an unidentified person published an advertisement on this platform that was **disparaging and offensive** towards the appellant, stating she offered sexual services. This advertisement included her photographs (used without consent from her social media) and telephone number, and the identity of the poster was not verified.

Russmedia removed the advertisement less than an hour after being contacted by the appellant. However, the same advertisement was later **reproduced by other websites**, indicating the original source, and remains accessible.

The appellant sued Russmedia, arguing an infringement of her rights.

- The **Judecătoria Cluj-Napoca (Court of First Instance)** upheld the action, finding Russmedia liable under Romanian Civil Code Article 253 for **breaching GDPR obligations** and infringing personal rights, ordering EUR 7,000 in damages.
- The **Tribunalul Specializat Cluj (Specialised Court)** overturned this, ruling Russmedia was **exempt from liability** under Article 14(1) of Law No 365/2002 as a mere storage service provider not actively involved in content. It noted Russmedia's expeditious removal of the ad and stated that the law does not require prior content checking.
- The appellant appealed to the **Curtea de Apel Cluj (Court of Appeal, Cluj)**, the referring court, arguing Law No 365/2002 is not special law concerning GDPR and Russmedia had a management role beyond mere technical provision, thus Article 14 should not apply, especially given GDPR liability.
- The referring court set aside the Specialised Court's judgment, noting its failure to consider GDPR applicability. It acknowledged no general prior verification obligation for online marketplaces but cited case-law (_L'Oréal and Others_, _Papasavvas_) suggesting **active roles or awareness of illegality preclude exemption**. The court highlighted the **"manifestly unlawful and deeply harmful"** nature of the ad, suggesting notification might not have been necessary for Russmedia to grasp its unlawfulness. It also pointed out the continued redistribution of the ad and Russmedia's **retained rights to use/modify content** under its terms, suggesting it was not a "mere passive user". #change 

# III. Questions Referred for Preliminary Ruling

The Curtea de Apel Cluj referred the following questions to the Court of Justice:

1. Whether **Articles 12-14 of Directive 2000/31** apply to a hosting provider that retains rights to use, copy, distribute, modify, and remove content, despite claiming a purely technical role.
2. Whether **GDPR provisions (Articles 2(4), 4(7)&(11), 5(1)(f), 6(1)(a), 7, 24, 25) and Article 15 of Directive 2000/31** require a hosting provider, as a data controller, to **verify advertiser identity** before publishing an advertisement.
3. Whether the same GDPR and Directive 2000/31 provisions require a hosting provider, as a data controller, to **verify content in advance** to exclude potentially unlawful or privacy-infringing advertisements.
4. Whether **GDPR provisions (Articles 5(1)(b)&(f), 24, 25) and Article 15 of Directive 2000/31** require a hosting provider, as a data controller, to **apply safeguards to prevent or limit reproduction and redistribution** of advertisement content.

# IV. Advocate General's Analysis

The Advocate General (AG) analyzes the questions in order, first addressing Directive 2000/31, then GDPR, and finally their interaction.

## A. The First Question (Directive 2000/31 - Exemption from Liability)

This question asks if Russmedia can benefit from the **hosting exemption** under Article 14(1) of Directive 2000/31, particularly given its terms and conditions regarding content use. The AG examines whether Russmedia acts as an "intermediary service provider" and if the conditions for exemption are met.

### 1. The Concept of 'Intermediary Service Provider'

- **Scope:** The AG focuses on Article 14, as the service appears to be hosting. Russmedia asserts a "purely technical" role, but the appellant contests this.
- **Neutrality and Passivity:** The exemption in Article 14(1) applies only if the service provider's activity is of a **"mere technical, automatic and passive nature,"** implying no knowledge or control over transmitted/stored information.
    - The Court's case-law states that to be exempt, an online marketplace operator must provide service **"neutrally"** by technical and automatic processing, rather than playing an **active role** that gives it knowledge or control.
    - Mere storage, setting terms, remuneration, or providing general information does not deny the exemption. Automatic indexing, search functions, or recommendations also do not automatically preclude exemption if the provider is otherwise neutral.
- **Relevance of Terms and Conditions of Use:**
    - Russmedia's T&Cs allow it to use, copy, distribute, modify, and remove content, despite not claiming ownership.
    - The AG argues that **contractual arrangements allowing potential action are not decisive** if the service provider is not _obliged_ to intervene or _does not actually implement_ such actions.
    - The focus should be on **actions actually undertaken**. Case law indicates that active assistance in optimizing or promoting offers, or inherent knowledge/control (e.g., newspaper publishing its own content), points away from neutrality. #change 
    - **In Russmedia's case, there is no indication it intervened in content creation, optimization, or presentation prior to publication.** Its control is merely potential and _ex post_. Such T&Cs are common and in line with the need for intermediaries to act against illegal content once identified. #change 
- **Interim Conclusion (First Question):** Article 14(1) of Directive 2000/31 applies if the provider retains rights to use content but **does not take any action that would cause it to cease to be classified as a neutral hosting provider**.

### 2. The Conditions Required for an Intermediary Service Provider to be Exempted from Liability

The referring court highlighted the "manifestly unlawful and deeply harmful" nature of the ad and its wider reproduction.

- **Manifestly Illegal Nature of the Information Stored:**
    - Article 14(1)(a) exempts if there's no knowledge of illegal activity. For damages claims, the provider is ineligible if **aware of facts/circumstances making illegality "apparent"** and fails to act expeditiously (Article 14(1)(b)).
    - Awareness must be of **specific illegal information/activities**, not just a general sense that the service is used for illegal content.
    - It **cannot be presumed** that an operator is aware of all manifestly illegal content, as this would imply a general monitoring obligation, which is incompatible with Article 15(1) of Directive 2000/31.
- **A Platform Involved in Illegal Activities:** The exemption may be lost if a service provider **deliberately collaborates** with users to undertake illegal acts (Recital 44), or adopts a business/organizational model that **encourages or promotes dissemination of manifestly illegal content**. There is no indication Russmedia adopted such a model.
- **Developments in EU Law:** Directive 2000/31, a product of its time, establishes a basic regime with a prohibition on general monitoring. While newer legislation (like the Digital Services Act) introduces content moderation obligations, these are not applicable to the facts of this case under Directive 2000/31. 
- **Redistribution of the Content:** The AG considers two scenarios for the ad's reproduction:
    - **Reproduction by Partners (contractual):** If Russmedia transmits ads to partners, its role might be less neutral. However, if its role in this transmission is purely technical and automated, Article 14(1) could still apply. For the condition in Article 14(1)(b) (expeditious removal) to be met, a hosting provider involved in redistributing information to contractual partners **should take reasonable steps to ensure that content is also removed or blocked by its partners** (e.g., via contract clauses). This balances protection of rights (privacy, freedom of expression). #change 
    - **Reproduction by Third Parties (without knowledge/consent):** The operator is **not required to take steps in relation to third parties** that reproduce publicly accessible ads without its knowledge or control, as Article 14(1)(b) applies only to measures within its power.

## B. The Second, Third, and Fourth Questions (GDPR - Obligations)

These questions ask about the obligations of a hosting provider, assumed to be a **personal data controller**, to verify identity/content and implement safeguards against redistribution. The AG first questions the "data controller" characterization.

### 1. Legal Characterizations (Controller vs. Processor)

- **Definitions:** A **controller** determines purposes and means; a **processor** processes data on behalf of the controller.
- **Purpose and Means of Processing:** The "purpose" is _why_ data are processed; the "means" are _how_. The controller does not need to determine _every_ technical aspect; non-essential means can be left to a processor.
- **Operator of an Online Marketplace and the GDPR:**
    - The processing of personal data _in advertisements_ must be distinguished from the processing of data _of user advertisers_ (e.g., account registration).
    - For **personal data _in advertisements_**, the **user advertiser determines the purpose** (e.g., to promote a service) and influences the _essential_ means (e.g., what data is included). The operator (Russmedia) does not determine these for its own purposes. Its influence on non-essential technical aspects does not make it a controller.
    - Therefore, the AG concludes that, for personal data contained in advertisements, the **online marketplace operator acts as a processor**, on behalf of the user advertiser (who is the data controller). #change #vendor_management 
    - However, if the operator _actively uses_ the data for its own purposes (e.g., copying, modifying as allowed by T&Cs), it could become a **controller for _that specific processing operation_**. #change 
    - This aligns with Article 29 Working Party and EDPB guidance that a hosting service is generally a processor unless it processes data for its own purposes beyond storage.

### 2. The Obligations and Responsibilities of the Operator

## (a) Where the Operator is a Processor (for ad content)

- If Russmedia is a **processor** for the personal data in advertisements, it is **not responsible for checking whether the processing (by the user advertiser) is authorized and legal**. It is not required to verify content in advance. #change 
- Regarding security measures (fourth question, Article 32 GDPR): A processor must implement **appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk** against interference from third parties.
- If ads are reproduced by _contractual partners_ (known to user advertisers via T&Cs), the processor **should implement measures enabling it to require removal** of ads from its partners once removed from its own platform. This is consistent with Directive 2000/31's requirement for expeditious removal. #change 
- However, a processor should **not be required to prevent or limit copying/redistribution** generally, as it's not for the processor to determine who accesses data or how.

## (b) Where the Operator is a Controller (for user advertiser data)

- For the processing of data of **user advertisers (e.g., account creation)**, the operator is the **data controller**, and Articles 24 and 25 of the GDPR fully apply. #pbd 
- **Obligations (Articles 24 & 25):** The controller must implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure compliance and protect data subjects, taking into account risks.
- **Risk of Identity Theft/Impersonation:** Given risks like identity theft and irresponsible anonymous use, an online marketplace operator **must implement measures to reduce that risk**.
    - This includes implementing measures to **verify the identity of user advertisers** to limit illegal/unfair processing and combat impunity. This does not preclude anonymous internet use, as data can be verified and stored only by the controller for compliance purposes. #pbd 
    - This is a "best endeavors" obligation, not a guarantee, but Russmedia reportedly implemented no such verification measures. #pbd #change 
- **Conclusion for Questions 2, 3, 4 (AG's proposal):** Russmedia, as a **processor** for ad content, is **not required to verify ad content or prevent redistribution by third parties**, but must implement **security measures against third-party interference**. As a **controller** for user advertiser data, it **is required to verify the identity of user advertisers**.

## (c) Alternative Case (If operator _were_ a data controller for ad content)

- If the operator were a data controller for content in ads, it would need to ensure processing is lawful (e.g., based on consent) and check ad content before publication. It would need to introduce measures to prevent harm to data subjects.
- Regarding redistribution, while the controller _may_ consider measures if there's a risk of incompatible processing, the primary purpose of online content is public accessibility, implying redistribution. The AG reiterates that the ad should not have been published without consent in the first place.

## C. The Relationship between Directive 2000/31 and the GDPR

The questions suggest parallel application of these two instruments.

### 1. A Neutral Hosting Provider as Data Controller

- There is a **tension** between being a "neutral hosting provider" under Directive 2000/31 and a "data controller" under GDPR for the _same_ information.
- A "neutral" intermediary has no knowledge or control. A "data controller" determines purpose and means, exerting influence and proactive obligations over data.
- Therefore, if a hosting service provider is a **data controller for personal data it stores, its role is not neutral**, and it is **ineligible for the Article 14(1) exemption**. This reinforces the AG's view that Russmedia is a processor for ad content. #change 
- The AG notes that the Digital Services Act (DSA), adopted later, _does_ include a clause (Article 7) that _would_ allow a hosting provider to maintain neutrality even while complying with Union law (like GDPR), but Directive 2000/31 does not contain such a clause. #change 

### 2. Parallel Application of the GDPR and Directive 2000/31

- **Directive 2000/31, Article 1(5)(b), explicitly states it does not apply to personal data protection issues covered by specific data protection rules**. Recital 14 emphasizes that data protection is _solely_ governed by these specific directives.
- Case-law (e.g., _La Quadrature du Net and Others_) confirms that Directive 2000/31 is **not applicable** where data protection is governed by GDPR or Directive 2002/58/EC. However, where other matters are concerned, they can apply in parallel, but **Directive 2000/31 may not undermine GDPR requirements**.
- **GDPR, Article 2(4), states it applies "without prejudice" to Directive 2000/31, indicating it does not limit the scope of the Directive's specific provisions**. GDPR does not take precedence merely because it was adopted later.
- **GDPR's liability exemption (Article 82(3))** is distinct from Directive 2000/31's. Article 82(3) allows a controller/processor to be exempt from liability if it proves it is "not in any way responsible for the event". This is a condition for liability, based on _fault_, not a broad exemption like Article 14(1).
- **Conclusion on Parallel Application:** A party involved in processing as a **processor** that _could_ be held liable under GDPR can **still rely on the exemption from liability provided for in Article 14(1) of Directive 2000/31**. This means the AG's proposed answers are consistent regardless of whether liability arises from GDPR or national law.

# V. Conclusion (Proposed Answers)

The Advocate General proposes the following answers to the Curtea de Apel Cluj:

1. **Article 14(1) of Directive 2000/31/EC** should be interpreted to mean that a hosting provider operating an online marketplace, even if its terms allow it to use, copy, distribute, modify, and remove content, **may still be eligible for the liability exemption, provided it does not take actions that would cause it to cease to be classified as a neutral hosting provider**.
2. **Articles 5(1)(f), 6(1)(a), 7, 24, and 25 of the GDPR** should be interpreted to mean that an online marketplace hosting provider **acts as a processor** for personal data in advertisements posted by users. In this capacity, it is **not required to verify content or prevent copying/redistribution** of ad content. However, it must implement **appropriate technical and organizational measures to ensure processing security against third parties**. Conversely, for the personal data of registered user advertisers, the provider **acts as a data controller** and **is required to verify their identity**.
