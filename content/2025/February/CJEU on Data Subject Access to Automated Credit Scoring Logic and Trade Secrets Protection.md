# Summary

This judgment from the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) addresses a request for a preliminary ruling from an Austrian court concerning the interpretation of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Trade Secrets Directive. The case involves a data subject, CK, who was denied a mobile phone contract due to an automated credit assessment by Dun & Bradstreet (D&B). CK sought "meaningful information about the logic involved" in this profiling, as provided by GDPR Article 15(1)(h). #profiling  The CJEU ruled that data subjects have a right to a clear explanation of the procedures and principles used in automated decision-making to obtain a credit profile. Furthermore, it clarified that concerns about trade secrets or third-party data do not automatically preclude this right; instead, the controller must provide such information to a competent supervisory authority or court, which is then responsible for balancing the conflicting rights and interests on a case-by-case basis.

# Details

## Background

The legal proceedings originated when CK was refused a mobile telephone contract due to an automated credit assessment performed by Dun & Bradstreet Austria GmbH (D&B), which indicated insufficient financial creditworthiness. CK then brought the matter before the Austrian data protection authority, which ordered D&B to disclose meaningful information about the logic involved in the automated decision-making that used her personal data.

D&B challenged this decision, claiming that the information was protected as a trade secret. However, the Bundesverwaltungsgericht (Federal Administrative Court, Austria) found that D&B had infringed Article 15(1)(h) of the GDPR by failing to provide sufficient explanations or reasons. #rights  This decision became final and enforceable under Austrian law.

Despite the final decision, CK's subsequent application for enforcement with the City Council of Vienna was rejected, as the Council deemed D&B had met its obligation without providing additional information. Consequently, CK brought an action against this rejection before the Verwaltungsgericht Wien (Administrative Court, Vienna), the referring court in this preliminary ruling.

An expert appointed by the referring court determined that D&B should provide specific minimum information to meet its obligations, including:

- **The personal data concerning CK processed to formulate a 'factor'** (e.g., date of birth, address, sex).
- **The mathematical formula** on which the calculation leading to the score is based. #change 
- **The specific value attributed to CK** for each relevant factor.
- **The precise intervals within which the same value is attributed** to different data for the same factor.
- A list of scoring for a 12-month period to enable verification of accuracy.

The referring court observed discrepancies between the information provided by D&B and CK's actual credit assessment, leading to questions about the right to verify accuracy and the extent to which trade secrets or third-party data could restrict the right of access. The court also raised concerns about the compatibility of a specific Austrian law (Paragraph 4(6) of the Datenschutzgesetz), which generally precludes data subject access if it compromises a business or trade secret, with GDPR provisions. The proceedings were briefly suspended awaiting a judgment in another related case (SCHUFA Holding and Others (Scoring)), but the referring court maintained its request, stating the previous judgment did not fully answer its questions.

## Legal Analysis

The CJEU examined the interpretation of **"meaningful information about the logic involved"** in Article 15(1)(h) of the GDPR by considering its wording, context, and purposes.

Regarding the **wording**, the Court noted that "meaningful information" has diverse complementary meanings across different language versions, relating to the information's functionality, relevance, importance, intelligibility, or value. The term "logic involved" is broad, covering the "procedure" and "principles" concerning the automated use of personal data. Thus, the wording implies that all relevant information regarding the procedure and principles for automated data use to achieve a specific result should be covered.

In **context**, this information is part of the broader right of access under Article 15, which also includes the significance and envisaged consequences of processing. It forms a whole with other information obligations under Articles 13(2)(f) and 14(2)(g) of the GDPR. Crucially, Article 12(1) of the GDPR requires this information to be provided in a **concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language**.

As for the **purposes of the GDPR**, the regulation aims to ensure a high level of protection for fundamental rights, particularly the right to personal data. The right of access under Article 15 enables data subjects to verify the correctness and lawfulness of their personal data processing and to effectively exercise other rights like rectification, erasure, and objection. Specifically, in automated decision-making, the purpose of Article 15(1)(h) is to enable data subjects to **effectively exercise their rights under Article 22(3) of the GDPR**, which include the right to express their point of view and contest the decision. This requires the data subject to understand the reasons behind the automated decision. Recital 71 of the GDPR further confirms the data subject's right to an explanation of the decision.

Therefore, the CJEU concluded that "meaningful information about the logic involved" means a **right to an explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied** to use personal data by automated means to obtain a specific result, like a credit profile. #rights #change #profiling  This explanation must be provided using relevant, concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible information. It is not satisfied by a complex mathematical formula or a detailed description of all steps; it must enable the data subject to understand how their data was used. #profiling  The controller should provide simple rationales and criteria, and for profiling, demonstrate how a variation in data would alter the outcome. The right to verify the accuracy of the underlying personal data is covered by the introductory sentence of Article 15(1), not specifically by 15(1)(h).

The Court also addressed the **tension between the right of access and the protection of third-party data or trade secrets**. It recalled that the right to personal data protection is not absolute and must be balanced against other fundamental rights, including trade secrets and intellectual property, as per Recital 63 of the GDPR. However, this balancing should **not result in a refusal to provide all information**. When information to be provided contains third-party data or trade secrets, the controller is **required to provide this allegedly protected information to the competent supervisory authority or court**. This authority or court must then conduct a **case-by-case balancing of the rights and interests at issue** to determine the extent of the data subject's right of access. The Court found that national laws, such as Paragraph 4(6) of the Austrian DSG, that generally exclude access if it compromises a trade secret, are incompatible with Article 15(1)(h) of the GDPR, because they prescribe a definitive result instead of allowing for a necessary case-by-case balancing.

## Decision

The Court (First Chamber) ruled as follows:

1. **Article 15(1)(h) of Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)** must be interpreted as meaning that, in the case of automated decision-making, including profiling, the data subject may require the controller, as **‘meaningful information about the logic involved’**, to **explain, by means of relevant information and in a concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, the procedure and principles actually applied** in order to use, by automated means, the personal data concerning that person with a view to obtaining a specific result, such as a credit profile.
2. **Article 15(1)(h) of Regulation 2016/679** must also be interpreted as meaning that, where the controller believes the information to be provided contains data of third parties protected by that regulation or **trade secrets** (within the meaning of Directive (EU) 2016/943), that controller is **required to provide the allegedly protected information to the competent supervisory authority or court**. This authority or court must then **balance the rights and interests** at issue to determine the extent of the data subject’s right of access provided for in Article 15 of that regulation. #change #rights #profiling 

The decision on costs for the parties involved in the main proceedings rests with the referring court.
