# Summary

The "OSS Case Digest on the right of access" is a report that analyzes decisions related to Article 15 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) made by national Supervisory Authorities (SAs) under the one-stop-shop (OSS) mechanism. Commissioned by the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) as part of the SPE program, this digest draws on 52 selected decisions from 185 reviewed final decisions between May 1, 2024, and August 1, 2024. The purpose is to shed light on how components and elements of the right to access are applied in OSS decisions. It is important to note that the views expressed are those of the author and do not necessarily reflect the official position of the EDPB, nor does the EDPB guarantee the accuracy of the information.

## Introduction and Overall Observations

The **right of access to personal data** is a fundamental data protection right, enshrined in Article 8 of the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights. Article 15 of the GDPR, applicable to access requests made after the GDPR became effective, specifies this right into three key components:

- **Confirmation** of whether personal data related to the data subject is processed.
- **Access to and a copy** of the personal data if it is being processed at the time of the request.
- **Information** about the processing activities and the data subject's other data protection rights.

The practical aim of this right, as repeatedly stated by the European Court of Justice (ECJ), is to enable data subjects to verify the correctness and lawfulness of their personal data processing, and to facilitate the exercise of other rights such as rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, and the right to object.

OSS decisions generally reflect a consistent understanding of Article 15's aim and its interplay with other GDPR rights. They are also influenced by relevant GDPR recitals (especially Recital 63 and 39 regarding transparency). A notable point is that the underlying motives of data subjects for requesting access are generally **of no significance unless misuse of law can be proven**. This consistent approach is likely to prevent fragmentation in GDPR implementation.

However, the digest observes a potential challenge to the effective enforcement of Articles 15 and 12 of the GDPR: almost all reviewed OSS decisions originate from complaints almost exclusively concerning **private sector data controllers**. This low representation of public authorities is logical given that the OSS mechanism rarely applies to public sector access rights (Article 55(2) GDPR), and national legislation often governs access to public documents. This focus on national regulation might lead to data protection issues being overlooked in cases also dealing with national administrative law. In contrast, many reviewed access decisions arise in commercial contexts between consumers and businesses, or in employment/recruitment settings.

Other observations from the OSS decisions include:

- The majority of complaints regarding the right of access also involve **other data protection issues** (e.g., Article 5 principles or other Chapter III rights).
- A significant percentage touch upon **designs, internal procedures, staff training, or roles** in relation to data processors. For example, **OSS 2022:268** highlighted a case where a platform for online reviews, acting as a data processor, was found regrettable for not having consistent procedures for searching relevant information to assist the controller in responding to access requests.
- Scenarios involving **parents acting on behalf of their children** pose challenges for controllers due to interpretive insecurity, suggesting a need for further guidance.

## Article 15 GDPR: Components and Case Insights

### The Scope of Article 15 GDPR

Article 15 provides access to personal data processed at the time of the request, referring to the definitions in Article 4(1) (personal data) and 4(2) (processing) of the GDPR.

- **Personal Data Identification**: Most OSS decisions involve clear personal data like names, email addresses, financial transactions, and phone numbers. Distinctions between personal data and business matters or legal assessments are consistent with ECJ rulings. **OSS 2022:407** provides an example where a request for documentation relating to water leak damages in a residence was not considered a request for access to personal data. Similarly, **OSS 2020:108** stated that medical assessments are personal data.
- **Online Behavior Data**: For online behavior data (activity logs, search activities) identified via usernames, verified emails, or assigned IDs, ECJ case law is consistently followed, and the definition of personal data is interpreted broadly. **OSS 2023:809** illustrates this: browsing data collected via cookies, where a unique identifier allowed the controller to re-identify individuals by reasonable means, was deemed personal data even without directly holding the data subject's identity.
- **Advancement: Impersonation and Fake Profiles**: OSS decisions offer more detailed insights into varying data protection issues in online environments, particularly concerning fake online profiles and identity theft.
    - **Personal data generated by impersonating or hacked profiles is to be considered data related to the impersonated data subject**. Examples include **OSS 2023:685** (social media) and **OSS 2023:962** (online forum). **OSS 2022:527** further clarifies this in non-social media contexts (e.g., fraudulent account registration). #change 
    - A condition for this is that the profile **must appear to the controller and third parties as one that actually pretends to be the data subject**. Consequently, online parody profiles are not regarded as impersonation, meaning data subjects cannot gain access under Article 15 to data generated during parody activities if they do not aim to pretend to be the data subject.
    - **OSS 2023:784** demonstrated a unique scenario where a data subject who created a profile under a fake name was later granted access when wanting to change it to his genuine name.
- **Processing Definition and Data Erasure**: While interpretations of "processing" generally cause no difficulties, misunderstandings can lead to controllers erasing personal data after receiving an access request. In **OSS 2023:962**, the controller deleted an account (created fraudulently using the data subject's email) _after_ the access request was made. The LSA found this erasure unjustified since the account was active during the request, thereby violating Article 15.

### Confirmation on Whether Personal Data is Processed

Controllers are primarily obliged to confirm whether or not they process personal data concerning the requesting individual. If no data is processed, only this confirmation is required. Violations in this area often stem from a lack of response, which can be due to staff forgetting, unclear internal procedures, or communication mishaps like emails getting lost in spam filters. **OSS 2021:209** provides an example where a data subject claimed no reaction to their request, while the controller claimed to have informed them of data deletion, leading to dismissal of the case once confirmation was received during proceedings.

### Access to and Copy of the Personal Data

Data subjects have the right to access all personal data processed by the controller at the time of the request. The provided data must be complete, up-to-date, and a true reflection of the processing state when the request was received. The first copy must be free of charge, and if requested electronically, it should be provided electronically using commonly used means unless otherwise specified by the data subject.

- **Data Erasure After Request**: Many complaints arise because personal data has been erased _after_ the request. **OSS 2020:159** illustrates this: a flight company deleted a call recording requested by a data subject due to company policy and an unintended delay in processing the request. The LSA found a violation of Article 15 because the data was undergoing processing at the time of the request.
- **Security Measures for Electronic Access**: Controllers often refuse specific electronic means of providing data (e.g., email) due to security concerns. **OSS 2021:270** saw a controller refuse email access, instead making data available on the data subject's secure account. The LSA found no GDPR violation, acknowledging the controller's responsibility to ensure appropriate security (Article 5(2)(f) and Article 32 GDPR).
- **Insight: Digital Skills**: The digest notes that cases often revolve around difficulties using self-service systems or requests for email provision, potentially indicating varying levels of digital skills within European populations. **This is highlighted as a potential underlying problem not directly addressed in the reviewed OSS decisions**, suggesting a need for user-friendly and inclusive system design.
- **Clarity of Information**: Surprisingly, OSS decisions do not indicate friction between providing understandable data and complete data; data subjects generally seem able to handle and understand the provided personal data.

### Contextual Information

Beyond access to personal data, controllers must provide additional contextual information about the processing and other relevant Chapter III GDPR rights (Article 15(1)). This information must be accurate, clear, and understandable, reflecting the specific processing operations for the data subject.

- **Purpose and Recipients**: Controllers must describe the purposes of processing, categories of personal data, and recipients. **OSS 2019:42** showed incomplete information where data was used for marketing purposes by sponsors, despite the controller stating it was solely for a prize competition.
- **Advancement: Specific Recipients**: Regarding recipients, controllers are to inform of **specific recipients when such detailed information is possible**. **OSS 2022:367** clarified that the controller must provide information about _actual_ recipients, especially when explicitly requested, unless proving impossibility or disproportionate effort. This aligns with ECJ case law and the principles of fairness and transparency.
- **Storage Period**: The envisaged storage period or criteria for its determination must be described immediately understandably. **OSS 2019:42** deemed a reference to "national statutory retention periods" insufficient, emphasizing that **data subjects should not have to check applicable legislation themselves**. #retention 
- **Information on Rights and Source**: Controllers must also inform about the right to rectification, erasure, restriction of processing, objection, the right to lodge a complaint with an SA, and the source of data if not collected from the data subject.
- **Insight: Competent SA**: **OSS 2019:42** stated that while there's no legal obligation, the Liechtenstein SA recommends that the controller's information should state the competent supervisory authority or at least the criteria for its designation to facilitate the exercise of rights.
- **Automated Decision-Making**: Article 15(1)(h) requires information on automated decision-making, including profiling, and meaningful information about the logic, significance, and envisaged consequences. The digest notes that the OSS register lacks decisions clarifying how detailed the description of underlying logic needs to be, pending future ECJ clarification.

### Limitations and Exceptions to the Right to Access

The GDPR sets out limitations and restrictions in Articles 15(4) and 12(5). If only parts of the information are subject to exceptions, the remaining information must still be provided. **OSS 2022:517** exemplified this, where a data subject was granted access to non-damaging personal data, while information related to a serious Terms of Service violation that could pose a security risk to others was withheld via amicable settlement.

### Article 15(4) GDPR (Rights and Freedoms of Others)

This provision states that the right to obtain a copy of personal data shall not adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others. The interpretation is broad, extending to trade secrets, intellectual property, and copyright (Recital 63).

- **Business Secrets and Communications**: **OSS 2022:457** addressed withholding anti-cheat information (business secrets) and in-game chat messages from access. The LSA found it permissible to withhold anti-cheat measures if decisive interests were at stake and there was an obvious danger of adverse effects. However, **there was no legal basis for not providing a copy of chat messages sent _directly to and from_ the complainant**. Messages between others could potentially be withheld, especially if in different languages or jargon implying confidentiality.
- **Advancement: Employee Voices in Recorded Calls**: **OSS 2021:254** and **OSS 2022:407** clarified the application of Article 15(4) to recorded phone calls. In **OSS 2021:254**, a controller refused to provide copies of call recordings containing employee voices. The LSA stated that **recording a person doing a job for the controller cannot be considered to adversely affect the rights and freedoms of the employee** under Article 15(4). The data subject had a right to a copy, and merely offering to listen was insufficient. This indicates a narrow interpretation of restrictions on the right to access.

### Article 12(5) GDPR (Manifestly Unfounded or Excessive Requests)

A controller may reject or charge a reasonable fee for requests that are manifestly unfounded or excessive.

- **Advancement: "Manifestly Unfounded" including Abuse of Law**: "Manifestly unfounded" means the requirements of Article 15 are clearly not met, which has a limited scope given few prerequisites for access requests. However, **OSS 2021:218** indicated that "manifestly unfounded" may **include data subjects' abuse of the law**. In this case, the data subject explicitly offered to drop the access request if a financial reimbursement claim was met, which was deemed an abuse of rights.
- **"Excessive" Requests**: Whether a request is excessive depends on all circumstances and isn't limited to repetitive requests. Repetitive use of electronic self-service systems is unlikely to be excessive if it doesn't strain the controller.
- **Challenges with Self-Service Systems**: The digest highlights a potentially overlooked challenge: complications arise when data subjects lose passwords, lack digital skills to use self-service systems, or their accounts are hacked. This points to an increasing need for **user-friendly and inclusive designs for electronic self-service systems** and robust procedures for atypical scenarios to support data subjects in exercising their rights effectively.

## Perspectives on Article 12 GDPR

Article 12 of the GDPR sets out crucial requirements to ensure the effective exercise of data subjects' rights, specifying the controller's broad obligation to facilitate these rights. Most OSS decisions related to Article 15 also concern Article 12.

- **Communication Requirements**: Controllers must communicate information in a concise, transparent, intelligible, and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language, especially for children. They must also facilitate the exercise of rights by providing appropriate and user-friendly communication channels. Data subjects are not obliged to use specific channels and do not need to mention GDPR or justify their request.
- **Response Timeframes**: Requests should be answered without undue delay, generally within one month, extendable by two months for complex or numerous requests, provided justified notification is given before the initial month expires. This aggregated three months is an absolute time limit. Doubts about identity may suspend the countdown if the controller requests adequate information without delay.
- **Timeframe Violations**: Human errors, inadequate procedures, and misunderstandings frequently lead to controllers exceeding these timeframes.
- **Advancement: Identity Verification**: The means for identifying data subjects (Article 12(6) GDPR) and broader data security requirements (Article 5(1)(f), Article 32 GDPR) proportionally cause difficulties. **Controllers requiring official documents (e.g., passport, ID card) for identity proof when other verification means are available and were not needed for initial profile creation/login are problematic if there is no reasonable doubt about the data subject's identity**. This clarifies that additional information for identity verification can only be gathered if there is reasonable doubt.

## Concluding Remarks

The analysis indicates that the enforcement of Article 12 significantly supports data subjects in invoking their right to access under Article 15. Most reviewed OSS decisions involve private sector data controllers and processors, particularly concerning social media and online environments with commercial elements. SAs are familiar with cooperating on access issues and often dismiss or settle cases if matters are resolved during proceedings. OSS decisions frequently rely on ECJ case law and increasingly refer to EDPB Guidelines on the right of access. Crucially, OSS decisions provide valuable guidance on interpretation issues related to Article 15, **especially regarding themes arising in online environments, such as handling impersonation in various contexts and for different purposes**.

# Detailed Analysis of Specific Cases

## Trustpilot and Obligation to Search using All Available Data

The case involves a complaint made on February 10, 2020, by a data subject against Trustpilot A/S regarding Trustpilot’s response to an access request. The Danish Data Protection Agency (DPA) was designated as the lead supervisory authority for Trustpilot A/S.

Here's a summary focusing on the obligation to make use of provided data to search for associated data:

- **Background of the Complaint**
    
    - On May 11, 2019, the complainant made an online purchase from Asus on Ebay, using the email `ebay@levaria.de`.
    - On February 3, 2020, and again on February 8, 2020, the complainant received invitation emails from `noreply.invitations@trustpilot.com` to `ebay@levaria.de`, asking them to evaluate the Asus buying experience.
    - On February 4, 2020, the complainant contacted Trustpilot from a _different_ email address, `service@le-varia.de`, and requested access to personal data Trustpilot might process about them. This inquiry included their name and address.
- **Trustpilot's Initial Response and Search Process**
    
    - Trustpilot replied on February 6, 2020, stating they could not locate an active user for `service@le-varia.de` and therefore did not process any information about the complainant associated with that email.
    - Trustpilot explained they **searched on `service@le-varia.de`** the first and second time the complainant contacted them and could not identify them because this email was not registered.
    - When Trustpilot became aware of the complaint, they also **conducted a search using the complainant's name**. However, they stated they could not uniquely identify the complainant by name alone or in conjunction with `service@le-varia.de`, as Trustpilot had several registered names matching the complainant's.
    - Trustpilot confirmed that they processed information associated with `ebay@levaria.de` as a **data processor for Asus Online Shop**. They stated that if `ebay@levaria.de` had been provided in the access request, they would have referred the complainant to Asus Online Shop, for whom Trustpilot processed that data.
- **Trustpilot's Role Distinction**
    
    - Trustpilot generally describes itself as an open platform where people can read, write, and collect reviews.
    - Trustpilot considers itself the **data controller** for information collected when data subjects use Trustpilot's website, create user profiles, or submit reviews.
    - However, Trustpilot considers itself a **data processor** when sending invitation emails, as companies like Asus decide whether and when to use Trustpilot's invitation software and provide the personal data used for invitations. The Danish DPA agreed with Trustpilot's assessment of acting as a data processor for sending invitation emails on behalf of Asus Online Shop.
- **DPA's Assessment of Obligations and Search Practices**
    
    - The DPA clarified that under GDPR Articles 12 and 15, it is the **data controller's responsibility** to handle and respond to a request for access, not the data processor's. Therefore, the DPA did not find Trustpilot in breach of these specific provisions.
    - **However, the DPA found it regrettable that Trustpilot did not have a consistent practice to search relevant information, including the name and address, which the data subject had submitted in connection with their request under Article 15 of the GDPR**. #change 
    - The DPA emphasized that Trustpilot, in its role as a processor, should have **"exhaustively explored the possibility of uniquely identifying"** the data subject to assist the controller (Asus Online Shop) to the extent agreed in their data processing agreement. #change 
    - Trustpilot’s Data Processing Agreement states that Trustpilot **will promptly assist** with data subject requests relating to their processing of relevant data and will inform the requesting data subject if a review invitation email has been sent on the controller's behalf, directing them to submit their request to the controller.
- **Trustpilot's Subsequent Action**
    
    - Trustpilot stated that, based on this case, they have **rethought their communications to data subjects**, focusing more on providing guidance on Trustpilot's different roles in personal data processing.

In summary, while Trustpilot was deemed a data processor and not directly responsible for fulfilling the access request, the Danish DPA criticized Trustpilot for **not exhaustively utilizing the provided name and address to uniquely identify the data subject** when conducting searches. This failure to consistently search all provided data points hindered Trustpilot's ability to fully assist the data controller, as outlined in their data processing agreement.

## CNIL's Decision on Browsing Data
### **Summary of the Case: Obligation to Make Use of Provided Data to Search for Associated Data**

The case details the CNIL's investigation into a company, hereinafter referred to as "the Company," which specializes in the display of targeted advertising through "advertising retargeting" by using cookies to track internet users' browsing habits. The CNIL acted as the lead supervisory authority for this cross-border processing. The Company was deemed a **data controller** for these processing operations.

Regarding the obligation to make use of provided data to search for associated data, the CNIL's findings focused on the Company's non-compliance with its **obligation to uphold the right of access of data subjects to their personal data** under Articles 12 and 15 of the GDPR.

- **Context of Access Requests:** The Company received requests from individuals to exercise their right of access to their personal data.
- **Company's Initial Response and Data Provided:**
    - When individuals made access requests, the Company provided data extracted from three specific tables: "Advertiser_advent," "Banner_display," and "Click_cas". These tables stored data related to advertiser events, ad display information, and user interactions with ad banners, respectively.
- **Rapporteur's Findings on Missing Data:** #rights #personal_data 
    - The rapporteur argued that the Company **only partially responded** to these requests because it **did not supply data contained in three other significant tables**:
        - **"Usermatching" table**: This table contains information that enables the reconciliation of identifiers (e.g., if the same user uses multiple devices) in a "deterministic" manner. It links the Company's unique cookie identifier ( ID) with other identifiers provided by partners, such as loyalty card numbers, Apple or Android identifiers, and/or hashed email addresses. The Company later undertook to provide this data in its responses to access requests from November 2022.
        - **"bc_tcp_timestamp" table**: This table contains information for _probabilistic_ reconciliation of identifiers, meaning it applies a prediction model to link two identifiers believed to correspond to the same user. The Company argued that providing data from this table could potentially harm the rights and interests of third parties if data relating to another individual were communicated. The **Restricted Committee accepted this reasoning**, noting that the Company was _not required_ to provide data from this table due to the risk of concerning multiple individuals without certainty.
        - **"Bid_request" table**: This table contains technical data related to online auction protocols, with approximately 400 fields. The Company committed to providing all data from this table by March 2023.
- **Lack of Intelligibility:** Beyond the missing data, the rapporteur also noted that the information provided by the Company was **not intelligible to the user**. The Company only offered a brief summary description of each table's goal, without explaining the purpose of each column or their content. While the Company later supplemented explanations with a table detailing the nature, description, and examples of data for each table, this was a post-finding improvement. #change 
- **CNIL's Conclusion on the Breach:**
    - The Restricted Committee noted that, _on the date of the findings_, the Company's explanations **did not justify the non-communication of data from the "Usermatching" and "Bid_request" tables**, as these tables contained personal data that could be combined with the identifier assigned to each internet user.
    - Consequently, the CNIL found that by **not communicating all the personal data of individuals** exercising their right of access and by **not providing them with documentation enabling them to understand the data supplied**, the Company failed to fulfill its obligations under **Articles 12 and 15 of the GDPR**. This highlights that a data controller must not only gather but also fully communicate all relevant personal data, associated through various identifiers, in a comprehensible manner.
- **Corrective Actions and Sanction:** The Company updated its practices during the proceedings, including providing more data for access requests and improving intelligibility. However, this **late achievement of compliance did not negate the past breaches**. As a result of these and other GDPR breaches (Articles 7, 12, 13, 17, and 26), the CNIL imposed an administrative fine of **forty million euros (€40,000,000)**.
