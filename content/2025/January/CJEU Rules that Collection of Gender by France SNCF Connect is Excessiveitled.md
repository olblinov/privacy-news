# Summary

The Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) issued a preliminary ruling in a case concerning SNCF Connect's practice in France of requiring customers to provide their title (Mr/Ms) when purchasing online travel documents. The association Mousse challenged this practice, arguing it infringed the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) principles of lawfulness and data minimisation, as well as risking discrimination, particularly for non-binary individuals. The French data protection authority (CNIL) initially rejected Mousse's complaint, prompting Mousse to seek annulment before the Conseil d’État, which then referred questions to the CJEU. The Court ruled that processing customer title data for purposes like personalizing commercial communication is **not objectively indispensable** for contract performance. Furthermore, it held that such processing is **not necessary for legitimate interests** if customers were not informed of the interest, if the processing is not strictly necessary, or if it risks overriding fundamental rights like freedom from discrimination. The Court also clarified that the existence of a data subject's right to object under Article 21 GDPR does not determine the lawfulness or necessity of the initial data processing.

# Details

## Background

SNCF Connect, a French railway undertaking, systematically requires its customers to indicate their title ('Monsieur' or 'Madame') when purchasing travel documents via its website and online applications. Mousse, an association, lodged a complaint with the Commission nationale de l’informatique et des libertés (CNIL), arguing that this practice violated the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Specifically, Mousse contended that the collection of title data was inconsistent with the principle of **lawfulness** (Article 5(1)(a) GDPR) because it lacked a valid legal basis under Article 6(1) GDPR. Mousse also claimed it infringed the principle of **data minimisation** (Article 5(1)(c) GDPR) and the **obligations of transparency and information** (Article 13 GDPR). It was further argued that such an obligation could infringe upon the right to travel without disclosing one's title, the right to respect for private life, and the freedom to define one's gender expression, potentially leading to a risk of **discrimination**, especially for nationals whose civil status recognizes "non-binary" identities.

The CNIL, by a decision dated 23 March 2021, rejected Mousse's complaint, finding that the processing of title data was lawful under Article 6(1)(b) GDPR (necessary for the performance of a contract). The CNIL also viewed the processing as consistent with the data minimisation principle, considering that addressing customers in a personalized manner using their title aligns with accepted practices in commercial, civil, and administrative communications.

Mousse subsequently brought an action for annulment of the CNIL's decision before the Conseil d’État (Council of State, France), which is the referring court. The Conseil d’État posed two primary questions to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling:

1. Whether commonly accepted practices in civil, commercial, and administrative communications can justify the collection of customer titles limited to 'Monsieur' or 'Madame' as necessary under Article 5(1)(c) and Article 6(1)(b) and (f) of the GDPR.
2. Whether the potential existence of a data subject's right to object under Article 21 GDPR should be considered when assessing the necessity of compulsory collection and processing of title data.

## Legal Analysis

The Court reiterated that the GDPR's objective is to ensure a **high level of protection** for fundamental rights and freedoms, particularly the right to privacy in personal data processing. Any processing of personal data must adhere to the principles outlined in Article 5 of the GDPR and satisfy the lawfulness conditions in Article 6.

**Principles of GDPR:**

- **Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency:** Personal data must be processed lawfully, fairly, and in a transparent manner.
- **Data minimisation:** Personal data must be **adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary** in relation to the purposes for which they are processed. This principle gives expression to **proportionality**.
- The first subparagraph of Article 6(1) GDPR provides an **exhaustive and restrictive list** of cases where personal data processing is lawful. These justifications, allowing processing without data subject consent, must be **interpreted restrictively**. The requirement of necessity for any justification is not met if the objective can be achieved just as effectively by **less restrictive means** of fundamental rights.

**Lawfulness of Processing (Article 6(1) GDPR):**

1. **Necessity for the performance of a contract (Article 6(1)(b) GDPR):**
    
    - For processing to be necessary for contract performance, it must be **objectively indispensable** for a purpose integral to the contractual obligation. The controller must demonstrate that the main subject matter of the contract cannot be achieved without that processing. 
    - Being merely "useful" or "referred to in the contract" is **irrelevant**; the processing must be **essential** for proper performance, and there should be **no workable, less intrusive alternatives**.
    - The Court found that personalizing commercial communication based on a customer's presumed gender identity (derived from title) is **not objectively indispensable** for the proper performance of a rail transport contract. #minimization
    - A less intrusive solution exists: the transport undertaking can use **generic, inclusive expressions** when addressing customers who do not wish to indicate their title or in general, without correlation to their presumed gender identity.
    - SNCF Connect's additional justification that title data is needed for adapting night train services or assisting disabled passengers was deemed **disproportionate** for the systematic and generalized processing of all customers' data, as it could be limited to only those customers requiring such specific services. #change 
    - Therefore, the Court concluded that processing title data for personalised commercial communication **cannot be regarded as necessary for contract performance**.
2. **Necessity for the purposes of legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f) GDPR):**
    
    - This justification requires three cumulative conditions:
        - **Pursuit of a legitimate interest:** A wide range of interests can be legitimate, including direct commercial marketing, especially when there's a client relationship. However, the controller must **inform the data subject of the legitimate interest pursued at the time of data collection** (Article 13(1)(d) GDPR).
        - **Necessity to process personal data for that interest:** The processing must be **strictly necessary** for the legitimate interest and cannot be reasonably achieved as effectively by less restrictive means. The Court noted that personalised commercial communication can often be limited to surnames and forenames, making title/gender identity data **not strictly necessary**. It explicitly stated that **common practices and social conventions are not a basis for assessing necessity** under this provision, given its restrictive interpretation. #legitimate_interest 
        - **Balancing test (interests or fundamental rights of the data subject do not override the legitimate interest):** This requires a balancing of opposing rights and interests, dependent on specific circumstances. The reasonable expectations of the data subject and the processing's scale and impact are crucial. A customer of a transport undertaking is **not reasonably supposed to expect** their title or gender identity data to be processed for purchasing a travel document, especially for direct marketing. The legitimate interest for direct marketing **cannot prevail where there is a risk that the fundamental freedoms and rights of the data subject will be infringed**, particularly if it could lead to **discrimination** (Recital 75 GDPR). The Court referenced Directive 2004/113/EC, which combats sex discrimination in goods/services, noting its applicability to discrimination arising from a change in a person’s gender identity. #legitimate_interest #change 
    - Therefore, processing title data for personalised commercial communication based on gender identity **cannot be regarded as necessary for legitimate interests** if customers were not informed of the legitimate interest, if the processing is not strictly necessary, or if fundamental rights (especially freedom from discrimination) prevail.

**Right to Object (Article 21 GDPR):**

- The Court clarified that the applicability of the right to object under Article 21 presupposes the existence of **lawful processing** based on Article 6(1)(f).
- The **existence of a right to object cannot be taken into consideration for the purpose of assessing the lawfulness or necessity of processing personal data**. To do so would weaken the requirements of Article 6(1)(f) and contradict the GDPR's objective of ensuring a high level of protection.

## Sanction

The provided source is a **judgment of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) concerning a preliminary ruling**. The CJEU's role in a preliminary ruling is to interpret European Union law, not to decide the dispute itself or impose sanctions. The national court (Conseil d’État) will dispose of the case in accordance with the CJEU's decision.

Therefore, the sources **do not mention any sanction or fine** imposed on SNCF Connect. The initial complaint by Mousse to the CNIL was **rejected**, and the ongoing proceedings before the Conseil d’État aim to annul that rejection.
