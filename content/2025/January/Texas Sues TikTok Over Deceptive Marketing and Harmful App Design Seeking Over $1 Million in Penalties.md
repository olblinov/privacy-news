# Summary

Attorney General Ken Paxton of Texas has filed a lawsuit against TikTok and its parent companies, including ByteDance, for **deceptively marketing its app as safe for children** despite the widespread presence of inappropriate and explicit content. The lawsuit alleges that TikTok **lied about its safety standards and concealed the truth** regarding graphic videos depicting drugs, nudity, alcohol, and profanity, which were easily accessible to minors, contradicting its "infrequent" and "mild" claims. Furthermore, the State claims TikTok **purposefully designed its app to be addictive** to minors, prioritizing profit over the well-being, development, and mental and physical health of young Texans, leading to issues like body image problems, eating disorders, and even suicide. The State of Texas is seeking a permanent injunction to halt these deceptive practices, civil penalties of up to **$10,000 per violation**, and total monetary relief exceeding **$1,000,000**.

# Details

## Background

On January 9, 2025, Attorney General Ken Paxton announced a lawsuit against TikTok, one of the largest social media platforms, for allegedly **deceptively promoting its app as safe for minors**. The lawsuit names TikTok Inc., TikTok Ltd., TikTok Pte. Ltd., ByteDance Ltd., and ByteDance Inc. as defendants. This action builds on a previous lawsuit filed by Attorney General Paxton against TikTok for violating the Securing Children Online Through Parental Empowerment (“SCOPE”) Act, which aimed to protect the online safety and privacy of Texas children.

The core of the current complaint is that TikTok has **"utterly disregarded the health and safety of Texas minors"** by allowing its platform to be "rife with profanity, sexual content, violence, mature themes, and drug and alcohol content". An investigation conducted by the State of Texas found a "virtually endless stream of videos on such topics easily accessible to minors," even those as young as thirteen, with some content having millions of views. TikTok is accused of **falsely representing that the presence of graphic videos was "infrequent" and "mild"** to deceive parents and maintain its age ratings in major app stores.

The lawsuit also highlights that TikTok "specifically targets its app to minors and purposefully designed its app to be addictive". This design, characterized by an "endless scroll of videos," is seen as a "core element" of TikTok's business model to maximize ad revenue by keeping users, especially young Texans, "glued to the app". The State alleges that TikTok has **failed to disclose the truth about the mature and extreme content** and its addictive nature, and has "affirmatively lied about it". These actions are reported to have had "particularly pronounced" effects on young women, causing "rampant body image issues, eating disorders, and even suicide". The complaint cites specific instances of harm to Texas minors, including the assault of an Arlington teen, the death of an eight-year-old girl in Temple, and teen hospital visits in Tarrant County.

## Legal Analysis

The lawsuit is brought under the **Texas Deceptive Trade Practices—Consumer Protection Act (“DTPA”)**, Tex. Bus. & Com. Code Ann. §§ 17.41 et seq., which declares "false, misleading, or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce" unlawful. The Attorney General is charged with enforcing these protections.

The State alleges that TikTok has violated DTPA by:

- **Misrepresenting characteristics or standards of its service**.
- **Advertising with intent not to sell as advertised**.
- **Failing to disclose information** known at the time of the transaction, which was intended to induce the consumer into a transaction they would not have entered had the information been disclosed.

Specifically, the State makes the following detailed allegations:

- **Deceptive Age Ratings**: TikTok self-selects a **"12+" age rating** in Apple’s App Store and a **"T for Teen" rating** in the Microsoft and Google Play app stores. These ratings are based on TikTok's representations that categories like "Alcohol, Tobacco or Drug Use or References," "Sexual Content or Nudity," "Mature/Suggestive Themes," and "Profanity or Crude Humor" are "infrequent/mild". The State's investigation found these claims to be **"misleading, deceptive, and false,"** as such content is **"present in large quantities on the TikTok app and are accessible to 13-year-old users"** in their "For You Feed" and through search.
    
- **Profanity or Crude Humor**: The State asserts that profanity and crude humor are **neither "infrequent" nor "mild"** on TikTok. Examples from the State's investigation include videos with extremely profane song lyrics and racial slurs that have hundreds of millions of views.
    
- **Alcohol, Tobacco, and Drug Use or References**: The lawsuit claims such content is **"neither 'infrequent' nor 'mild'"**. Internal content moderation policies do not align with public representations, and there are high levels of "leakage" of drug-related content onto users' "For You Feed". Examples from the State's research include videos promoting meth use, recipes for high-alcohol drinks, discussions about microwaving marijuana, mixing alcohol and drugs (like Xanax into "jungle juice"), and references to fentanyl.
    
- **Sexual Content and Nudity**: This content is also alleged to be **"neither infrequent nor mild,"** with high "leakage" rates. The State's investigation found abundant sexual content, including videos prominently showing genitalia, twerking in thongs, explicit lyrics, and even the ability for minor users to access hardcore pornography via TikTok's in-app browser.
    
- **Mature/Suggestive Themes**: These themes, which include content related to alcohol, drugs, sex, nudity, profanity, and other complex topics, are said to be **frequent and intense**. Examples found include content depicting self-harm (cutting, eraser burns), eating disorders ("I can't eat. I can't stop eating...", "thinspiration"), and distorted body image. The algorithm's ability to create **"filter bubbles"** can further expose minors to psychologically damaging content.
    
- **Addictive Design and Mental Health Problems**: TikTok is accused of deliberately designing its app to be addictive, including to minors, and is aware that minors become addicted, yet fails to inform them or their parents. Features like **infinite scroll, the "slot-machine" effect, constant push notifications, autoplay, autoscroll, and ephemeral content (Live, Stories)** are cited as coercive designs that tether users to the app to increase TikTok's revenue. The highly personalized algorithm is also designed to be addictive, keeping users engaged and potentially pushing them into "rabbit holes" or "filter bubbles" of harmful content. TikTok's social reward features ("likes," "follows," "comments") exploit minors' vulnerability, contributing to excessive use and harmful social comparison. The State claims these designs lead to significant mental health problems, including anxiety, depression, social isolation, and disordered eating, disproportionately affecting girls. #dark_pattern 
    
- **Deceptive Community Guidelines**: TikTok's public-facing Community Guidelines are alleged to be "false, deceptive, and misleading" because they claim to provide a safe space and prohibit content (e.g., drug promotion, nudity, sexual activity, profanity) that is, in fact, permitted or leaks through moderation. TikTok is accused of omitting crucial information about the extent of content leakage and its internal policies, such as cannabis exceptions in the U.S. that contradict its stated prohibitions.
    
- **Interactive Features' Threats**: Features like **TikTok Live** are cited for facilitating dangerous activities (e.g., binge drinking leading to death) and frequently showcasing violative content, particularly to young users, despite TikTok earning significant revenue from it. **Direct Messages and Group Chats** are criticized for easily exposing minors to inappropriate content and harmful exchanges, including with strangers. **"Effects," especially beauty filters**, are said to harm younger users' mental health and development by encouraging self-editing and distorting reality, with 52% of girls using filters daily and 80% having used an app to change their appearance before age 13.
    

The lawsuit argues that TikTok's misrepresentations are "highly relevant" to parents' decisions about whether to allow minors to use the app, as parents rely on age ratings and content descriptions.

## Sanction

The State of Texas is seeking the following relief from the court:

- A declaration that TikTok's conduct violates the DTPA.
- A **permanent injunction** ordering TikTok to cease its false, misleading, and deceptive representations to Texas minors and their parents. This includes stopping:
    - False claims that the app contains "infrequent/mild" mature content and is safe for minors.
    - Continuing to claim and advertise a "12+" age rating on the Apple App Store and a "T for Teen" rating on Google Play and Microsoft Stores.
    - False representations about the addictive nature of the app.
    - False representations in the Community Guidelines about permitted content.
    - Failing to disclose abundant mature, inappropriate, and adult-only content.
    - Failing to disclose all content TikTok actually permits per its internal policies and Playbook Rules in its Community Guidelines.
- **Civil penalties of not more than $10,000 per violation** of the DTPA.
- **Monetary relief in an amount greater than $1,000,000**, which includes civil penalties, reasonable attorney’s fees, litigation expenses, restitution, and costs.
- Any other general, equitable, and/or further relief deemed just and proper by the Court.

The State has demanded a jury trial and states that venue is proper in Galveston County, Texas, because defendants have done business there and relevant transactions occurred there.
