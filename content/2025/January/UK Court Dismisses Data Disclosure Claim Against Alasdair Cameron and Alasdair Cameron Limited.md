# Summary

The High Court of Justice in England and Wales dismissed a claim brought by Mark Harrison against Alasdair Cameron and Alasdair Cameron Limited (ACL) under the UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR) and Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA 2018). Harrison sought an order for the Defendants to comply with his subject access requests (SARs), specifically to disclose the identities of 15 people to whom recordings of his threatening telephone calls with Alasdair Cameron had been disseminated. The court found that Alasdair Cameron was not a data controller in his personal capacity, thus dismissing the claim against him . For Alasdair Cameron Limited, the court determined that while it was a data controller and Harrison was entitled under Article 15(1)(c) UK GDPR to the specific identities of recipients (as per the _Austrian Post_ case interpretation), ACL was nonetheless entitled to withhold these identities by relying on the 'rights of others' exemption under Schedule 2, paragraph 16 of the DPA 2018 .

# Details

## Background

The case arose from a contractual dispute between Mark Harrison, Chief Executive of Praxis Group Ltd (a real estate investment company), and Alasdair Cameron, a landscape gardener who owns and operates Alasdair Cameron Ltd (ACL). The dispute concerned landscaping work at Mr. Harrison's property, which led to Mr. Harrison terminating the agreement.

On May 7, 2022, Mr. Harrison became angry after a site foreman informed him that Mr. Cameron had been questioning other contractors about payments, despite Mr. Harrison having paid ACL a substantial sum. Mr. Harrison initiated several calls with Mr. Cameron that day, two of which Mr. Cameron **recorded without Mr. Harrison's consent**. Mr. Cameron stated he recorded the calls because Mr. Harrison had threatened him, acting on advice from a family member. The recordings captured Mr. Harrison making **"seriously and persistently menacing"** statements, including threats to send "two of my friends from Manchester" to pay Mr. Cameron a visit he and his family would "never forget," threatening "severe consequences," and stating he was "coming down to see you" to "administer an education". The judge found Mr. Harrison's denials that he made threats of physical violence to be **"untenable and untrue"** and that his references to Mr. Cameron's wife were intended to "instil in Mr Cameron fear for his wife's well-being". Mr. Cameron complied with Mr. Harrison's demands by removing ACL's equipment from the property by 6pm that day.

Mr. Cameron subsequently **shared the Recordings with 12 people**: five ACL employees ('ACL1-5'), two family members ('Family 1-2'), and five friends ('Friends 1-4 and 7'). Further dissemination occurred through 'Family 1' sharing with 'Friend 5', who then shared with his brother, 'Friend 6'. Mr. Cameron's wife also shared transcripts of the Recordings with 'Family 3'. Mr. Cameron stated he shared them for personal reasons, to inform family and friends of the threats and seek advice for his and his family's safety. However, the judge noted he also likely recorded them in part for business reasons given the impending contractual dispute.

The Recordings were also **disseminated more widely within the property investment industry**, including to individuals involved in the sale of the Grosvenor Shopping Centre in Chester. Mr. Harrison contended that this dissemination caused his company, Praxis, to lose out on the acquisition of the shopping centre, resulting in over £10 million in financial loss.

Mr. Harrison's solicitors made Subject Access Requests (SARs) to Mr. Cameron on July 6, 2022, and to ACL on August 24, 2022, seeking, among other things, the names of all individuals to whom the Recordings or transcripts had been shared . The Defendants initially refused, claiming the UK GDPR did not apply due to the "purely personal or household activity" exemption . An offer to disclose names, subject to Mr. Harrison providing undertakings not to threaten, harass, or bring claims against the recipients (except for DPA 2018/UK GDPR claims), was rejected by Mr. Harrison as "wholly unacceptable" .

## Legal Analysis

The claim was brought under the **UK General Data Protection Regulation (UK GDPR)** and the **Data Protection Act 2018 (DPA 2018)**. The agreed issues for determination included:

- **Article 2(2)(a) UK GDPR: "Purely personal or household activity" exemption**.
    
    - The court found that Mr. Cameron's processing of Mr. Harrison's personal data by sharing the Recordings with family and friends **did not fall within this exemption**.
    - The judge reasoned that the activity was not "purely personal" because it involved recordings of business calls held by ACL and was related to the termination of a business contract, extending beyond a mere general chat with family or friends .
    - The exemption was also inapplicable to ACL's dissemination, as it is not a natural person, and to Mr. Cameron's sharing with ACL employees .
- **Article 4(7) UK GDPR: Is Mr. Cameron a "data controller"?**.
    
    - The court concluded that **Mr. Cameron was not a "controller"** .
    - Applying established legal principles (_In re Southern Pacific Personal Loans Ltd_ and _Ittihadieh v 5-11 Cheyne Gardens RTM Company Ltd_), the judge stated that the company (ACL) is the data controller when directors process data in the course of their duties, not the directors personally, unless they act in an unauthorised fashion . The court found Mr. Cameron was acting in his capacity as a director of ACL when recording and sharing the calls .
- **Article 15(1)(c) UK GDPR: Entitlement to identities of recipients.*
    
    - The court considered the interpretation of Article 15(1)(c), which refers to "the recipients or categories of recipient" .
    - The judge, while noting that the provision's wording alone is ambiguous, adopted the reasoning of the CJEU in **_RW v Österreichische Post AG_ (_Austrian Post_ case)** .
    - This interpretation means that the data subject's right of access **entails an obligation on the controller to provide the actual identity of recipients**, unless it's impossible to identify them or the request is manifestly unfounded or excessive . 
    - The judge found the _Austrian Post_ reasoning "coherent and compelling" and rejected arguments that "categories of recipients" was sufficient or that the rule only applied to external recipients . Thus, Harrison was entitled to the specific identities from ACL . #rights 
- **Article 15(4) UK GDPR and Schedule 2, paragraph 16 DPA 2018: "Rights of others" exemption.**
    
    - This exemption allows a controller to withhold information if disclosing it would "adversely affect the rights and freedoms of others" or "involve disclosing information relating to another individual who can be identified" . It requires a balancing of competing interests .
    - The court affirmed that the controller (ACL) has a **"wide margin of discretion"** in assessing the reasonableness of disclosure without consent .
    - ACL's decision to withhold the identities of the 15 recipients was found to be **reasonable and within its margin of discretion** .
    - Key factors in this decision included:
        - **The nature of Mr. Harrison's threats:** The judge found that Mr. Harrison genuinely made "serious and ugly threats of violence" and Mr. Cameron was "understandably concerned". Mr. Harrison's persistent denial of these threats was given "little weight" .
        - **Mr. Harrison's conduct towards ACL employees:** The judge considered the letters sent by Mr. Harrison's solicitors to approximately two dozen ACL employees, including subsequent "threatening letters," to be "intimidating and unwarranted" .
        - **Protection of recipients:** The court found it reasonable for the Defendants to prioritize protecting family, friends, and colleagues from potential "intimidating, harassing and hostile legal correspondence and litigation" . Mr. Harrison's rejection of the open offer, which would have allowed for disclosure with protective undertakings, further supported this .
        - **Purpose of SAR:** While not devaluing the request's motive, the court noted the SAR regime's specific purpose is for individuals to check processing lawfulness, not to obtain documents for wider litigation or investigation .

## Sanction

The claim was dismissed in its entirety.

- The claim against the **First Defendant, Alasdair Cameron, was dismissed** because he was not found to be a data controller .
- The claim against the **Second Defendant, Alasdair Cameron Limited (ACL), was dismissed** because the "rights of others" exemption applied, meaning ACL was lawfully entitled to withhold the identities of the recipients of the Recordings and Transcripts .
- The claim did not include any request for compensation or damages, and therefore, no financial sanctions or fines were imposed.
