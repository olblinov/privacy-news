# Summary

The Polish Supreme Administrative Court (NSA) confirmed a decision by the President of the Personal Data Protection Office (Prezes UODO) ruling that **Bank Millennium could not process former clients' personal data "on reserve" to protect against potential future claims**. The bank had continued to process data of clients whose accounts were closed and who had objected to marketing, justifying it under the "legitimate interest" basis (Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR). However, both UODO and NSA clarified that this legal basis applies to existing claims, not hypothetical future ones, and the bank failed to demonstrate any actual dispute that would necessitate such processing. Consequently, UODO had ordered the bank to delete the data, a decision upheld by the courts.

# Details

## Background

Bank Millennium was found to be processing the personal data of its former clients even though their bank accounts had been closed. These former clients had also explicitly objected to their personal data being processed for marketing purposes. The bank's continued processing of their data came to light when it sent them marketing correspondence.

The bank's justification for this ongoing data processing was to secure itself against potential future claims from these former clients, invoking its **"legitimate interest" as the legal basis under Article 6(1)(f) of the GDPR**. Subsequently, the affected couple filed a complaint with the President of the Personal Data Protection Office (Prezes UODO).

## Legal Analysis

The Prezes UODO determined that the bank's reliance on Article 6(1)(f) GDPR was **unjustified**. UODO clarified that this provision applies to situations involving an _existing_ need to prove, pursue, or defend against an existing claim, rather than merely securing against a _potential_ or _eventual_ claim. UODO concluded that the bank was processing the data "on reserve" to protect against future and uncertain claims, which, if allowed, could lead to permanent data processing without the necessity of deletion. Furthermore, UODO noted that the bank failed to demonstrate any ongoing dispute with the data subjects that would justify the processing under Article 6(1)(f) GDPR.

The Supreme Administrative Court (NSA) upheld Prezes UODO's position, dismissing Bank Millennium's cassation appeal. The NSA emphasized that for processing to be justified on the basis of an administrator's legitimate interest (Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR), three cumulative conditions must be met:

1. **The administrator must have specific goals for which data processing is necessary**.
2. **These goals must stem from "legitimate interests" pursued by the administrator**.
3. **These "legitimate interests" must be overriding compared to the interests, fundamental rights, and freedoms of the data subject**.

In this specific case, the NSA stated that while the bank cited securing its interests against potential claims, it **failed to prove that the applicants had actually made any claims against it**. #legitimate_interest  The NSA also highlighted the **principle of accountability**, reminding that the data administrator bears the burden of proof to demonstrate the legal grounds for data processing and compliance with data protection principles. The Court underscored that in the event of a dispute with a data subject or a supervisory authority, the administrator must be able to provide evidence of compliance. The NSA concluded that Bank Millennium could not prove its right to process the former clients' data after they withdrew their consent.

## Sanction

Prezes UODO issued an **administrative decision ordering Bank Millennium to delete the data** of the couple who filed the complaint. This decision was subsequently confirmed by the Provincial Administrative Court (WSA) in Warsaw and finally upheld by the Supreme Administrative Court (NSA), which dismissed Bank Millennium's appeal.
