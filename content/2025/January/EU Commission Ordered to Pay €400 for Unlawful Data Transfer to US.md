# Summary

The General Court of the European Union, in a judgment delivered on 8 January 2025, primarily addressed a case brought by Thomas Bindl against the European Commission concerning the processing and transfer of his personal data. Mr. Bindl alleged unlawful transfers of his data to third countries (the United States), the Commission's failure to respond adequately to his information requests, and sought annulment of transfers and compensation for damages. The Court dismissed the claims for annulment as inadmissible, finding that the alleged data transfers were physical IT operations without binding legal effects. It also declared there was no need to adjudicate on the claim for failure to act, as the Commission had responded after the action was brought, ending its inaction. However, the Court partially upheld the claim for damages, ordering the European Commission to pay **€400 in compensation** to Mr. Bindl for non-material damage. This compensation was awarded specifically for the unlawful transfer of his personal data, notably his IP address, to Meta Platforms (Facebook) in the United States when he signed in to the EU Login service via his Facebook account on 30 March 2022, without appropriate safeguards as required by Regulation (EU) 2018/1725.

# Details

## Background

The applicant, **Mr. Thomas Bindl**, is a German citizen with an interest in information technology (IT) and personal data protection. He visited the website of the Conference on the Future of Europe (CFE website), for which the European Commission’s Directorate-General for Communication acts as the data controller.

Mr. Bindl initiated two information requests to the Commission concerning the processing of his data under Regulation (EU) 2018/1725:

- **9 November 2021:** He noticed connections with third-party providers like Amazon Web Services (US undertaking) when logging into the CFE website and requested information on processed/stored personal data, transfers to third parties, legal basis, and guarantees for transfers to countries without adequate protection. The Commission replied on 3 December 2021, stating that his personal data had not been transferred outside the European Union and were stored and processed by the CFE website using Amazon Web Services EMEA SARL (AWS EMEA), established in Luxembourg. It also noted that transfers outside the EU were, in principle, not authorized under their contractual arrangements. #transfers 
- **1 April 2022:** He reiterated his request, specifically mentioning connections to AWS EMEA and Microsoft (via Facebook login) upon logging into the CFE website. He also requested a copy of his data, including data processed by third parties like Facebook. The Commission later replied on 30 June 2022, stating that this request was "virtually identical" to the previous one and had already been answered.

Mr. Bindl subsequently brought an action before the General Court on 9 June 2022, seeking:

- Annulment of alleged transfers of his personal data to third countries without adequate protection on 30 March and 8 June 2022.
- A declaration that the European Commission unlawfully failed to define its position on his information request of 1 April 2022.
- Compensation for non-material damage: €800 for infringement of his right of access to information and €400 for damage sustained due to the data transfers.

The alleged data transfers at issue included:

- On 30 March 2022, when visiting the CFE website, personal data (IP address, browser/terminal info) were allegedly transferred to Amazon Web Services (US undertaking) via Amazon CloudFront.
- On 30 March 2022, when signing in to EU Login via his Facebook account to register for a "GoGreen" event, personal data (IP address, browser/terminal info) were allegedly transferred to Meta Platforms, Inc. (US undertaking).
- On 8 June 2022, when visiting the CFE website, personal data were allegedly transferred to an Amazon CloudFront server in Newark, New Jersey, United States.

## Legal Analysis

The case was examined under **Regulation (EU) 2018/1725** on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data by Union institutions, bodies, offices, and agencies, which applies to the European Commission. This regulation is to be interpreted homogeneously with the General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR).

**1. Admissibility of the Claim for Annulment (of Data Transfers):**

- Mr. Bindl sought annulment of the data transfers themselves.
- The Court found that the alleged data transfers were **"physical, not legal, acts"**, described as "IT operations migrating data from one terminal or server to another". #transfers 
- Such operations are not intended to have "binding legal effects capable of affecting the interests of the applicant by bringing about a distinct change in his or her legal position".
- Therefore, the claim for annulment was **dismissed as inadmissible** because the transfers did not constitute challengeable acts under Article 263 TFEU. 

**2. Admissibility of the Claim for a Declaration of Failure to Act (regarding information request):**

- Mr. Bindl alleged that the Commission unlawfully failed to define its position on his information request of 1 April 2022.
- The Commission had, however, replied to this request by email on 30 June 2022, after the action was brought.
- The Court ruled that the Commission's reply, even if unsatisfactory to the applicant, ended the alleged inaction.
- Consequently, there was **no longer any need to adjudicate** on this claim.

**3. Claim for Damages (Non-contractual liability of the European Union):**

- The European Union may incur non-contractual liability if three cumulative conditions are met: **(i) unlawfulness of conduct**, (ii) **actual damage**, and (iii) **a causal link** between the conduct and the damage. The unlawfulness must be a "sufficiently serious breach of a rule of law that is intended to confer rights on individuals".
    
    **a. First Claim for Damages: Infringement of Right of Access to Information (€800 sought):**
    
    - Mr. Bindl complained that the Commission failed to reply timely to his 1 April 2022 request and provided incorrect information in its 3 December 2021 email, infringing Article 14(3) and (4) and Article 17(1) and (2) of Regulation 2018/1725. These provisions were deemed to confer rights on individuals.
    - The Court found that the Commission **did not observe the one-month time limit** for replying to the 1 April 2022 request (it replied on 30 June 2022).
    - However, the Court concluded that **no actual non-material damage was demonstrated** as a result of this procedural infringement. The delay was only two months, and the applicant had already received a reply to a "virtually identical" earlier request. #damages 
    - Therefore, this claim for damages was **dismissed**.
    
    **b. Second Claim for Damages: Unlawful Data Transfers (€400 sought):**
    
    - Mr. Bindl alleged that transfers to the US (via Amazon and Meta Platforms) violated Article 46 and Article 48(1) and (2)(b) of Regulation 2018/1725, as well as Articles 7, 8, and 47 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights. These provisions were deemed to confer rights on individuals. Chapter V of Regulation 2018/1725 governs data transfers to third countries, requiring adequacy decisions or appropriate safeguards. At the time, no adequacy decision existed for the United States.
        
    - **i. Disputed transfer at the time of the visit to the CFE website on 30 March 2022 (Amazon CloudFront):**
        
        - The applicant's IP address (personal data) was transmitted via the Amazon CloudFront service.
        - However, the transmission was to a server located in **Munich, Germany**, belonging to a German undertaking, and therefore the recipient was established in the European Union.
        - The Court found that this was **not a "transfer of personal data to a third country"** within the meaning of Article 46 of Regulation 2018/1725, which requires the data to be made available to a recipient established outside the EEA. #transfers 
        - The argument about the **mere risk of access** by US authorities (due to AWS EMEA being a US subsidiary) was not sufficient to establish a direct infringement of Article 46 or a "sufficiently serious breach" by the Commission. #change 
        - This part of the claim was **dismissed**.
    - **ii. Disputed transfer at the time of the visits to the CFE website on 8 June 2022 (Amazon CloudFront):**
        
        - The applicant claimed his IP address was transferred to Amazon CloudFront servers in the US.
        - The Court noted that while his IP address did connect to servers in the US (among others in Europe), these connections were **atypical**.
        - The routing mechanism of Amazon CloudFront normally directs requests to the closest server. The connections to US servers from Germany were attributed to a "technical adjustment made by the applicant to change his apparent location," rather than the normal operation of the service or a malfunction.
        - The Court concluded that the **causal link was missing**: the applicant's own conduct, by manipulating his apparent location, was the direct and immediate cause of the data reaching US servers, not the Commission's use of the service itself. #change 
        - This part of the claim was **dismissed**.
    - **iii. Disputed transfer on signing in to EU Login on 30 March 2022 (Facebook/Meta Platforms):**
        
        - When Mr. Bindl chose to sign in to EU Login via his Facebook account, clicking the "Sign in with Facebook" hyperlink caused his web browser to access Facebook's URL address and transmit his IP address to Facebook (Meta Platforms).
        - The Court confirmed that this constituted a **"transfer of personal data to a third country"** (the United States) within the meaning of Article 46 of Regulation 2018/1725. #transfers #change 
        - Crucially, the Commission **did not demonstrate or claim that appropriate safeguards** (e.g., standard data protection clauses) were in place as required by Article 48(1) of Regulation 2018/1725 for transfers to countries without an adequacy decision. The display of the hyperlink was governed solely by Facebook's general terms and conditions.
        - The Court found that the Commission committed a **"sufficiently serious breach" of Article 46 of Regulation 2018/1725** by creating the conditions for this transfer without appropriate safeguards.
        - **Damage was established:** The unlawful transfer put the applicant in a "position of some uncertainty as regards the processing of his personal data," which constituted **actual and certain non-material damage**. The regulation allows for compensation for non-material damage without a seriousness threshold.
        - A **sufficiently direct causal link** was found between the Commission's breach and this non-material damage.

## Sanction

The General Court ordered the European Commission to pay **EUR 400** to Mr. Thomas Bindl as compensation for the non-material damage he sustained as a result of the unlawful transfer of his personal data when signing in to EU Login via his Facebook account on 30 March 2022.

Additionally, the Commission was ordered to **bear its own costs and pay one half of the costs incurred by Mr. Bindl**, while Mr. Bindl must bear one half of his own costs.
