# Summary

The Spanish Data Protection Agency (AEPD) has issued a **sanction against Cartonajes Bañeres, S.A. (CB)**, a company that used a **biometric facial recognition system for employee timekeeping**. The AEPD imposed **two administrative fines totaling €220,000**: **€200,000 for a grave infringement of Article 35 of the GDPR** for failing to conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) for its biometric system, and **€20,000 for a very grave infringement of Article 15 of the GDPR** for failing to effectively attend an employee's right of access request. The AEPD determined that the company's facial recognition system, which stored biometric patterns ("hashes") centrally and performed one-to-many identification, constituted a high-risk data processing operation requiring a DPIA under the GDPR, which came into effect in May 2018, despite the system's installation in 2016. Furthermore, CB failed to provide the complainant with a proper response to their data access request, sending it to an incorrect address and omitting crucial information.

# Details

## Background

The case originated from a **reclamation filed by a former employee, A.A.A., against Cartonajes Bañeres, S.A. (CB)** on October 11, 2022. The complainant stated that CB used a device at the entrance to take photographs of employees' faces for timekeeping, which caused discomfort due to its use of biometric data. Despite being told the photo was only for employee records and that the "face is not saved," the complainant wished to clock in without biometric data and inquired about alternative methods like cards. The complainant also asserted that a **request for access to their personal data, submitted on August 29, 2022, had not received a response** via postal or electronic mail.

In response to the AEPD's transfer of the complaint, CB submitted a written statement on December 22, 2022. CB denied taking photographs, claiming their device performed a "triangulation of eyes-mouth-nose and pixel count" for identification, storing only the employee's name, day, and time in an Excel file (later clarified as Microsoft Access) without saving or transferring any biometric data. CB also stated that the complainant had been informed of data processing and had given **explicit consent** through a document signed on May 11, 2022, which mentioned "digital fingerprint capture" and "facial scan" for time control and stated that not providing consent would prevent employment. Regarding the access request, CB claimed they sent an email on September 15, 2022, informing the complainant that the request was being processed, and later sent a burofax on December 9, 2022, after the complainant allegedly failed to pick up the response. However, the burofax was sent to a different address than the one provided by the complainant and did not mention facial recognition data.

The AEPD's investigation, which included an on-site inspection on May 31, 2023, found that CB was acquired by SAICA PACK S.L. on July 7, 2022, though CB retained its legal personality. SAICA's Data Protection Delegates Committee (CDPD), formed on May 6, 2022, had, as early as **October 19, 2022, recommended that CB immediately replace its biometric timekeeping system** due to its use of biometric data. By January 20, 2023, SAICA was actively working on suppressing the biometric system and implementing a new card-based system, which was finalized and implemented on **May 29, 2023**. The old system, supplied by SABACOINSA SL, used a "3D facial recognition terminal" to generate a biometric pattern ("hash") from facial images, which was stored in the **PHUC Control software database on CB's server** and linked to user IDs. While the image itself was not stored, the hash could uniquely identify a person. Upon the new system's implementation, the stored hashes were destroyed. The AEPD confirmed that the complainant's clock-in data was no longer available, and the WhatsApp messages exchanged between CB and the complainant regarding the access request were unclear and did not confirm the purpose of the meeting or the delivery of the response.

## Legal Analysis

The AEPD's legal analysis focused on two main infringements: the processing of biometric data without a DPIA and the failure to attend the right of access.

1. **Definition and Treatment of Biometric Data:** The AEPD reiterated that **biometric data** are personal data derived from specific technical processing of physical, physiological, or behavioral characteristics to uniquely identify an individual, such as facial images or fingerprints. The system used by CB, which created a "hash" or mathematical pattern from facial images stored in a central database to identify employees for timekeeping, falls under this definition. Crucially, the AEPD clarified that CB's system constituted **biometric identification (one-to-many)**, where a new scan is compared against a database of all stored templates to find a match, rather than mere **verification/authentication (one-to-one)**, where a scan is compared against a single, pre-selected template. This distinction is significant as one-to-many identification typically poses higher risks. #sensitive 
    
2. **Legitimacy of Biometric Data Processing (Article 9 GDPR):** The AEPD stated that processing special categories of data, including biometric data for unique identification, is **generally prohibited** under Article 9.1 of the GDPR. Exceptions exist for labor law obligations (Article 9.2.b GDPR) but require explicit authorization by EU or Member State law. While the Spanish Workers' Statute (ET) mandates daily work hour registration, it **does not explicitly authorize the use of biometric data** for this purpose. The AEPD emphasized the principles of **necessity and proportionality**, noting that less intrusive alternatives, such as the card-based system later adopted by CB, were available. This indicated that the biometric system was not strictly necessary for fulfilling the legal obligation of timekeeping. Furthermore, the AEPD considered the **consent obtained from employees not freely given** due to the power imbalance in the employment relationship and the lack of alternative options.
    
3. **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) (Article 35 GDPR):** The GDPR requires a DPIA for processing operations likely to result in a **high risk** to individuals' rights and freedoms, especially when involving new technologies, large-scale processing of special categories of data (such as biometrics), or vulnerable subjects like employees. The AEPD asserted that the biometric facial recognition system inherently presented such high risks. CB installed its system in 2016. However, the AEPD clarified that even if a system was established before the GDPR's application date of May 25, 2018, **ongoing processing operations must comply with the GDPR**, and a DPIA is required if there have been changes in risks or if the processing falls under the high-risk criteria, which biometric data processing certainly does under the GDPR. CB failed to perform a DPIA or even document the reasons for not doing so, despite the GDPR's principle of **proactive responsibility** (Article 5.2 GDPR). The AEPD rejected CB's argument that the infraction was prescribed or that it was applying the GDPR retroactively, stating that the continuous processing from May 2018 until May 2023 without a DPIA constituted an ongoing violation. #dpia 
    
4. **Right of Access (Article 15 GDPR) and Transparency (Article 12 GDPR):** The AEPD found that CB failed to adequately respond to the complainant's request for access to their personal data within the **one-month deadline**. The attempts to contact the complainant via WhatsApp were for an unclear purpose (to sign a letter, not to deliver the data access response), and the burofax was sent to an incorrect address and lacked complete information about the data processed, especially regarding facial recognition data and the right to complain to the AEPD. The AEPD reclassified the violation from a general transparency breach (Article 12) to a specific **failure to attend the right of access** (Article 15), deeming it a more precise legal classification for the facts.
    

## Sanction

The AEPD concluded that CB committed two infringements:

- **Article 35 GDPR (Failure to conduct a DPIA):** This was classified as a **grave (serious) infringement** under Article 73.t) of the LOPDGDD. The AEPD noted several aggravating factors: the **nature, gravity, and duration** of the infringement, affecting 99 employees with high-risk special category data (biometrics) in a power-imbalanced employment context. A significant aggravating factor was the **maintenance of the biometric system for several months** (from October 2022, when SAICA's internal committee recommended its immediate cessation, until its replacement in May 2023), prolonging the effects of the non-compliant processing. The AEPD also found a **grave lack of diligence** because CB voluntarily implemented the system without anticipating its impact under the GDPR and failed to document any assessment regarding the need for a DPIA, acting as if no such obligation existed post-GDPR. The fine for this infringement was set at **€200,000**.
- **Article 15 GDPR (Failure to attend the right of access):** This was classified as a **muy grave (very serious) infringement** under Article 72.1.k) of the LOPDGDD. Aggravating factors included the **deficient implementation of organizational measures** for handling data subject rights and a **clear lack of diligence** in attempting to respond to the complainant's request, characterized by unclear communication attempts and sending the burofax to an incorrect address. The fine for this infringement was **€20,000**.

The **total administrative fine imposed is €220,000**. The AEPD also **ordered CB to comply with the complainant's right of access request** within 30 days of the resolution becoming effective.

The AEPD rejected CB's arguments regarding retroactivity and prescription, affirming that the ongoing nature of the data processing after the GDPR's effective date meant the new regulation applied and the infringement was not prescribed. It also dismissed CB's claims for attenuating factors, stating that changing the system _after_ being advised to do so and after the complaint was filed, did not constitute sufficient mitigation to be an attenuating factor, but rather the delay became an aggravating one. Other claimed attenuating factors, such as the company's main activity not being data processing or not obtaining financial benefits, were also rejected, as they did not reduce the severity or culpability of the infringement.

The resolution is executive after one month if no administrative appeal is filed. Payment deadlines are set based on the date of executivity. CB has the option to file an administrative appeal or directly a contentious-administrative appeal with the Audiencia Nacional.
