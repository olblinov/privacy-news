# Summary

The game developer Cognosphere Pte. Ltd. and its U.S. subsidiary, Cognosphere LLC, doing business as HoYoverse, have agreed to pay **$20 million** to settle Federal Trade Commission (FTC) charges related to their popular video game, Genshin Impact. The settlement addresses allegations that HoYoverse violated the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) by collecting personal information from children under 13 without verifiable parental consent and engaged in deceptive and unfair business practices related to in-game loot boxes. The proposed order will also prohibit the company from selling loot boxes to teens under 16 without parental consent and require changes to their disclosure and business practices.

# Details

## Background

Genshin Impact, released in September 2020, is a popular online video game, particularly among children and teenagers. The game features **anime-style cartoon graphics, bright and colorful animation, and characters that have the speech or appearance of children**, which helps it appeal to a younger audience. Although free to download and play, HoYoverse primarily generates revenue by selling in-game virtual currency, which players use to purchase "loot boxes," referred to as "Wishes".

These loot boxes offer a chance to obtain desirable "5-star" virtual heroes, which are more powerful and sought after. HoYoverse extensively promoted the game, including to children, through advertisements, social media channels, and by hiring paid "influencers" who are popular among children, some of whom were known for content related to games like Minecraft and Roblox. The promotional tactics included emphasizing limited-time availability of desirable prizes through "Event Banners" and glamorizing the excitement of opening loot boxes. Some children and teenagers have reportedly spent **hundreds or even thousands of dollars** to obtain these rare prizes.

## Legal Analysis

The FTC's complaint, filed by the Department of Justice, alleged multiple violations by HoYoverse:

- **Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) Violations**:
    
    - HoYoverse was deemed an "operator" of an online service directed to children and had actual knowledge of collecting personal information from children under 13.
    - Despite this, HoYoverse allegedly failed to provide parents with direct notice of its information practices regarding children.
    - The company also failed to obtain verifiable parental consent before collecting, using, and disclosing children's personal information, even when aware that users were under 13. Personal information collected included email addresses, mobile numbers, user names, unique user IDs, and device-related persistent identifiers, which were used to track player progress, purchases, and settings, and were shared with third-party analytics and advertising providers.
    - The game did not utilize an age-gate to screen players' ages.
    - The COPPA Rule defines "personal information" to include persistent identifiers used to recognize a user over time and across different websites/online services, photographs/videos/audio files containing a child's image or voice, and geolocation information.
    - HoYoverse's social networking service, "HoYoLAB," also collected personal information from users, and in some instances, HoYoverse was aware that users were children but continued to collect, use, and disclose their information without parental consent.
- **Federal Trade Commission Act (FTC Act) Violations**:
    
    - **Deceptive Practices** (Section 5(a) of the FTC Act):
        - HoYoverse allegedly **misrepresented players' odds of obtaining rare loot box prizes**. For example, "huge drop-rate boost" promotions did not actually increase the odds, but merely indicated the prize was available during the promotional period. The actual odds for a featured 5-star hero were 0.3%. #dark_pattern 
        - The "guaranteed to win 5-star character at least once per 90 attempts" was misleading, as players often received an unrelated 5-star prize, potentially requiring up to 180 attempts (costing $360-$540) to get the featured character. #dark_pattern 
        - HoYoverse paid influencers to promote loot boxes and approved misleading videos that depicted fake wins and unachievable gameplay, which were viewed millions of times.
    - **Unfair Practices** (Section 5(a) and 5(n) of the FTC Act):
        - The game's **complicated and confusing multi-tier virtual currency system** (real dollars to Genesis Crystals to Primogems to Wish tokens to loot boxes) obscured the true amount of money players were spending and the likely cost of rare prizes. This system made it difficult for consumers, especially children and teenagers with less developed executive function skills, to understand their expenditures.
        - HoYoverse **unfairly promoted and offered rare, chance-based loot box prizes to children and teenagers**. They did not use age-gating technology to prevent children from purchasing expensive virtual currency bundles (up to $99) or spending money on loot boxes.
        - The design elements, such as limited-time event banners and suspenseful animations, combined with the "variable ratio reinforcement" principle (like traditional gambling), made loot boxes particularly appealing to impulsive children and teenagers. These practices led to substantial financial losses for consumers, which could have been avoided through transparent pricing, age-gating, or discontinuing loot box sales.

## Sanction

Under the proposed order, which requires approval from a federal judge, Cognosphere Pte. Ltd. and Cognosphere LLC will face the following sanctions and requirements:

- **Monetary Penalty**: A **$20 million** civil penalty will be paid to the Treasurer of the United States.
- **Prohibitions on Loot Box Sales**: The companies are permanently prohibited from allowing children under **16 years old** to purchase loot boxes in their video games without a parent's affirmative express consent. Affirmative express consent requires clear disclosure of the game's nature, the use of paid virtual currency, and material aspects of the loot box mechanism, including chance-based outcomes and potential costs. Consent cannot be inferred from inaction or subverted by user interface design.
- **Direct Purchase Option**: They are prohibited from selling loot boxes using virtual currency without also providing consumers an option to purchase them directly with real money, with clear cost indication.
- **Misrepresentation Ban**: The companies are prohibited from misrepresenting loot box odds, prices, features, or any other material fact about their products.
- **Required Disclosures**:
    - They must clearly and conspicuously disclose the likelihood of obtaining featured digital content via loot boxes and the range of potential total costs in U.S. dollars at the point of sale.
    - At the point of sale for virtual currency bundles, they must disclose the number of transactions the bundle provides for loot boxes and the exchange rate in U.S. dollars for one loot box transaction.
    - An online notice containing featured in-game items, their likelihood of acquisition, and potential costs must be easily accessible on their website and at the game login screen.
- **COPPA Compliance**:
    - The companies must delete any personal information previously collected from children under 13 unless verifiable parental consent is obtained to retain such data.
    - They must implement a **neutral age gate** within 120 days of the order's effective date.
    - They are required to comply with COPPA, including its notice and consent requirements.
    - If a user's age is not identified within 180 days, their personal information will be subject to deletion, and further collection/disclosure will cease unless parental consent is obtained.
- **Compliance Monitoring**: The order includes provisions for compliance reporting, recordkeeping for up to 10 years, and monitoring by the FTC.
