# Summary

This judgment by the Court of Justice (First Chamber) provides a preliminary ruling on the interpretation of Articles 57(4) and 77(1) of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The case originated from the Austrian Data Protection Authority's (DSB) refusal to act on a complaint by a data subject, F R, citing its excessive nature. The Court clarified three key points: first, that the concept of "requests" in Article 57(4) GDPR **does cover "complaints"** referred to in Article 57(1)(f) and Article 77(1) of that regulation. Second, for requests to be deemed "excessive," it is **not sufficient for them to be numerous alone**; the supervisory authority must also demonstrate an **abusive intention** on the part of the person lodging the requests. Third, a supervisory authority faced with excessive requests **may choose** between charging a reasonable fee or refusing to act, but this decision must be **reasoned, appropriate, necessary, and proportionate**.

# Details

## Background

The legal proceedings arose from a complaint lodged by F R, a natural person, with the Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde (DSB - Data Protection Authority, Austria) on February 17, 2020. F R's complaint, submitted under Article 77(1) of the GDPR, alleged an infringement of his right of access to personal data under Article 15, because a company (the controller) had failed to respond to his request for access within one month.

On April 22, 2020, the DSB refused to act on F R's complaint, invoking Article 57(4) of the GDPR due to its "excessive nature". The DSB noted that F R had sent 77 similar complaints against various controllers within a period of approximately 20 months and had frequently contacted the DSB by telephone to report additional facts and make further requests.

F R challenged the DSB's decision before the Bundesverwaltungsgericht (Federal Administrative Court, Austria), which subsequently annulled the DSB's decision on December 22, 2022. That court found that "excessive," as per Article 57(4) GDPR, requires not just repetition but also that requests are "manifestly vexatious or abusive," which the DSB had not demonstrated. It also held that a supervisory authority is not free to choose between the options in Article 57(4) without justification. The DSB then appealed this judgment to the Verwaltungsgerichtshof (Supreme Administrative Court, Austria), which referred several questions to the Court of Justice for a preliminary ruling.

## Legal Analysis

The Supreme Administrative Court of Austria sought clarification on three main questions concerning the interpretation of GDPR Articles 57(4) and 77(1):

- **Does the concept of "requests" in Article 57(4) GDPR include "complaints" under Article 77(1) GDPR?**
    
    - The Court found that the ordinary meaning of "request" is broad, encompassing any act of asking for something.
    - Article 57(4) establishes an **exception to the general principle** that tasks performed by supervisory authorities are free of charge, as stated in Article 57(3) GDPR. Since Article 57(3) applies to _all_ tasks, including handling complaints under Article 57(1)(f), it logically follows that the exception in Article 57(4) should also apply to complaints.
    - Excluding complaints from the scope of Article 57(4) would significantly diminish its effectiveness and run counter to the goal of effective protection of rights under the GDPR.
    - The Court concluded that the concept of a "request" in Article 57(4) GDPR **does cover complaints** referred to in Article 57(1)(f) and Article 77(1) of that regulation.
- **Is a mere high number of complaints sufficient for them to be "excessive," or is an abusive intention required?**
    
    - The Court noted that while Article 57(4) refers to "repetitive character" as an example of excessive requests, the term "excessive" itself implies exceeding a reasonable amount.
    - However, the exercise of the option in Article 57(4) is **exceptional**, constituting an abuse of rights. An **absolute numerical threshold** alone would risk undermining the rights guaranteed by the GDPR.
    - Member States are **obliged to provide supervisory authorities with sufficient resources** to handle all submitted complaints, and a supervisory authority cannot refuse to act based solely on a person's complaints significantly mobilizing its resources.
    - Complaints are crucial for supervisory authorities to identify infringements and contribute to a high level of personal data protection. Allowing a finding of excessiveness based solely on numbers could undermine this objective.
    - The supervisory authority must establish, considering all relevant circumstances, an **abusive intention** on the part of the data subject. This means the complaints were lodged when it was **not objectively necessary to protect their rights**. A large number of complaints _can_ be an indication of excessive requests if they are not objectively justified by protection of rights, or if they show an intention to paralyze the supervisory authority by flooding it with requests, particularly if they relate to controllers with whom the data subject has no connection.
    - The Court ruled that requests **cannot be classified as "excessive" solely due to their number**; the supervisory authority must demonstrate an **abusive intention** by the complainant.
- **Does Article 57(4) allow the supervisory authority to freely choose between charging a fee and refusing to act, or is there a specific order or criteria?**
    
    - The wording of Article 57(4) uses "or" between the two options (charging a fee or refusing to act), suggesting **no inherent order of priority** .
    - However, the choice must ensure the effective exercise of the right to lodge complaints .
    - Considering the importance of the right to complain and the supervisory authorities' obligation to act with due diligence, the chosen option must be **appropriate, necessary, and proportionate**, taking into account all relevant circumstances .
    - While charging a reasonable fee might be a less severe and more dissuasive initial measure, Article 57(4) **does not obligate** the supervisory authority to always apply the fee option first .
    - The Court concluded that when faced with excessive requests, a supervisory authority **may choose**, by a reasoned decision, between charging a reasonable fee or refusing to act, provided the chosen option is **appropriate, necessary, and proportionate** given all relevant circumstances.

## Sanction

The source is a preliminary ruling by the Court of Justice, which interprets EU law (GDPR). It does not impose a sanction, fine, or penalty on any party. Instead, it clarifies the legal framework within which national supervisory authorities, like the Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde (DSB), can act when faced with what they perceive as manifestly unfounded or excessive requests or complaints. The ruling defines the conditions under which a supervisory authority may either **charge a reasonable fee based on administrative costs or refuse to act on such requests**. The specific financial implications for the data subject, F R, or the DSB, would depend on the subsequent decision of the referring Austrian court based on this preliminary ruling.
